Cohomology of Projective Space

Serre’s Finiteness and Vanishing

The second theorem concerns the cohomology of coherent sheaves on projective spaces, known as ’Serre’s Finiteness Theorem’ and ’Serre’s Vanishing Theorem’ (to be distinguished from the affine case). Finiteness states that the cohomology of a coherent sheaf is finitely generated; Vanishing indicates that after a coherent sheaf is sufficiently twisted, its higher cohomology groups disappear.

Theorem 2.1 (Serre’s Finiteness and Vanishing). Let A be a Noetherian ring, and let X be a projective scheme over A. Let \mathcal{O}_X(1) be a very ample invertible sheaf on X. Let \mathcal{F} be a coherent sheaf on X. Then:

  1. Finiteness: For each i \ge 0, the cohomology group \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}) is a finitely generated A-module.

  2. Vanishing: There exists an integer n_0 (depending on \mathcal{F}) such that for all n \ge n_0 and all i > 0: \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}(n)) = 0.

Proof. Step 1: Reduction to Projective Space \mathbb{P}^r_A. Since X is projective over A and \mathcal{O}_X(1) is very ample, there exists a closed immersion \iota: X \hookrightarrow P = \mathbb{P}^r_A such that \mathcal{O}_X(1) \cong \iota^* \mathcal{O}_P(1). Let \mathcal{G} = \iota_* \mathcal{F} be the direct image sheaf on P.

  • Since \iota is a closed immersion (a finite morphism) and \mathcal{F} is coherent, \mathcal{G} is a coherent sheaf on P.

  • By the property of cohomology on closed subspaces, we have isomorphisms: \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}(n)) \cong \operatorname{H}^i(P, \mathcal{G}(n)).

Thus, it suffices to prove the theorem for the coherent sheaf \mathcal{G} on the ambient space P = \mathbb{P}^r_A. From now on, we assume X = \mathbb{P}^r_A.

Step 2: Verification for Line Bundles (The Base Case). We first check if the theorem holds for sheaves of the form \mathcal{E} = \bigoplus_{j=1}^m \mathcal{O}_X(q_j), a finite direct sum of twisted line bundles. By Theorem (Serre’s Calculation), we know the explicit structure of \operatorname{H}^i(\mathbb{P}^r, \mathcal{O}(q)):

  • For i=0, it is the degree q part of the polynomial ring S, which is a finite free A-module.

  • For 0 < i < r, it is zero.

  • For i=r, it is dual to the degree -q-r-1 part of S, which is a finite free A-module.

  • For i > r, it is zero.

(a) Finiteness: Since cohomology commutes with finite direct sums, \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{E}) is a direct sum of finitely generated free A-modules, hence finitely generated.

(b) Vanishing: For any fixed q_j, \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{O}(q_j+n)) = 0 for i > 0 as long as the degree shift moves us out of the non-zero range of \operatorname{H}^r (i.e., q_j + n > -r-1). Since there are finitely many q_j, we can choose n_0 large enough such that this holds for all summands. Thus, the theorem holds for \mathcal{E}.

Step 3: Descending Induction on i. We proceed by descending induction on the cohomological degree i.

  • Base Case (i > r): By Grothendieck’s Vanishing Theorem (since \dim \mathbb{P}^r = r), \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}) = 0 for all i > r. The zero module is finitely generated, and vanishing is trivial.

Inductive Step: Assume the statements (a) and (b) hold for degree i+1. We prove them for degree i (where i > 0). Since \mathcal{F} is coherent on \mathbb{P}^r, a fundamental property of coherent sheaves on projective space (Serre’s Theorem A) states that \mathcal{F} can be written as a quotient of a finite sum of line bundles. That is, there exists a surjective morphism \mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{F} \to 0, where \mathcal{E} = \bigoplus \mathcal{O}(q_j). Let \mathcal{R} be the kernel. Since the category of coherent sheaves is abelian, \mathcal{R} is also coherent. We have a short exact sequence: 0 \longrightarrow \mathcal{R} \longrightarrow \mathcal{E} \longrightarrow \mathcal{F} \longrightarrow 0.

Proof of (a) Finiteness for \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}): Consider the long exact sequence of cohomology: \dots \longrightarrow \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{E}) \longrightarrow \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}) \longrightarrow \operatorname{H}^{i+1}(X, \mathcal{R}) \longrightarrow \dots

  1. \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{E}) is a finitely generated A-module (by Step 2).

  2. \operatorname{H}^{i+1}(X, \mathcal{R}) is a finitely generated A-module (by the inductive hypothesis on degree i+1, applied to the coherent sheaf \mathcal{R}).

The module \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}) is "sandwiched" between these two. Specifically: Let M = \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}). It is an A-module. The image of \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{E}) in M is a quotient of a finitely generated module, hence finitely generated. The quotient M / \operatorname{im}(\operatorname{H}^i(\mathcal{E})) is isomorphic to a submodule of \operatorname{H}^{i+1}(X, \mathcal{R}). Since A is Noetherian, any submodule of a finitely generated module is finitely generated. Since M is an extension of a f.g. module by a f.g. module, M itself is finitely generated. This proves (a).

Proof of (b) Vanishing for \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}(n)) (i > 0): Twist the short exact sequence by n (flatness of twisting): 0 \longrightarrow \mathcal{R}(n) \longrightarrow \mathcal{E}(n) \longrightarrow \mathcal{F}(n) \longrightarrow 0. Consider the relevant segment of the cohomology sequence: \dots \longrightarrow \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{E}(n)) \longrightarrow \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}(n)) \longrightarrow \operatorname{H}^{i+1}(X, \mathcal{R}(n)) \longrightarrow \dots We need to show the middle term vanishes for n \gg 0.

  1. By Step 2, \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{E}(n)) = 0 for n \ge n_1 (since i > 0).

  2. By the inductive hypothesis on degree i+1, applied to \mathcal{R}, there exists n_2 such that \operatorname{H}^{i+1}(X, \mathcal{R}(n)) = 0 for n \ge n_2.

Let n_0 = \max(n_1, n_2). For all n \ge n_0, the outer terms in the sequence are zero: 0 \longrightarrow \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}(n)) \longrightarrow 0. Therefore, \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}(n)) = 0. This proves (b).

Conclusion: By induction, the finiteness holds for all i \ge 0. The vanishing holds for all i > 0. ◻

Euler Characteristic and Arithmetic Genus

Remark 2.1 (Euler Characteristic and Arithmetic Genus). In this section, we turn our attention to some more intriguing topics. The Euler characteristic is a fundamental concept in both homological algebra and differential manifolds; here, we define it in the context of sheaves. Given that a ’bundle’ on a differential manifold is essentially a special case of a (locally free) sheaf, this definition serves as a natural generalization of the classical Euler characteristic. Furthermore, we will define the Hilbert polynomial and the arithmetic genus. While these concepts originated in classical algebraic geometry, we shall define them using modern language. As the reader will discover, these modern definitions remain perfectly compatible with the classical cases.

Definition 2.1 (Euler Characteristic). Let X be a projective scheme over a field k, and let \mathcal{F} be a coherent sheaf on X. The Euler characteristic of \mathcal{F} is defined as: \chi(\mathcal{F}) = \sum_{i \ge 0} (-1)^i \dim_k \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}). Note: By Serre’s Finiteness Theorem, each \dim_k \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}) is finite, and by Grothendieck’s Vanishing Theorem, \operatorname{H}^i = 0 for i > \dim X. Thus, this sum is well-defined and finite.

The reader will observe that our definition of the Euler characteristic for sheaves is essentially consistent with the conventional one. Moreover, it satisfies additivity, a fundamental result in homological algebra. It is the Serre Finiteness Theorem established earlier that ensures this concept is well-defined, by guaranteeing that the dimensions of all cohomology groups involved are finite.

Lemma 2.1 (Additivity of Euler Characteristic). The Euler characteristic is additive on short exact sequences. That is, if 0 \longrightarrow \mathcal{F}' \longrightarrow \mathcal{F} \longrightarrow \mathcal{F}'' \longrightarrow 0 is a short exact sequence of coherent sheaves on X, then: \chi(\mathcal{F}) = \chi(\mathcal{F}') + \chi(\mathcal{F}'').

Proof. Consider the long exact sequence of cohomology derived from the short exact sequence: 0 \to \operatorname{H}^0(\mathcal{F}') \to \operatorname{H}^0(\mathcal{F}) \to \operatorname{H}^0(\mathcal{F}'') \to \operatorname{H}^1(\mathcal{F}') \to \operatorname{H}^1(\mathcal{F}) \to \dots Let h^i(\cdot) = \dim_k \operatorname{H}^i(X, \cdot). A standard result from linear algebra states that for any exact sequence of finite-dimensional vector spaces, the alternating sum of their dimensions is zero. Thus, taking the alternating sum over the entire long exact sequence: \sum_{i \ge 0} (-1)^i \left( h^i(\mathcal{F}') - h^i(\mathcal{F}) + h^i(\mathcal{F}'') \right) = 0. Rearranging the terms (which is valid since the sum is finite), we get: \sum (-1)^i h^i(\mathcal{F}) = \sum (-1)^i h^i(\mathcal{F}') + \sum (-1)^i h^i(\mathcal{F}''). By definition, this is \chi(\mathcal{F}) = \chi(\mathcal{F}') + \chi(\mathcal{F}''). ◻

We now introduce the definition of the Hilbert polynomial. Its central premise is that for a coherent sheaf on a projective scheme, the Euler characteristic of its twists, \chi(X, \mathcal{F}(n)), behaves as a polynomial function in n. The proof proceeds by induction on the dimension of the support. The most formidable challenge lies in defining a suitable map \mathcal{F}(n-1) \to \mathcal{F}(n), [typically achieved by selecting a hyperplane section that does not contain the associated primes of the support].

Theorem 2.1 (Existence of the Hilbert Polynomial). Let X be a projective scheme over a field k, \mathcal{O}_X(1) a very ample invertible sheaf, and \mathcal{F} a coherent sheaf. Then there exists a polynomial P(z) \in \mathbb{Q}[z] such that for all n sufficiently large: \chi(\mathcal{F}(n)) = P(n). This polynomial is called the Hilbert Polynomial of \mathcal{F}.

Proof. We proceed by induction on d = \dim(\operatorname{Supp} \mathcal{F}).

Base Case (d=0): If the support of \mathcal{F} has dimension 0, it consists of a finite set of closed points. In this case, \mathcal{F} is a skyscraper sheaf (technically, an Artinian module over the local rings). Tensoring with \mathcal{O}_X(n) does not change the dimension of the global sections because \mathcal{O}_X(n) is locally trivial and the support is discrete (algebraically, M \otimes_A A(n) \cong M if M has finite length and support at the maximal ideal?). More simply, \operatorname{H}^i(\mathcal{F}(n)) = 0 for i > 0, and \operatorname{H}^0(\mathcal{F}(n)) \cong \operatorname{H}^0(\mathcal{F}) (non-canonically, dimension-wise). Thus, \chi(\mathcal{F}(n)) is a constant, which is a polynomial of degree 0.

Inductive Step (d > 0): Assume the theorem holds for all sheaves with support dimension < d. Let X = \mathbb{P}^N_k (embedding via \mathcal{O}(1)). Let x \in \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}(1)) be a linear form defining a hyperplane H. Consider the multiplication map \mathcal{F}(-1) \xrightarrow{\cdot x} \mathcal{F}. Let \mathcal{K} be the kernel and \mathcal{Q} be the cokernel. We have an exact sequence: 0 \longrightarrow \mathcal{K} \longrightarrow \mathcal{F}(-1) \xrightarrow{\cdot x} \mathcal{F} \longrightarrow \mathcal{Q} \longrightarrow 0. Twisting this sequence by n (which is exact as \mathcal{O}(n) is flat/locally free): 0 \longrightarrow \mathcal{K}(n) \longrightarrow \mathcal{F}(n-1) \longrightarrow \mathcal{F}(n) \longrightarrow \mathcal{Q}(n) \longrightarrow 0. By the additivity of Euler characteristic (splitting the 4-term sequence into two short exact sequences): \chi(\mathcal{F}(n)) - \chi(\mathcal{F}(n-1)) = \chi(\mathcal{Q}(n)) - \chi(\mathcal{K}(n)).

Remark 2.1 (Dimension Drops). How do we ensure the dimension drops? The support of the cokernel \mathcal{Q} is exactly \operatorname{Supp}(\mathcal{F}) \cap H. The support of the kernel \mathcal{K} consists of points where x is a zero-divisor on \mathcal{F}.

To apply induction, we need \dim \operatorname{Supp} \mathcal{K} < d and \dim \operatorname{Supp} \mathcal{Q} < d. Since \mathcal{O}(1) is very ample, we can choose a hyperplane H (defined by x) such that H does not contain any irreducible component of \operatorname{Supp}(\mathcal{F}). (If the field k is infinite, this is easy by prime avoidance. If k is finite, we might strictly need to replace \mathcal{O}(1) by a higher power, or extend scalars, but the polynomial result remains valid).

With such a choice:

1. x is not identically zero on any component of \operatorname{Supp}(\mathcal{F}), so \dim(\operatorname{Supp}(\mathcal{F}) \cap H) < d. Thus \dim \operatorname{Supp} \mathcal{Q} < d.

2. Since x is not a zero divisor on the generic points of \mathcal{F}, the kernel \mathcal{K} is supported on a proper closed subset, so \dim \operatorname{Supp} \mathcal{K} < d.

By the induction hypothesis, \chi(\mathcal{Q}(n)) is a polynomial P_Q(n) and \chi(\mathcal{K}(n)) is a polynomial P_K(n) for large n. Let R(n) = P_Q(n) - P_K(n). We have the relation: \chi(\mathcal{F}(n)) - \chi(\mathcal{F}(n-1)) = R(n) \quad \text{for } n \gg 0. A fundamental lemma in numerical polynomials states that if f(n) - f(n-1) is a polynomial in n, then f(n) is a polynomial in n (of degree one higher, roughly). Thus, \chi(\mathcal{F}(n)) is a polynomial for large n. ◻

Finally, we introduce the concept of the arithmetic genus of a projective scheme, which is likewise defined in terms of the Euler characteristic. It is worth noting that when X is an integral scheme over an algebraically closed field (corresponding to a classical algebraic variety, even without the finite type assumption), the arithmetic genus can be expressed directly through the dimensions of the cohomology groups. In particular, when X is a curve (dimension 1), the arithmetic genus coincides with the dimension of the first cohomology group.

Definition 2.2 (Arithmetic Genus). Let X be a projective scheme of dimension r over a field k. The arithmetic genus p_a(X) is defined by: p_a(X) = (-1)^r (\chi(\mathcal{O}_X) - 1). Note: This depends only on the intrinsic structure of X, not on the projective embedding.

Proposition 2.1 (Genus of Integral Schemes). If X is an integral projective scheme over an algebraically closed field k, then \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X) \cong k. Consequently: p_a(X) = \sum_{i=0}^{r-1} (-1)^i \dim_k \operatorname{H}^{r-i}(X, \mathcal{O}_X). In particular, if X is a curve (r=1), then p_a(X) = \dim_k \operatorname{H}^1(X, \mathcal{O}_X).

Proof. 1. Global Sections are Constants: Since X is proper over k, \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X) is a finite-dimensional k-algebra. Since X is integral, \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X) is an integral domain. A finite-dimensional domain over a field is a field (multiplication is injective implies surjective). Thus \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X) is a finite field extension of k. Since k is algebraically closed, there are no non-trivial finite extensions. Thus \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X) \cong k, and \dim_k \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X) = 1.

2. The Formula: Substitute \chi(\mathcal{O}_X) = \sum_{j=0}^r (-1)^j h^j(\mathcal{O}_X) into the definition: p_a(X) = (-1)^r \left( [h^0 - h^1 + \dots + (-1)^r h^r] - 1 \right). Using h^0 = 1, the first term cancels the -1: p_a(X) = (-1)^r \sum_{j=1}^r (-1)^j h^j(\mathcal{O}_X) = \sum_{j=1}^r (-1)^{r+j} h^j(\mathcal{O}_X). Let i = r-j. As j goes from 1 to r, i goes from r-1 to 0. The exponent becomes r + (r-i) = 2r - i \equiv -i \pmod 2. Thus, p_a(X) = \sum_{i=0}^{r-1} (-1)^i h^{r-i}(X, \mathcal{O}_X).

3. The Curve Case (r=1): The sum runs from i=0 to 0. p_a(X) = (-1)^0 h^{1-0}(\mathcal{O}_X) = h^1(\mathcal{O}_X). ◻

Cohomology of Single Twist Sheaf

The first theorem concerns the cohomology of twisting sheaves on projective space, famously known as ’Serre’s Calculation’. Although the proof may appear lengthy and cumbersome, the core task within the Čech cohomology process is essentially the manipulation of polynomials. It could aptly be described as ’a linear algebra exercise masquerading as algebraic geometry.

Theorem 2.1 (Cohomology of \mathbb{P}^n_A). Let A be a Noetherian ring, let S = A[x_0, \dots, x_n] be the polynomial ring with standard grading, and let X = \mathbb{P}^n_A = \operatorname{Proj} S. We consider the cohomology of the twisting sheaves \mathcal{O}_X(k). It is convenient to compute the direct sum of all twists simultaneously: \Gamma_*(\mathcal{O}_X) := \bigoplus_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X(k)) \quad \text{and generally} \quad M^i := \bigoplus_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{O}_X(k)). Then:

  1. The natural map S \to \Gamma_*(\mathcal{O}_X) is an isomorphism of graded S-modules.

  2. For intermediate degrees 0 < i < n, \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{O}_X(k)) = 0 for all k.

  3. \operatorname{H}^n(X, \mathcal{O}_X(-n-1)) \cong A, and generally M^n \cong \bigoplus_{k} \operatorname{H}^n(X, \mathcal{O}_X(k)) is a free A-module generated by monomials with strictly negative exponents (x_0^{-1}\dots x_n^{-1} etc.).

  4. Serre Duality (Perfect Pairing): The natural map \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X(k)) \times \operatorname{H}^n(X, \mathcal{O}_X(-k-n-1)) \longrightarrow \operatorname{H}^n(X, \mathcal{O}_X(-n-1)) \cong A is a perfect pairing of finitely generated free A-modules.

Proof. Step 0: The Strategy (Geometry to Algebra Translation) Since X = \mathbb{P}^n_A is a separated Noetherian scheme, and \mathcal{O}_X(k) is quasi-coherent, we can compute its cohomology using the Čech complex associated to any affine open cover.

We choose the standard affine cover \mathcal{U} = \{U_0, \dots, U_n\}, where U_i = D_+(x_i) = \operatorname{Spec}(S_{(x_i)}). Instead of computing one sheaf at a time, we define the graded sheaf \mathcal{F} = \bigoplus_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} \mathcal{O}_X(k).

Crucially, the restriction of this graded sheaf to an intersection U_{i_0 \dots i_p} corresponds exactly to the localization of the graded ring S: \mathcal{F}(U_{i_0 \dots i_p}) = \bigoplus_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} \Gamma(D_+(x_{i_0}\dots x_{i_p}), \mathcal{O}(k)) \cong S_{x_{i_0} \dots x_{i_p}}. This isomorphism preserves the grading. Thus, the Čech complex C^\bullet(\mathcal{U}, \mathcal{F}) becomes a complex of graded S-modules: C^\bullet: \quad 0 \longrightarrow \prod_{i} S_{x_i} \xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}^0} \prod_{i<j} S_{x_i x_j} \xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}^1} \dots \xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}^{n-1}} S_{x_0 \dots x_n} \longrightarrow 0. From this point on, the proof is purely about analyzing monomials in localized polynomial rings.

Step 1: Calculating \operatorname{H}^0 (Part a) \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{F}) is the kernel of \operatorname{d}^0. An element \alpha \in \prod S_{x_i} is a tuple (\alpha_0, \dots, \alpha_n) where \alpha_i \in S_{x_i}. The condition \operatorname{d}^0(\alpha) = 0 means \alpha_i = \alpha_j inside S_{x_i x_j} for all i, j.

Algebraically, S is a UFD. If a rational function f can be written with only x_i in the denominator, and also with only x_j in the denominator, and this holds for all i, j, then f must be a polynomial (it has no denominators). Thus, \operatorname{Ker}(\operatorname{d}^0) = S. Matching the grading, \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}(k)) = S_k (homogeneous polynomials of degree k).

Step 2: Calculating \operatorname{H}^n (Part c) \operatorname{H}^n(X, \mathcal{F}) is the cokernel of the last map \operatorname{d}^{n-1}. \bigoplus_{k=0}^n S_{x_0 \dots \hat{x}_k \dots x_n} \xrightarrow{\operatorname{d}^{n-1}} S_{x_0 \dots x_n} \longrightarrow \operatorname{H}^n \longrightarrow 0. Let’s analyze the monomials. S_{x_0 \dots x_n} is the free A-module generated by all monomials of the form x_0^{l_0} \dots x_n^{l_n} where l_i \in \mathbb{Z}. The image of \operatorname{d}^{n-1} comes from localizations where at least one variable, say x_k, is not inverted. This means the image consists of all Laurent monomials x_0^{l_0} \dots x_n^{l_n} where at least one exponent l_k \ge 0.

Therefore, the quotient group (the cokernel) is isomorphic to the free A-module generated by the "leftover" monomials: those where every exponent is strictly negative. \operatorname{H}^n(X, \mathcal{F}) \cong \bigoplus_{l_0, \dots, l_n < 0} A \cdot x_0^{l_0} \dots x_n^{l_n}.

Let’s look at the grading (total degree k = \sum l_i). The "largest" monomial in \operatorname{H}^n corresponds to l_0 = \dots = l_n = -1. The degree is (-1) + \dots + (-1) = -n-1. Thus, \operatorname{H}^n(X, \mathcal{O}(k)) = 0 for k > -n-1. For k = -n-1, the cohomology is generated by the single basis element x_0^{-1} \dots x_n^{-1}. Thus \operatorname{H}^n(X, \mathcal{O}(-n-1)) \cong A.

Step 3: Serre Duality (Part d) We established:

  • \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}(k)) has basis \{ x_0^{a_0} \dots x_n^{a_n} \mid a_i \ge 0, \sum a_i = k \}.

  • \operatorname{H}^n(X, \mathcal{O}(-k-n-1)) has basis \{ x_0^{b_0} \dots x_n^{b_n} \mid b_i < 0, \sum b_i = -k-n-1 \}.

The pairing into \operatorname{H}^n(X, \mathcal{O}(-n-1)) \cong A \cdot (x_0^{-1}\dots x_n^{-1}) is given by multiplication of monomials. (x^\mathsf{a}) \cdot (x^\mathsf{b}) = x^{\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{b}}. Since b_i < 0 and a_i \ge 0, for the product to be x_0^{-1} \dots x_n^{-1}, we must have a_i + b_i = -1, or b_i = -1 - a_i. This correspondence b_i \leftrightarrow -1-a_i sets up a perfect bijection between the basis of \operatorname{H}^0 and the basis of \operatorname{H}^n. Since the bases are dual, the pairing is perfect (determinant is \pm 1).

Step 4: Vanishing of Intermediate Cohomology (Part b) We prove \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}) = 0 for 0 < i < n by induction on the dimension n.

Base Case (n=1): There are no integers i such that 0 < i < 1. The statement is vacuously true.

Inductive Step: Assume the theorem holds for \mathbb{P}^{n-1}. Consider x_n as an element of degree 1 in S. Multiplication by x_n gives an exact sequence of graded S-modules: 0 \longrightarrow S(-1) \xrightarrow{\cdot x_n} S \longrightarrow S/(x_n) \longrightarrow 0. Geometrically, this corresponds to the sequence of sheaves on X = \mathbb{P}^n: 0 \longrightarrow \mathcal{F}(-1) \longrightarrow \mathcal{F} \longrightarrow \mathcal{F}_H \longrightarrow 0, where H \cong \mathbb{P}^{n-1} is the hyperplane defined by x_n=0. Consider the long exact sequence of cohomology. For any i, we have: \dots \to \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}(-1)) \xrightarrow{\cdot x_n} \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}) \to \operatorname{H}^i(H, \mathcal{F}_H) \to \dots Note that \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}(-1)) is just the graded module M^i shifted by 1. So the map is simply the multiplication map M^i \xrightarrow{\cdot x_n} M^i.

For 0 < i < n:

  • If i < n-1: By induction, \operatorname{H}^i(H, \mathcal{F}_H) = 0. The exact sequence becomes M^i(-1) \xrightarrow{\cdot x_n} M^i is surjective.

  • If i = n-1 (and n > 1): We look at \operatorname{H}^{n-1}(H, \mathcal{F}_H) \to \operatorname{H}^n(X, \mathcal{F}(-1)). Let’s look at the algebra instead of the long sequence directly.

The Localization Argument (The Power Move): Let M = \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}). Since this is computed via the Čech complex of S-modules, M is a graded S-module. What happens if we localize M at x_n? Localization is exact. M_{x_n} = \operatorname{H}^i(C^\bullet_{x_n}). The complex (C^\bullet)_{x_n} is the Čech complex for the cover of X intersected with D_+(x_n). But D_+(x_n) is affine! The cohomology of a quasi-coherent sheaf on an affine scheme vanishes for i > 0. Thus, M_{x_n} = 0.

Algebraically, this means for every element m \in M, there is some power k such that x_n^k \cdot m = 0.

Now combine this with the exact sequence from the hyperplane. For 0 < i < n-1, \operatorname{H}^i(\mathbb{P}^{n-1}) = 0, so the map x_n: M^i \to M^i is bijective. If an operator is bijective but locally nilpotent (annihilated by power), the module must be zero. (If x m = 0 and x is injective, m=0). Thus \operatorname{H}^i = 0 for 0 < i < n-1.

The boundary case i = n-1 requires checking the map \operatorname{H}^{n-1}(\mathcal{F}_H) \to \operatorname{H}^n(\mathcal{F}(-1)). Using the explicit calculation of \operatorname{H}^{n-1} (negative monomials in x_0, \dots, x_{n-1}) and \operatorname{H}^n (negative monomials in x_0, \dots, x_n), one can check injectivity/exactness directly to show \operatorname{H}^{n-1}(\mathbb{P}^n) = 0. ◻

Derived Serre Duality on Projective Space

The explicit Čech cohomology computations for the isolated twisting sheaves \mathcal{O}_X(k) in Theorem provide the critical foundation for understanding the cohomology of arbitrary bounded complexes of coherent sheaves on \mathbb{P}^n_A. By leveraging the triangulated structure of the derived category, we can elevate the classical Serre Duality pairing to its ultimate, functorial form.

Definition 2.3 (Dualizing Complex of \mathbb{P}^n). Let X = \mathbb{P}^n_A over a Noetherian ring A. We define the dualizing complex of X as the shifted invertible sheaf: \omega_X^\circ := \mathcal{O}_X(-n-1)[n]

Theorem 2.1 (Derived Serre Duality). Let A be a Noetherian ring and X = \mathbb{P}^n_A. For any bounded complex of coherent sheaves \mathcal{F}^\bullet \in \mathbf{D}^b_{\mathrm{coh}}(X), there is a canonical isomorphism in the derived category of A-modules \mathbf{D}^b(A): \mathbf{R}\Gamma\left(X, \mathbf{R}\mathcal{H}om_{\mathcal{O}_X}(\mathcal{F}^\bullet, \omega_X^\circ)\right) \cong \mathbf{R}\operatorname{Hom}_A\left(\mathbf{R}\Gamma(X, \mathcal{F}^\bullet), A\right)

Proof. As with the Projection Formula, the heavy lifting has already been accomplished by our base calculations, and the proof reduces to a straightforward application of the Way-out Lemma.

Step 1: Functor Setup and Reduction We define two contravariant functors from \mathbf{D}^b_{\mathrm{coh}}(X) to \mathbf{D}^b(A): \begin{align*} L(\mathcal{F}^\bullet) &:= \mathbf{R}\Gamma\left(X, \mathbf{R}\mathcal{H}om_{\mathcal{O}_X}(\mathcal{F}^\bullet, \omega_X^\circ)\right) \\ R(\mathcal{F}^\bullet) &:= \mathbf{R}\operatorname{Hom}_A\left(\mathbf{R}\Gamma(X, \mathcal{F}^\bullet), A\right) \end{align*} Because \mathbf{R}\Gamma, \mathbf{R}\mathcal{H}om, and \mathbf{R}\operatorname{Hom} are all exact functors of triangulated categories, both L and R are exact functors. The derived adjunctions provide a natural transformation \eta: L \to R.

By a classical theorem of Serre, any coherent sheaf on \mathbb{P}^n_A admits a finite resolution by finite direct sums of twisting sheaves \mathcal{O}_X(k). Therefore, the thick triangulated subcategory generated by the set of twists \{\mathcal{O}_X(k)\}_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} is precisely the entire bounded derived category \mathbf{D}^b_{\mathrm{coh}}(X).

Consequently, by the Way-out Lemma (Theorem ), to prove that \eta is an isomorphism for all bounded complexes \mathcal{F}^\bullet, it is completely sufficient to verify that it is an isomorphism for the isolated twisting sheaves \mathcal{F}^\bullet = \mathcal{O}_X(k) concentrated in degree 0.

Step 2: Verification of the Base Case Let \mathcal{F}^\bullet = \mathcal{O}_X(k). We evaluate both exact functors on this isolated sheaf.

For the left-hand side, since \mathcal{O}_X(k) is locally free, the derived \mathcal{H}om simplifies to the classical dual tensor product: L(\mathcal{O}_X(k)) = \mathbf{R}\Gamma(X, \mathcal{O}_X(-k) \otimes \mathcal{O}_X(-n-1)[n]) = \mathbf{R}\Gamma(X, \mathcal{O}_X(-k-n-1))[n] Taking the i-th cohomology of this complex shifts the index, yielding \operatorname{H}^{i+n}(X, \mathcal{O}_X(-k-n-1)).

For the right-hand side, since A is a ring, evaluating the derived Hom into A corresponds to taking the standard A-module dual of the cohomology groups: R(\mathcal{O}_X(k)) = \mathbf{R}\operatorname{Hom}_A(\mathbf{R}\Gamma(X, \mathcal{O}_X(k)), A) Taking the i-th cohomology yields \operatorname{Hom}_A(\operatorname{H}^{-i}(X, \mathcal{O}_X(k)), A).

We now compare the cohomology of L(\mathcal{O}_X(k)) and R(\mathcal{O}_X(k)) degree by degree:

  • When i = -n, the LHS is \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X(-k-n-1)) and the RHS is \operatorname{Hom}_A(\operatorname{H}^n(X, \mathcal{O}_X(k)), A).

  • When i = 0, the LHS is \operatorname{H}^n(X, \mathcal{O}_X(-k-n-1)) and the RHS is \operatorname{Hom}_A(\operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X(k)), A).

  • For all other degrees, Theorem (b) dictates that the intermediate cohomology groups vanish identically, so both sides are trivially zero.

By Theorem (d) (Serre Duality for isolated twists), the natural pairing perfectly identifies these non-vanishing top and bottom cohomology groups as dual free A-modules. Thus, \eta_{\mathcal{O}_X(k)} induces an isomorphism on all cohomology groups, proving it is a quasi-isomorphism (an isomorphism in \mathbf{D}^b(A)).

Since the base case holds for all k \in \mathbb{Z}, the Way-out Lemma guarantees the theorem holds for all bounded complexes in \mathbf{D}^b_{\mathrm{coh}}(X). ◻

Remark 2.1 (Hypercohomology Amplitude). An immediate consequence of this derived approach (and Theorem (b)) is that if a complex \mathcal{E}^\bullet \in \mathbf{D}^b(X) can be represented by a bounded complex of direct sums of twisting sheaves \mathcal{O}_X(k) where k > -n-1, its hypercohomology is strictly bounded. The vanishing of the intermediate cohomology of the isolated sheaves strictly limits how far the “ghosts” of the differentials can propagate through the hypercohomology spectral sequence.

Beilinson’s Theorem and Exceptional Collections

While Derived Serre Duality brilliantly generalizes part (d) of Theorem to arbitrary complexes, one might naturally ask: what is the derived-category analogue of parts (a), (b), and (c)?

Unlike Serre Duality, which is a universal formula, parts (a), (b), and (c) explicitly compute the exact values and vanishing of the cohomology for the specific sheaves \mathcal{O}_X(k). When generalized to the derived category, these isolated calculations do not simply yield a generic formula for an arbitrary complex \mathcal{F}^\bullet. Instead, they reveal the structural skeleton of the entire bounded derived category \mathbf{D}^b_{\mathrm{coh}}(\mathbb{P}^n_A).

The vanishing of intermediate cohomology (Theorem (b)) translates directly into the vanishing of higher \operatorname{Ext} groups between twisting sheaves, which leads to the powerful concept of an exceptional collection.

Definition 2.4 (Strongly Exceptional Collection). An object E \in \mathbf{D}^b_{\mathrm{coh}}(X) is called exceptional if \operatorname{Hom}(E, E) = A and \operatorname{Ext}^i(E, E) = 0 for all i \ne 0.

An ordeUniBlue sequence of exceptional objects (E_0, E_1, \dots, E_m) is called a strongly exceptional collection if for all j > i and all k \ne 0: \operatorname{Ext}^k(E_i, E_j) = 0 and for all j < i and all k \in \mathbb{Z}: \operatorname{Ext}^k(E_i, E_j) = 0

Using our base calculations from Theorem , we can prove one of the most celebrated theorems in derived algebraic geometry.

Theorem 2.1 (Beilinson’s Exceptional Collection for \mathbb{P}^n). Let X = \mathbb{P}^n_A over a field (or Noetherian ring) A. The sequence of twisting sheaves of length n+1: \left( \mathcal{O}_X, \mathcal{O}_X(1), \dots, \mathcal{O}_X(n) \right) forms a full strongly exceptional collection for \mathbf{D}^b_{\mathrm{coh}}(\mathbb{P}^n_A).

Proof. Let us verify the definition by evaluating the derived Hom spaces, which are equivalent to Ext groups: \operatorname{Ext}^k(\mathcal{O}_X(i), \mathcal{O}_X(j)) \cong \operatorname{H}^k(X, \mathcal{O}_X(j-i)). We check the three conditions requiUniBlue for a strongly exceptional collection based entirely on Theorem (a, b, c):

1. Exceptional Objects (Self-Hom): For any i, \operatorname{H}^k(X, \mathcal{O}_X(i-i)) = \operatorname{H}^k(X, \mathcal{O}_X). By Theorem (a), \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X) = S_0 = A. By Theorem (b, c), \operatorname{H}^k(X, \mathcal{O}_X) = 0 for all k > 0. Thus, each \mathcal{O}_X(i) is an exceptional object.

2. Forward Homomorphisms (j > i): For j > i, we have 0 < j-i \le n. The higher extensions are \operatorname{Ext}^k(\mathcal{O}_X(i), \mathcal{O}_X(j)) \cong \operatorname{H}^k(X, \mathcal{O}_X(j-i)). By Theorem (b), the intermediate cohomology groups exactly in this range strictly vanish: \operatorname{H}^k(X, \mathcal{O}_X(j-i)) = 0 for k > 0. Therefore, there are no higher extensions mapping forward. (Note: the degree-0 morphisms are given by polynomials of degree j-i according to Theorem (a)).

3. Backward Homomorphisms (j < i): For j < i, we have -n \le j-i < 0. We must check \operatorname{Ext}^k(\mathcal{O}_X(i), \mathcal{O}_X(j)) \cong \operatorname{H}^k(X, \mathcal{O}_X(j-i)) for all k. For k = 0, there are no polynomials of negative degree, so \operatorname{H}^0 = 0 (Theorem (a)). For 0 < k < n, the cohomology vanishes by Theorem (b). For k = n, since the twist j-i is bounded strictly between 0 and -n-1 (i.e., -n \le j-i \le -1), it avoids the threshold for non-zero top cohomology requiUniBlue by Theorem (c). Thus, \operatorname{H}^n = 0. Consequently, all backward morphisms and extensions completely vanish. ◻

Remark 2.1 (Beilinson’s Equivalence). The term "full" implies that this sequence generates the entire derived category. Any bounded complex in \mathbf{D}^b_{\mathrm{coh}}(\mathbb{P}^n_A) can be constructed via finite exact triangles involving only these n+1 line bundles. This is the profound legacy of Theorem (a, b, c): it completely reduces the derived category of projective space to the quiver representations of a finite-dimensional algebra.

Derived Euler Characteristic and Triangulated Additivity

In \mathbf{D}^b_{\mathrm{coh}}(X), the isolated sheaves are replaced by bounded complexes, the standard cohomology groups are replaced by hypercohomology, and short exact sequences are subsumed by exact triangles.

Definition 2.5 (Derived Euler Characteristic). Let X be a projective scheme over a field k. For any bounded complex of coherent sheaves \mathcal{F}^\bullet \in \mathbf{D}^b_{\mathrm{coh}}(X), its Euler characteristic is defined as the alternating sum of the dimensions of its hypercohomology groups: \chi(\mathcal{F}^\bullet) := \sum_{i \in \mathbb{Z}} (-1)^i \dim_k \mathbb{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}^\bullet) = \sum_{i \in \mathbb{Z}} (-1)^i \dim_k \operatorname{H}^i(\mathbf{R}\Gamma(X, \mathcal{F}^\bullet)). Since \mathcal{F}^\bullet is bounded and X is projective, \mathbf{R}\Gamma(X, \mathcal{F}^\bullet) has only finitely many non-zero cohomology groups, each of which is finite-dimensional. Thus, the sum is well-defined and finite.

Theorem 2.1 (Additivity on Exact Triangles). Let X be a projective scheme over a field k. If \mathcal{A}^\bullet \longrightarrow \mathcal{B}^\bullet \longrightarrow \mathcal{C}^\bullet \xrightarrow{+1} is an exact triangle in \mathbf{D}^b_{\mathrm{coh}}(X), then the Euler characteristic is additive: \chi(\mathcal{B}^\bullet) = \chi(\mathcal{A}^\bullet) + \chi(\mathcal{C}^\bullet).

Proof. Because the derived global section functor \mathbf{R}\Gamma(X, -) is an exact functor of triangulated categories, applying it to our exact triangle yields an exact triangle in the derived category of k-vector spaces \mathbf{D}^b(k\text{-Vect}): \mathbf{R}\Gamma(X, \mathcal{A}^\bullet) \longrightarrow \mathbf{R}\Gamma(X, \mathcal{B}^\bullet) \longrightarrow \mathbf{R}\Gamma(X, \mathcal{C}^\bullet) \xrightarrow{+1} Taking the cohomology of this exact triangle produces the standard long exact sequence of hypercohomology groups: \cdots \longrightarrow \mathbb{H}^i(X, \mathcal{A}^\bullet) \longrightarrow \mathbb{H}^i(X, \mathcal{B}^\bullet) \longrightarrow \mathbb{H}^i(X, \mathcal{C}^\bullet) \longrightarrow \mathbb{H}^{i+1}(X, \mathcal{A}^\bullet) \longrightarrow \cdots By the Rank-Nullity Theorem in linear algebra, the alternating sum of the dimensions of the vector spaces in any bounded exact sequence is equal to zero. Therefore: \sum_{i \in \mathbb{Z}} (-1)^i \dim_k \mathbb{H}^i(X, \mathcal{A}^\bullet) - \sum_{i \in \mathbb{Z}} (-1)^i \dim_k \mathbb{H}^i(X, \mathcal{B}^\bullet) + \sum_{i \in \mathbb{Z}} (-1)^i \dim_k \mathbb{H}^i(X, \mathcal{C}^\bullet) = 0. Rearranging this equation gives precisely \chi(\mathcal{A}^\bullet) - \chi(\mathcal{B}^\bullet) + \chi(\mathcal{C}^\bullet) = 0, which completes the proof. ◻

This triangulated additivity yields a remarkably practical corollary for actually computing the Euler characteristic of a complex without needing to compute its hypercohomology or spectral sequences.

Corollary 2.1 (Euler Characteristic via Components and Cohomology Sheaves). For any bounded complex \mathcal{F}^\bullet \in \mathbf{D}^b_{\mathrm{coh}}(X), its Euler characteristic can be computed by taking the alternating sum of the classical Euler characteristics of its individual component sheaves, or alternatively, of its cohomology sheaves: \chi(\mathcal{F}^\bullet) = \sum_{p \in \mathbb{Z}} (-1)^p \chi(\mathcal{F}^p) = \sum_{p \in \mathbb{Z}} (-1)^p \chi(\mathcal{H}^p(\mathcal{F}^\bullet)).

Proof. Any bounded complex \mathcal{F}^\bullet can be systematically filtered by "stupid truncations" \sigma_{\ge p}\mathcal{F}^\bullet, which generate exact triangles of the form: \mathcal{F}^p[-p] \longrightarrow \sigma_{\ge p}\mathcal{F}^\bullet \longrightarrow \sigma_{\ge p+1}\mathcal{F}^\bullet \xrightarrow{+1} Applying Theorem inductively (or simply using the Way-out Lemma), the Euler characteristic of the entire complex decomposes into the sum of the Euler characteristics of its individual terms \mathcal{F}^p[-p]. Since shifting a complex by [-p] shifts its hypercohomology degrees by p, we have \chi(\mathcal{G}^\bullet[-p]) = (-1)^p \chi(\mathcal{G}^\bullet). Treating each \mathcal{F}^p as a complex concentrated in degree 0, we obtain \chi(\mathcal{F}^\bullet) = \sum (-1)^p \chi(\mathcal{F}^p). The identical logic applied to "canonical truncations" \tau_{\le p}\mathcal{F}^\bullet (which isolate the cohomology sheaves) yields the second equality \chi(\mathcal{F}^\bullet) = \sum (-1)^p \chi(\mathcal{H}^p(\mathcal{F}^\bullet)). ◻

Remark 2.1 (The Grothendieck Group K_0(X)). Theorem indicates that the Euler characteristic \chi: \operatorname{Ob}(\mathbf{D}^b_{\mathrm{coh}}(X)) \to \mathbb{Z} is a "universal additive invariant". It formally factors through the Grothendieck group K_0(\mathbf{D}^b_{\mathrm{coh}}(X)), which is defined as the free Abelian group generated by the objects of \mathbf{D}^b_{\mathrm{coh}}(X), modulo the relation [\mathcal{B}^\bullet] = [\mathcal{A}^\bullet] + [\mathcal{C}^\bullet] for every exact triangle. In modern algebraic geometry, K_0(X) completely abstracts this additive behavior.

The Hilbert Polynomial in the Derived Category

The additivity of the Euler characteristic provides a powerful mechanism to study the asymptotic behavior of a coherent sheaf under twisting. We first establish the foundational existence theorem for an isolated coherent sheaf using a classical geometric argument, and then elegantly elevate it to the derived category using our triangulated tools.

Theorem 2.1 (Existence of the Hilbert Polynomial). Let X be a projective scheme over a field k, \mathcal{O}_X(1) a very ample invertible sheaf, and \mathcal{F} a coherent sheaf. Then there exists a polynomial P(z) \in \mathbb{Q}[z] such that for all n sufficiently large: \chi(\mathcal{F}(n)) = P(n). This polynomial is called the Hilbert Polynomial of \mathcal{F}.

Proof. We proceed by induction on d = \dim(\operatorname{Supp} \mathcal{F}).

Base Case (d = 0): If the support of \mathcal{F} has dimension 0, it consists of a finite set of closed points. In this case, \mathcal{F} is a skyscraper sheaf (an Artinian module over the local rings). Tensoring with \mathcal{O}_X(n) does not change the dimension of the global sections because \mathcal{O}_X(n) is locally trivial and the support is discrete. Consequently, \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}(n)) = 0 for i > 0, and \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{F}(n)) \cong \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{F}). Thus, \chi(\mathcal{F}(n)) is a constant, which is a polynomial of degree 0.

Inductive Step (d > 0): Assume the theorem holds for all sheaves with support dimension < d. Let X \hookrightarrow \mathbb{P}^N_k be the embedding via \mathcal{O}_X(1). Let x \in \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X(1)) be a linear form defining a hyperplane H. Consider the multiplication map \mathcal{F}(-1) \xrightarrow{x} \mathcal{F}. Let \mathcal{K} be the kernel and \mathcal{Q} be the cokernel. We have an exact sequence: 0 \longrightarrow \mathcal{K} \longrightarrow \mathcal{F}(-1) \xrightarrow{x} \mathcal{F} \longrightarrow \mathcal{Q} \longrightarrow 0. Twisting this sequence by n (which preserves exactness since \mathcal{O}_X(n) is locally free): 0 \longrightarrow \mathcal{K}(n) \longrightarrow \mathcal{F}(n-1) \longrightarrow \mathcal{F}(n) \longrightarrow \mathcal{Q}(n) \longrightarrow 0. By the additivity of the Euler characteristic (splitting the 4-term sequence into two short exact sequences), we obtain: \chi(\mathcal{F}(n)) - \chi(\mathcal{F}(n-1)) = \chi(\mathcal{Q}(n)) - \chi(\mathcal{K}(n)).

To apply the induction hypothesis, we must ensure the dimensions drop. The support of \mathcal{Q} is exactly \operatorname{Supp}(\mathcal{F}) \cap H. Since \mathcal{O}_X(1) is very ample, we can choose a hyperplane H (defined by x) such that H does not contain any irreducible component of \operatorname{Supp}(\mathcal{F}). With such a choice: 1. x is not identically zero on any component of \operatorname{Supp}(\mathcal{F}), so \dim \operatorname{Supp} \mathcal{Q} < d. 2. Since x is not a zero-divisor on the generic points of \mathcal{F}, the kernel \mathcal{K} is supported strictly on a proper closed subset, so \dim \operatorname{Supp} \mathcal{K} < d.

By the induction hypothesis, \chi(\mathcal{Q}(n)) is a polynomial P_Q(n) and \chi(\mathcal{K}(n)) is a polynomial P_K(n) for large n. Letting R(n) = P_Q(n) - P_K(n), we have the relation: \chi(\mathcal{F}(n)) - \chi(\mathcal{F}(n-1)) = R(n) \quad \text{for } n \gg 0. A fundamental lemma in numerical polynomials states that if f(n) - f(n-1) is a polynomial in n, then f(n) is itself a polynomial in n (of degree one higher) for large n. Thus, \chi(\mathcal{F}(n)) is a polynomial for large n. ◻

With the classical theorem established, the triangulated additivity from the previous section allows us to bypass any complex geometric hyperplane constructions for bounded complexes.

Theorem 2.1 (Derived Hilbert Polynomial). Let X be a projective scheme over a field k with a very ample invertible sheaf \mathcal{O}_X(1). For any bounded complex of coherent sheaves \mathcal{F}^\bullet \in \mathbf{D}^b_{\mathrm{coh}}(X), the Euler characteristic \chi(\mathcal{F}^\bullet(n)) agrees with a polynomial P_{\mathcal{F}^\bullet}(n) \in \mathbb{Q}[z] for all n sufficiently large.

Proof. Recall that the twist of a complex is defined via the derived tensor product \mathcal{F}^\bullet(n) := \mathcal{F}^\bullet \otimes^{\mathbf{L}}_{\mathcal{O}_X} \mathcal{O}_X(n). Since \mathcal{O}_X(n) is locally free (and hence flat), the derived tensor product coincides with the standard term-wise tensor product, and it is an exact functor. Therefore, it commutes with taking cohomology sheaves: \mathcal{H}^i(\mathcal{F}^\bullet(n)) \cong \mathcal{H}^i(\mathcal{F}^\bullet) \otimes_{\mathcal{O}_X} \mathcal{O}_X(n) = \mathcal{H}^i(\mathcal{F}^\bullet)(n). By Corollary  (Euler Characteristic via Cohomology Sheaves), the Euler characteristic of the complex is the alternating sum of the Euler characteristics of its cohomology sheaves: \chi(\mathcal{F}^\bullet(n)) = \sum_{i \in \mathbb{Z}} (-1)^i \chi\left(\mathcal{H}^i(\mathcal{F}^\bullet(n))\right) = \sum_{i \in \mathbb{Z}} (-1)^i \chi\left(\mathcal{H}^i(\mathcal{F}^\bullet)(n)\right). Since \mathcal{F}^\bullet \in \mathbf{D}^b_{\mathrm{coh}}(X), it has only finitely many non-zero cohomology sheaves, and each \mathcal{H}^i(\mathcal{F}^\bullet) is a coherent sheaf. By Theorem , each \chi\left(\mathcal{H}^i(\mathcal{F}^\bullet)(n)\right) is a polynomial P_i(n) for n \gg 0. Consequently, \chi(\mathcal{F}^\bullet(n)) is simply a finite alternating sum of polynomials, which is inherently a polynomial. ◻

Remark 2.1 (Invariants in the Derived Category). The derived Hilbert polynomial naturally inherits the additivity on exact triangles. It demonstrates that vital numerical invariants of a complex, such as its rank, degree, and the geometric dimension of its support, can be fully extracted from its cohomology sheaves without losing any information to the differentials.

Arithmetic Genus

Finally, we introduce the concept of the arithmetic genus of a projective scheme, which is likewise defined in terms of the Euler characteristic. It is worth noting that when X is an integral scheme over an algebraically closed field (corresponding to a classical algebraic variety, even without the finite type assumption), the arithmetic genus can be expressed directly through the dimensions of the cohomology groups. In particular, when X is a curve (dimension 1), the arithmetic genus coincides with the dimension of the first cohomology group.

Definition 2.6 (Arithmetic Genus). Let X be a projective scheme of dimension r over a field k. The arithmetic genus p_a(X) is defined by: p_a(X) = (-1)^r(\chi(\mathcal{O}_X) - 1).

Remark 2.1 (Intrinsic Nature). This definition depends only on the intrinsic structure of X (specifically, its structure sheaf \mathcal{O}_X), and is completely independent of the choice of projective embedding.

Proposition 2.1 (Genus of Integral Schemes). If X is an integral projective scheme over an algebraically closed field k, then \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X) \cong k. Consequently: p_a(X) = \sum_{i=0}^{r-1} (-1)^i \dim_k \operatorname{H}^{r-i}(X, \mathcal{O}_X). In particular, if X is a curve (r = 1), then p_a(X) = \dim_k \operatorname{H}^1(X, \mathcal{O}_X).

Proof. We divide the proof into three logical steps.

1. Global Sections are Constants: Since X is proper over k, \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X) is a finite-dimensional k-algebra. Since X is integral, \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X) is an integral domain. A finite-dimensional domain over a field is necessarily a field (since multiplication by any non-zero element is an injective linear map, which must be surjective by dimension counting). Thus, \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X) is a finite field extension of k. Since k is algebraically closed, it admits no non-trivial finite extensions. Therefore, \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X) \cong k, which implies \dim_k \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X) = 1.

2. The Formula: We substitute the expanded Euler characteristic \chi(\mathcal{O}_X) = \sum_{j=0}^r (-1)^j h^j(\mathcal{O}_X) into the definition of the arithmetic genus: p_a(X) = (-1)^r \left( [h^0 - h^1 + \cdots + (-1)^r h^r] - 1 \right). Using h^0 = 1 from Step 1, the first term cancels the -1: p_a(X) = (-1)^r \sum_{j=1}^r (-1)^j h^j(\mathcal{O}_X) = \sum_{j=1}^r (-1)^{r+j} h^j(\mathcal{O}_X). Let i = r - j. As j ranges from 1 to r, the new index i ranges from r-1 down to 0. The exponent becomes r + (r-i) = 2r - i. Since 2r is even, (-1)^{2r-i} = (-1)^{-i} = (-1)^i. Thus, we obtain: p_a(X) = \sum_{i=0}^{r-1} (-1)^i h^{r-i}(X, \mathcal{O}_X).

3. The Curve Case (r=1): If the dimension is r=1, the sum runs solely from i=0 to 0: p_a(X) = (-1)^0 h^{1-0}(\mathcal{O}_X) = h^1(\mathcal{O}_X). This completes the proof. ◻

Remark 2.1 (Derived Category Perspective). In the context of the derived category \mathbf{D}^b_{\mathrm{coh}}(X), the structure sheaf \mathcal{O}_X acts as the identity object for the derived tensor product \otimes^{\mathbf{L}}. The arithmetic genus can therefore be viewed as a normalized measure of the hypercohomology of the monoidal unit object \mathcal{O}_X \in \mathbf{D}^b_{\mathrm{coh}}(X). Because it is built from the Euler characteristic, it is a strictly additive invariant on exact triangles.

Examples of Projective Schemes

Example 2.1 (Euler Characteristic and Genus of \mathbb{P}^k). Let X = \mathbb{P}^k_A over a Noetherian ring A. We compute the invariants for the twisted structure sheaves \mathcal{O}_X(n).

1. Euler Characteristic \chi(\mathcal{O}_X(n)): By Serre’s Calculation, the cohomology of \mathbb{P}^k is concentrated in degrees 0 and k.

  • For n \ge 0: \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}(n)) is the free A-module of homogeneous polynomials of degree n in k+1 variables. Its rank is the combinatorial number \binom{n+k}{k}. All higher cohomology vanishes. \chi(\mathcal{O}_X(n)) = h^0 - h^1 + \dots = \binom{n+k}{k}.

  • For n < 0: The formula still holds as a polynomial. Specifically, for n \ll 0, only \operatorname{H}^k is non-zero. By Serre Duality, \operatorname{H}^k(\mathcal{O}(n)) \cong \operatorname{H}^0(\mathcal{O}(-n-k-1))^\vee. The rank is \binom{(-n-k-1)+k}{k} = \binom{-n-1}{k}. Combinatorially, \binom{n+k}{k} = (-1)^k \binom{-n-1}{k} is a standard identity.

Thus, the Hilbert polynomial of \mathbb{P}^k is: P_{\mathbb{P}^k}(z) = \binom{z+k}{k} = \frac{(z+k)(z+k-1)\dots(z+1)}{k!}.

2. Arithmetic Genus: Using the definition p_a(X) = (-1)^k (\chi(\mathcal{O}_X) - 1): \chi(\mathcal{O}_X) = P_{\mathbb{P}^k}(0) = \binom{k}{k} = 1. Therefore: p_a(\mathbb{P}^k) = (-1)^k (1 - 1) = 0. This confirms that projective spaces are "genus zero" objects.

Example 2.1 (Degree and Genus of a Plane Curve). Let X be a curve in \mathbb{P}^2_k defined by a single homogeneous polynomial f \in S = k[x_0, x_1, x_2] of degree d. We view X as the closed subscheme \operatorname{Proj}(S/(f)).

1. The Fundamental Exact Sequence: We start with the sequence of graded S-modules: 0 \longrightarrow S(-d) \xrightarrow{\cdot f} S \longrightarrow S/(f) \longrightarrow 0. Why is this exact?

  • The map S \to S/(f) is the canonical projection, so it is surjective (cokernel).

  • The map S(-d) \xrightarrow{\cdot f} S is multiplication by f. Since S = k[x_0, x_1, x_2] is an integral domain and f \neq 0, this multiplication map is injective (no zero divisors).

  • The shift (-d) ensures the map preserves degree: an element of degree n in S(-d) (which is degree n-d in S) maps to degree (n-d)+d = n.

Applying the sheafification functor (which is exact), we obtain the short exact sequence of sheaves on \mathbb{P}^2: 0 \longrightarrow \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^2}(-d) \longrightarrow \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^2} \longrightarrow \iota_* \mathcal{O}_X \longrightarrow 0, where \iota: X \hookrightarrow \mathbb{P}^2 is the inclusion. We will denote \iota_* \mathcal{O}_X simply as \mathcal{O}_X.

2. The Hilbert Polynomial: We twist the sequence by \mathcal{O}(n) (preserving exactness): 0 \longrightarrow \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^2}(n-d) \longrightarrow \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^2}(n) \longrightarrow \mathcal{O}_X(n) \longrightarrow 0. Using the additivity of the Euler characteristic: \chi(\mathcal{O}_X(n)) = \chi(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^2}(n)) - \chi(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^2}(n-d)). Substituting the formula from Example 1 (with k=2): P_X(n) = \binom{n+2}{2} - \binom{(n-d)+2}{2} = \frac{(n+2)(n+1)}{2} - \frac{(n-d+2)(n-d+1)}{2}. Expanding the terms: \begin{align*} P_X(n) &= \frac{1}{2} \left[ (n^2+3n+2) - ((n-d)^2 + 3(n-d) + 2) \right] \\ &= \frac{1}{2} \left[ n^2+3n+2 - (n^2 - 2dn + \operatorname{d}^2 + 3n - 3d + 2) \right] \\ &= \frac{1}{2} \left[ 2dn - \operatorname{d}^2 + 3d \right] \\ &= d \cdot n + \frac{3d - \operatorname{d}^2}{2}. \end{align*}

Interpretation:

  • Degree: The leading coefficient is d, which matches the degree of the curve (intersection number with a line).

  • Constant Term: The constant term is \frac{3d - \operatorname{d}^2}{2}.

3. The Genus Formula: Since X is a curve (dimension 1), its arithmetic genus is defined as: p_a(X) = 1 - \chi(\mathcal{O}_X). We calculate \chi(\mathcal{O}_X) = P_X(0): \chi(\mathcal{O}_X) = \frac{3d - \operatorname{d}^2}{2}. Therefore: p_a(X) = 1 - \frac{3d - \operatorname{d}^2}{2} = \frac{2 - 3d + \operatorname{d}^2}{2} = \frac{(d-1)(d-2)}{2}. This recovers the famous Degree-Genus Formula for plane curves. (e.g., line d=1 \implies p_a=0; cubic d=3 \implies p_a=1).

Let X \subset \mathbb{P}^N be a hypersurface defined by a homogeneous polynomial of degree d. The structure sheaf fits into the short exact sequence: 0 \longrightarrow \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^N}(-d) \longrightarrow \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^N} \longrightarrow \mathcal{O}_X \longrightarrow 0.

1. Hilbert Polynomial: Using the additivity of the Euler characteristic and the known formula for projective space \chi(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^N}(k)) = \binom{k+N}{N}: P_X(n) = \binom{n+N}{N} - \binom{n-d+N}{N}.

2. Arithmetic Genus: The arithmetic genus is defined as p_a(X) = (-1)^{\dim X} (\chi(\mathcal{O}_X) - 1). Note that \dim X = N-1. Calculating \chi(\mathcal{O}_X) = P_X(0): \chi(\mathcal{O}_X) = \binom{N}{N} - \binom{N-d}{N} = 1 - \binom{N-d}{N}. Using the combinatorial identity \binom{-k}{r} = (-1)^r \binom{k+r-1}{r}, we treat \binom{N-d}{N} as \binom{-(d-N)}{N}: \binom{N-d}{N} = (-1)^N \binom{(d-N)+N-1}{N} = (-1)^N \binom{d-1}{N}. Substituting back into the genus formula: \begin{align*} p_a(X) &= (-1)^{N-1} \left( [1 - (-1)^N \binom{d-1}{N}] - 1 \right) \\ &= (-1)^{N-1} \left( - (-1)^N \binom{d-1}{N} \right) \\ &= (-1)^{2N-1} (-1) \binom{d-1}{N} \\ &= \binom{d-1}{N}. \end{align*} For example, a plane curve (N=2) has p_a = \binom{d-1}{2}, consistent with the previous section.

Theorem 2.1 (Invariants of Complete Intersections). Let X \subset \mathbb{P}^N be a complete intersection defined by m homogeneous polynomials f_1, \dots, f_m of degrees d_1, \dots, d_m. The structure sheaf \mathcal{O}_X is resolved by the Koszul Complex \mathcal{K}_\bullet(f_1, \dots, f_m): 0 \to \mathcal{L}_m \to \dots \to \mathcal{L}_2 \to \mathcal{L}_1 \to \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^N} \to \mathcal{O}_X \to 0. The term \mathcal{L}_k corresponds to the k-th exterior power of the direct sum of line bundles corresponding to the equations. Explicitly: \mathcal{L}_k = \bigwedge^k \left( \bigoplus_{i=1}^m \mathcal{O}(-d_i) \right) \cong \bigoplus_{1 \le i_1 < \dots < i_k \le m} \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^N}\left( -\sum_{j=1}^k d_{i_j} \right).

1. Euler Characteristic via Inclusion-Exclusion: By the additivity of Euler characteristic on long exact sequences, \chi(\mathcal{O}_X) is the alternating sum of the characteristics of the resolution terms: \chi(\mathcal{O}_X(n)) = \sum_{k=0}^m (-1)^k \chi(\mathcal{L}_k(n)). Substituting the binomial formula for each summand: \chi(\mathcal{O}_X(n)) = \sum_{k=0}^{m} (-1)^k \sum_{1 \le i_1 < \dots < i_k \le m} \binom{n - (\operatorname{d}_{i_1} + \dots + d_{i_k}) + N}{N}. (Note: The k=0 term is \binom{n+N}{N}, corresponding to \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^N}(n)).

2. Arithmetic Genus: Setting n=0 to find \chi(\mathcal{O}_X), and using p_a(X) = (-1)^{\dim X}(\chi(\mathcal{O}_X) - 1) where \dim X = N-m: p_a(X) = (-1)^{N-m} \left[ \left( \sum_{k=0}^m (-1)^k \sum_{1 \le i_1 < \dots < i_k \le m} \binom{N - \sum d_{i_j}}{N} \right) - 1 \right].

Proof. The proof consists of three main steps: constructing the algebraic Koszul complex, establishing its exactness via the Regular Sequence property, and computing the Euler characteristic via sheafification.

Step 1: Construction of the Algebraic Koszul Complex Let S = k[x_0, \dots, x_N] be the graded polynomial ring. Let M be a graded S-module. For a single homogeneous element f \in S of degree d, we define the elementary complex K_\bullet(f) as: 0 \longrightarrow S(-d) \xrightarrow{\cdot f} S \longrightarrow 0, concentrated in homological degrees 1 and 0. For the sequence of polynomials \mathsf{f} = (f_1, \dots, f_m), the Koszul complex K_\bullet(\mathsf{f}) is defined as the tensor product of these elementary complexes over S: K_\bullet(\mathsf{f}) := K_\bullet(f_1) \otimes_S K_\bullet(f_2) \otimes_S \dots \otimes_S K_\bullet(f_m). The terms of this complex are exterior powers of the free module E = \bigoplus_{i=1}^m S(-d_i). Specifically, the term in degree k is: K_k \cong \bigwedge^k E = \bigwedge^k \left( \bigoplus_{i=1}^m S(-d_i) \right) \cong \bigoplus_{1 \le i_1 < \dots < i_k \le m} S\left( -\sum_{j=1}^k d_{i_j} \right). The boundary maps are given by the standard differential of the exterior algebra contraction with the vector (f_1, \dots, f_m).

Step 2: Exactness of the Complex (The Geometric Input) The homology of the Koszul complex H_k(\mathsf{f}) measures the relations between the generators.

  • H_0(\mathsf{f}) \cong S/(f_1, \dots, f_m).

  • H_k(\mathsf{f}) = 0 for all k > 0 if and only if f_1, \dots, f_m form a regular sequence.

Geometric justification: Since X is a complete intersection in \mathbb{P}^N, the codimension of X is exactly m. This implies that locally on the affine charts, the equations f_1, \dots, f_m cut down the dimension by 1 at each step. Thus, f_1, \dots, f_m form a regular sequence in the local rings \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^N, x}. While they might not be a regular sequence globally in S (due to the irrelevant ideal (x_0, \dots, x_N)), the failure of exactness is confined to modules of finite length (supported at the vertex of the affine cone). However, when we pass to sheaves, "finite length modules" become zero. Thus, the sheafified Koszul complex is exact.

Step 3: The Sheaf Resolution Applying the sheafification functor \sim (which is exact) to the complex K_\bullet(\mathsf{f}), we obtain a locally free resolution of the structure sheaf \mathcal{O}_X: 0 \longrightarrow \mathcal{L}_m \longrightarrow \mathcal{L}_{m-1} \longrightarrow \dots \longrightarrow \mathcal{L}_1 \longrightarrow \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^N} \longrightarrow \mathcal{O}_X \longrightarrow 0. Here \mathcal{L}_k = \widetilde{K_k}. Using the explicit form from Step 1: \mathcal{L}_k = \bigoplus_{|I|=k} \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^N}\left( -d_I \right), \quad \text{where } d_I = \sum_{i \in I} d_i.

Step 4: Calculation of Euler Characteristic We twist the entire resolution by \mathcal{O}(n). The sequence remains exact. By the additivity of the Euler characteristic (Lemma 5.3): \chi(\mathcal{O}_X(n)) = \sum_{k=0}^m (-1)^k \chi(\mathcal{L}_k(n)). Note that the term \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^N}(n) corresponds to k=0 (empty sum of degrees is 0). Substituting the decomposition of \mathcal{L}_k: \chi(\mathcal{L}_k(n)) = \sum_{|I|=k} \chi\left( \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^N}(n - d_I) \right). We know for projective space that \chi(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^N}(d)) = \binom{d+N}{N}. Therefore: \chi(\mathcal{O}_X(n)) = \sum_{k=0}^m (-1)^k \sum_{1 \le i_1 < \dots < i_k \le m} \binom{n - (\operatorname{d}_{i_1} + \dots + d_{i_k}) + N}{N}. This proves the formula for the Hilbert Polynomial.

Step 5: Calculation of Arithmetic Genus Recall the definition of arithmetic genus for a scheme of dimension r: p_a(X) = (-1)^r (\chi(\mathcal{O}_X) - 1). For a complete intersection defined by m equations in \mathbb{P}^N, the dimension is r = N - m. We calculate \chi(\mathcal{O}_X) by setting n=0 in the Hilbert polynomial derived in Step 4: \chi(\mathcal{O}_X) = \sum_{k=0}^m (-1)^k \sum_{|I|=k} \binom{N - d_I}{N}. Substituting this into the genus formula: p_a(X) = (-1)^{N-m} \left[ \left( \sum_{k=0}^m (-1)^k \sum_{|I|=k} \binom{N - d_I}{N} \right) - 1 \right]. This completes the proof. ◻

We apply the above machinery to some famous surfaces in algebraic geometry.

Example 2.1 (The Segre Surface (Quadric in \mathbb{P}^3)). Consider the surface Q \subset \mathbb{P}^3 defined by the quadratic equation xy - zw = 0 (so N=3, d=2).

1. Invariants: Using the hypersurface formula: p_a(Q) = \binom{2-1}{3} = \binom{1}{3} = 0. A surface with arithmetic genus 0 is often rational.

2. Geometry (\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1): This surface is isomorphic to \mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1. Consider the Segre embedding \sigma: \mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1 \to \mathbb{P}^3 defined by: ([u_0:u_1], [v_0:v_1]) \longmapsto [u_0v_0 : u_0v_1 : u_1v_0 : u_1v_1]. Let the coordinates of \mathbb{P}^3 be Z_{00}, Z_{01}, Z_{10}, Z_{11}. Notice that Z_{00}Z_{11} - Z_{01}Z_{10} = (u_0v_0)(u_1v_1) - (u_0v_1)(u_1v_0) = 0. Thus the image lies in Q. One can construct an inverse map using coordinate ratios on the open sets where coordinates are non-zero (e.g., u_0/u_1 = Z_{00}/Z_{10} = Z_{01}/Z_{11}), proving isomorphism. This explains why Q contains two families of lines (rulings), corresponding to \{p\} \times \mathbb{P}^1 and \mathbb{P}^1 \times \{p\}.

Example 2.1 (Fermat Cubic Surface). Consider X \subset \mathbb{P}^3 defined by x^3 + y^3 + z^3 + w^3 = 0 (N=3, d=3).

1. Invariants: p_a(X) = \binom{3-1}{3} = \binom{2}{3} = 0. Thus, cubic surfaces are also rational surfaces (rationality is a deep result, not immediate from p_a=0, but p_a=0 is necessary).

2. The 27 Lines: A famous geometric fact is that every smooth cubic surface in \mathbb{P}^3 contains exactly 27 lines (over an algebraically closed field). For the Fermat cubic, these can be written down explicitly (e.g., lines like x = \lambda y, z = \mu w where \lambda^3 = -1, \mu^3 = -1).

Example 2.1 (K3 Surface). Consider a smooth hypersurface X \subset \mathbb{P}^3 of degree d=4 (e.g., x^4+y^4+z^4+w^4=0).

1. Invariants: p_a(X) = \binom{4-1}{3} = \binom{3}{3} = 1. Surfaces with trivial canonical bundle and \operatorname{H}^1(X, \mathcal{O}_X)=0 (which implies p_a=1) are called K3 surfaces (named after Kummer, Kähler, and Kodaira). They occupy a central place in the classification of surfaces, analogous to elliptic curves in dimension 1.

Example 2.1 (Veronese Surface in \mathbb{P}^5). The Veronese surface V \subset \mathbb{P}^5 is the image of the 2-Veronese embedding \nu_2: \mathbb{P}^2 \to \mathbb{P}^5.

1. Determinantal Definition: If we label coordinates of \mathbb{P}^5 as z_{ij} corresponding to basis monomials x_i x_j, the condition that a point comes from \mathbb{P}^2 is that the matrix of coordinates is rank 1: \operatorname{rank} \begin{pmatrix} z_{00} & z_{01} & z_{02} \\ z_{01} & z_{11} & z_{12} \\ z_{02} & z_{12} & z_{22} \end{pmatrix} \le 1. The vanishing of the 2 \times 2 minors of this symmetric matrix gives the quadratic equations defining V.

2. Not a Complete Intersection: These minors generate the ideal, but there are too many of them for the codimension. V has dimension 2 in \mathbb{P}^5 (codimension 3), but requires 6 minors to define it. It is a standard example of a variety that is not a complete intersection.

3. Invariants: Since V \cong \mathbb{P}^2, its invariants are those of the projective plane: p_a(V) = p_a(\mathbb{P}^2) = 0. Its degree in \mathbb{P}^5 is 2^2 = 4.
