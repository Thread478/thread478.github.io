Weil Conjectures on Curves

What are Weil Conjectures?

To understand the profound impact of the Weil Conjectures, we must trace their origins through the history of number theory and algebraic geometry, beginning with a striking analogy between numbers and polynomials.

Remark 3.1 (Historical Context: From Artin to Hasse). In 1924, Emil Artin observed a deep structural similarity between algebraic number fields (such as \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-D})) and function fields over finite fields (such as \mathbb{F}_p(x, \sqrt{f(x)})). Just as the classic Riemann \zeta-function studies the distribution of prime numbers over the ring of integers \mathbb{Z}, Artin defined a \zeta-function for the polynomial ring \mathbb{F}_q[x]: \zeta(s) = \sum_{A} \frac{1}{|A|^s} = \prod_{P} \left(1 - \frac{1}{|P|^s}\right)^{-1}. where A runs over all monic polynomials and P runs over all irreducible monic polynomials.

Following this analogy, Helmut Hasse (between 1933 and 1936) utilized the theory of complex multiplication to solve the Riemann Hypothesis for algebraic curves of genus g=1 (elliptic curves). By estimating the zeroes of the \zeta-function, Hasse proved the famous bound for the number of rational points N on an elliptic curve over \mathbb{F}_q: |N - (q + 1)| \le 2\sqrt{q}.

Between 1940 and 1941, while incarcerated in a military prison in Rouen, France, André Weil sought to generalize Hasse’s work from elliptic curves to smooth, projective, geometrically irreducible curves of arbitrary genus g.

Weil realized that the classical Italian school of algebraic geometry lacked the rigorous foundations necessary to handle spaces over fields of positive characteristic. Consequently, he spent the following years completely rebuilding algebraic geometry using the language of abstract algebra, culminating in his treatises Foundations of Algebraic Geometry (1946) and Sur les courbes algébriques (1948).

By 1949, after studying Gauss and Jacobi sums and counting points on diagonal hypersurfaces, Weil discovered that the patterns governing rational points on curves were not isolated phenomena. They were manifestations of a profound topological structure shaUniBlue by algebraic varieties of all dimensions. He formally proposed this in his landmark paper Numbers of solutions of equations in finite fields, giving birth to the classic Weil Conjectures.

To state the conjectures, we first define the generating function that encodes the number of solutions to a system of polynomial equations over extensions of a finite field.

Definition 3.1 (The Local Zeta Function). Let X be an n-dimensional smooth projective algebraic variety defined over a finite field \mathbb{F}_q. Let N_m denote the number of rational points of X over the degree-m extension field \mathbb{F}_{q^m} (i.e., N_m = |X(\mathbb{F}_{q^m})|). The local zeta function of X is defined as the formal power series: Z(X, t) = \exp\left( \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} \frac{N_m}{m} t^m \right).

The Weil Conjectures state that this power series, constructed purely from combinatorial counting, exhibits extraordinary analytic and topological properties.

Theorem 3.1 (The Weil Conjectures (1949)). Let X be an n-dimensional smooth projective variety over \mathbb{F}_q. The zeta function Z(X, t) satisfies the following four properties:

  1. Rationality: Z(X, t) is a rational function in t. That is, there exist polynomials P(t), Q(t) \in \mathbb{Q}[t] such that Z(X, t) = \frac{P(t)}{Q(t)}.

  2. Functional Equation: Let \chi be the Euler characteristic of X. The zeta function satisfies a highly symmetric functional equation linking the values at t and 1/(q^n t): Z\left(X, \frac{1}{q^n t}\right) = \pm q^{\frac{n\chi}{2}} t^\chi Z(X, t).

  3. Riemann Hypothesis: This is the deepest part of the conjecture. The rational function factors into an alternating product of polynomials: Z(X, t) = \frac{P_1(t) P_3(t) \cdots P_{2n-1}(t)}{P_0(t) P_2(t) \cdots P_{2n}(t)}. where each P_i(t) \in \mathbb{Z}[t]. Furthermore:

    • P_0(t) = 1 - t

    • P_{2n}(t) = 1 - q^n t

    • For 1 \le i \le 2n-1, every root \alpha of P_i(t) has absolute value |\alpha| = q^{-i/2} in the complex plane. (Substituting t = q^{-s} places the zeroes on the critical line \operatorname{Re}(s) = i/2).

  4. Betti Numbers: If X is obtained by the good reduction modulo p of a smooth projective variety \mathcal{X} defined over a number field K \subset \mathbb{C}, then the degree of P_i(t) is equal to the i-th topological Betti number of the complex manifold \mathcal{X}(\mathbb{C}): \deg P_i(t) = \beta_i(\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{C})).

To truly appreciate the power of these conjectures, let us specialize to the case of a curve, which is the core object of our current study. Let X be a smooth, projective, geometrically irreducible curve over \mathbb{F}_q.

Because a curve has dimension n = 1, the Betti number sequence for a corresponding compact Riemann surface of genus g is \beta_0 = 1, \beta_1 = 2g, and \beta_2 = 1. The Weil Conjectures drastically simplify this scenario.

Corollary 3.1 (Weil Conjectures for Curves). For a smooth projective curve X of genus g over \mathbb{F}_q, the polynomials P_i(t) vanish for i > 2. The zeta function takes the precise form: Z(X, t) = \frac{P_1(t)}{(1-t)(1-qt)}. where P_1(t) \in \mathbb{Z}[t] is a polynomial of degree 2g. The roots of P_1(t) can be written as \alpha_1^{-1}, \dots, \alpha_{2g}^{-1}, where each algebraic integer \alpha_j has complex absolute value |\alpha_j| = \sqrt{q}.

Remark 3.1 (Why this matters: The Point-Counting Formula). The true magic of the Riemann Hypothesis for curves reveals itself when we extract the point counts N_m. By taking the logarithmic derivative of Z(X, t) = \frac{\prod_{j=1}^{2g} (1 - \alpha_j t)}{(1-t)(1-qt)} and comparing coefficients with the original definition \exp(\sum \frac{N_m}{m} t^m), we obtain a breathtakingly exact formula for the number of points: N_m = q^m + 1 - \sum_{j=1}^{2g} \alpha_j^m. Because we know the absolute value of every \alpha_j is \sqrt{q}, we can apply the triangle inequality to instantly derive the generalized Hasse-Weil Bound for a curve of arbitrary genus g: |N_m - (q^m + 1)| \le 2g q^{m/2}. This tells us that the number of points on a curve over a finite field is approximately q^m + 1, with an error term tightly constrained by the geometry (the genus g) of the curve!

To rigorously define the point-counting numbers N_m = |X(\mathbb{F}_{q^m})| used in the Weil Conjectures, we must define what a “point” over a field extension means in the language of scheme theory, and how the Galois group acts upon these points.

Definition 3.2 (K-Rational Points). Let X be a scheme over a field k, and let K be a field extension of k. The set of K-rational points of X, denoted X(K), is defined as the set of k-morphisms from \operatorname{Spec}K to X: X(K) = \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}K, X). Every individual morphism P \colon \operatorname{Spec}K \to X is referUniBlue to as a K-point of X.

Definition 3.3 (Galois Action on Points). Let K/k be a Galois field extension with Galois group G = \operatorname{Gal}(K/k). For any automorphism \sigma \in G and any K-point P \in X(K), the action of \sigma on P is defined by the composition of morphisms: \sigma \cdot P := P \circ \operatorname{Spec}(\sigma^{-1}). where \operatorname{Spec}(\sigma^{-1}) \colon \operatorname{Spec}K \to \operatorname{Spec}K is the scheme morphism canonically induced by the field automorphism \sigma^{-1} \colon K \to K.

To compute with K-points, it is highly practical to relate these abstract morphisms back to the underlying topological space of the scheme X.

Proposition 3.1 (Characterization of Rational Points). Let X be a k-scheme and let k' be a field extension of k. The k'-rational points of X (the elements f \in \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}k', X)) are in canonical bijection with pairs (x, \iota), where x \in X is a point in the underlying topological space of X, and \iota \colon \kappa(x) \hookrightarrow k' is a k-linear field embedding of the residue field \kappa(x) into k'.

Proof. Let f \in \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}k', X) be a given morphism. The underlying topological space of \operatorname{Spec}k' is a single point, denoted *. The continuous map on topological spaces must send * to some specific point x \in X, so f(*) = x.

At the level of structure sheaves, the morphism f induces a homomorphism of local rings at the target point: f^\sharp _x \colon \mathcal{O}_{X,x} \to k'. Because f is a morphism of locally ringed spaces, f^\sharp _x is a local ring homomorphism. This strictly means the maximal ideal \mathfrak{m}_x of \mathcal{O}_{X,x} must be mapped into the unique maximal ideal of the field k', which is simply the zero ideal (0). Therefore, the kernel of f^\sharp _x contains \mathfrak{m}_x, and the homomorphism naturally factors through the quotient: \mathcal{O}_{X,x} \twoheadrightarrow \mathcal{O}_{X,x}/\mathfrak{m}_x = \kappa(x) \hookrightarrow k'. This canonical injection \kappa(x) \hookrightarrow k' provides our embedding \iota.

Conversely, suppose we are given a topological point x \in X and a k-linear embedding \iota \colon \kappa(x) \hookrightarrow k'. For any affine open set U \subseteq X containing x, we have a natural composite ring homomorphism: \mathcal{O}_X(U) \to \mathcal{O}_{X,x} \twoheadrightarrow \kappa(x) \xrightarrow{\iota} k'. Taking the spectrum of this ring homomorphism constructs a unique scheme morphism \operatorname{Spec}k' \to U \hookrightarrow X, which precisely yields the k'-point f. ◻

Corollary 3.1 (). When k' = k, the k-rational points X(k) correspond strictly bijectively to the closed points x \in X that satisfy the residue field condition \kappa(x) = k.

When we study the points of X over an algebraic closure, the Galois group groups these points into distinct orbits. The size of these orbits depends entirely on the degree of the residue fields of the closed points of X.

Proposition 3.1 (Fibers and Galois Orbits). Let \bar{k} be an algebraic closure of a perfect field k, and let \pi \colon \bar{X} = X \times_k \bar{k} \to X be the natural projection morphism. For any closed point x \in X, its geometric fiber \bar{X}_x = \pi^{-1}(x) satisfies: \#\bar{X}_x = [\kappa(x) : k]. Furthermore, the absolute Galois group G = \operatorname{Gal}(\bar{k}/k) acts transitively on the points of \bar{X}_x; that is, \bar{X}_x constitutes a single Galois orbit under the action of G.

Proof. By the definition of the scheme-theoretic fiber, the fiber over x is isomorphic to the base change of its residue field: \bar{X}_x \simeq \operatorname{Spec}(\kappa(x) \otimes_k \bar{k}). Because k is perfect, the finite field extension \kappa(x)/k is a separable extension. By the Primitive Element Theorem, \kappa(x) is generated by a single element, so \kappa(x) \simeq k[z]/(f(z)) for some irreducible separable polynomial f(z) \in k[z].

We can then compute the tensor product explicitly: \kappa(x) \otimes_k \bar{k} \simeq \left( k[z]/(f(z)) \right) \otimes_k \bar{k} \simeq \bar{k}[z]/(f(z)). Since \bar{k} is an algebraically closed field, the polynomial f(z) splits completely into distinct linear factors over \bar{k}, yielding roots \alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_n \in \bar{k}, where n = \deg(f) = [\kappa(x) : k].

Applying the Chinese Remainder Theorem to the pairwise coprime ideals (z - \alpha_i), the ring decomposes into a direct product: \bar{k}[z]/(f(z)) \simeq \prod_{i=1}^n \bar{k}[z]/(z - \alpha_i) \simeq \prod_{i=1}^n \bar{k}. Taking the spectrum transforms this direct product of rings into a disjoint union of schemes: \bar{X}_x \simeq \operatorname{Spec}\left( \prod_{i=1}^n \bar{k} \right) \simeq \coprod_{i=1}^n \operatorname{Spec}\bar{k}. This shows that the fiber consists of exactly n distinct points, proving that \#\bar{X}_x = \deg(f) = [\kappa(x) : k].

Finally, we consider the Galois action. Because f(z) is an irreducible polynomial over k, classical Galois theory states that the absolute Galois group G = \operatorname{Gal}(\bar{k}/k) acts transitively on the set of its roots \{\alpha_i\}. Since the geometric points of the fiber \bar{X}_x correspond exactly to these roots, the Galois group G acts transitively on the n points of the fiber. Therefore, the geometric points lying over a single closed point x \in X form exactly one complete Galois orbit. ◻

The proof of the First Part

To translate the geometric problem of point-counting into algebraic machinery, we must fix our base field and study the divisors on our curve.

Let k = \mathbb{F}_q be a finite field with q elements. Throughout this section, let X be a smooth, projective, and geometrically irreducible curve over k of genus g. In the language of schemes, we formulate the specific objects that construct the Weil Conjectures as follows:

  1. For any closed point x \in |X| (where |X| denotes the underlying topological space of the scheme X), the degree of x is defined as the degree of its residue field over the base field: \deg(x) = [\kappa(x) : k].

  2. An effective Weil divisor on X is a formal sum D = \sum_{x \in |X|} n_x x, where the coefficients n_x are non-negative integers (n_x \ge 0) and only finitely many n_x are strictly positive. The degree of the divisor extends the degree of points linearly: \deg(D) = \sum_{x \in |X|} n_x \deg(x). The set of all effective divisors is denoted \operatorname{Div}^+(X).

  3. Let k_s = \mathbb{F}_{q^s} be the unique field extension of k of degree s. The set of k_s-rational points on X is denoted X(k_s) = \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}k_s, X).

To construct the Zeta function combinatorially, we define three sequences of non-negative integers indexed by s \ge 1 (with A_0 = 1 by convention for the zero divisor): \begin{align*} A_s &= \#\{D \in \operatorname{Div}^+(X) \mid \deg(D) = s\} && \text{(Number of effective divisors of degree } s \text{)} \\ M_s &= \#\{x \in |X| \mid \deg(x) = s\} && \text{(Number of closed points of degree } s \text{)} \\ N_s &= \#X(k_s) && \text{(Number of } k_s \text{-rational points)} \end{align*}

Theorem 3.1 (Equivalent Expressions of the Zeta Function). For a curve X over a finite field k = \mathbb{F}_q, the following three formal power series in the formal variable t are absolutely identical:

  1. \displaystyle \sum_{s=0}^\infty A_s t^s

  2. \displaystyle \prod_{s=1}^\infty (1 - t^s)^{-M_s}

  3. \displaystyle \exp\left( \sum_{s=1}^\infty \frac{N_s}{s} t^s \right)

We formally define this identical series as the local Zeta function of the curve X, denoted Z(X, t).

Proof. Proof that = : The set of effective divisors \operatorname{Div}^+(X) forms a free commutative monoid generated strictly by the closed points x \in |X|. Because of this unique factorization property (every effective divisor is a unique finite sum of closed points), we can rewrite the sum over all effective divisors as an Euler product over all closed points: \sum_{s=0}^\infty A_s t^s = \sum_{D \in \operatorname{Div}^+(X)} t^{\deg(D)} = \prod_{x \in |X|} \left( \sum_{n=0}^\infty t^{n \deg(x)} \right). By evaluating the inner geometric series for each point x, this becomes: \prod_{x \in |X|} (1 - t^{\deg(x)})^{-1}. We can now group the terms in this infinite product by the degree of the closed points. Let s = \deg(x). By definition, there are exactly M_s closed points of degree s. Grouping these identical factors together yields: \prod_{s=1}^\infty (1 - t^s)^{-M_s}. This proves that and are equivalent.

Proof that = : By Proposition , specifying a k_s-rational point is perfectly equivalent to choosing a closed point x \in |X| alongside a k-linear field embedding of its residue field into the target field: \kappa(x) \hookrightarrow k_s. Such an embedding exists if and only if the degree d = \deg(x) = [\kappa(x):k] divides the extension degree s. Furthermore, standard Galois theory of finite fields dictates that if one such embedding exists, there are exactly d distinct embeddings, corresponding to the conjugates under the action of \operatorname{Gal}(\kappa(x)/k). Therefore, if we classify the rational points by the degree d of their underlying closed point, we obtain the fundamental counting formula: N_s = \sum_{d \mid s} d M_d. Now, we apply the formal logarithm to the infinite product in . Using the Taylor expansion \log(1-X) = -\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{X^k}{k}, we obtain: \begin{align*} \log \left( \prod_{s=1}^\infty (1 - t^s)^{-M_s} \right) &= -\sum_{d=1}^\infty M_d \log(1 - t^d) \\ &= \sum_{d=1}^\infty M_d \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{t^{dk}}{k} \end{align*} To re-index this double sum, let s = dk. Then k = s/d. For a fixed s \ge 1, the inner sum will iterate over all divisors d of s. Substituting k gives: \begin{align*} \sum_{d=1}^\infty \sum_{k=1}^\infty M_d \frac{t^{dk}}{k} &= \sum_{s=1}^\infty \left( \sum_{d \mid s} M_d \frac{d}{s} \right) t^s \\ &= \sum_{s=1}^\infty \frac{1}{s} \left( \sum_{d \mid s} d M_d \right) t^s \\ &= \sum_{s=1}^\infty \frac{N_s}{s} t^s \end{align*} Taking the formal exponential \exp of both sides immediately shows that the product equals the exponential generating function . This concludes the proof of the theorem. ◻

To prove the rationality of Z(X, t), we will eventually group the effective divisors D not just by their degree, but by their linear equivalence classes. We must therefore count how many effective divisors exist in a given linear equivalence class.

Theorem 3.1 (Size of a Complete Linear System). Let D be a divisor on the curve X, and let \ell(D) = \dim_k \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X(D)). Assuming \ell(D) > 0, the number of effective divisors that are linearly equivalent to D (i.e., the cardinality of the complete linear system |D|) is exactly: \frac{q^{\ell(D)} - 1}{q - 1}.

Proof. Because X is a geometrically irreducible projective scheme over k, the ring of global regular functions is simply the base field itself: \Gamma(X, \mathcal{O}_X) = k. Consequently, the group of globally invertible regular functions is the multiplicative group of the field: \Gamma(X, \mathcal{O}_X^\times) = k^\times.

There is a canonical bijection between the effective divisors in the complete linear system |D| and the non-zero global sections of the line bundle \mathcal{O}_X(D) modulo scaling by global invertible functions. That is: |D| \longleftrightarrow \left( \Gamma(X, \mathcal{O}_X(D)) \setminus \{0\} \right) / k^\times. The space of global sections \Gamma(X, \mathcal{O}_X(D)) is a k-vector space of dimension \ell(D). Since the base field has q elements, this vector space contains exactly q^{\ell(D)} total elements. Removing the zero section leaves q^{\ell(D)} - 1 non-zero sections.

The group k^\times acts on these non-zero sections by scalar multiplication. Because X is an integral scheme, the space of sections is torsion-free, meaning the multiplication action by any element of k^\times (other than 1) has no fixed points on the non-zero sections. Thus, the action is free.

Since the group k^\times has order q - 1, each orbit under this action has exactly q - 1 elements. The number of orbits, which corresponds exactly to the number of effective divisors in |D|, is the total number of non-zero sections divided by the size of the orbits: \#|D| = \frac{q^{\ell(D)} - 1}{q - 1}. Geometrically, this demonstrates that over a finite field, the complete linear system |D| is simply the set of rational points of the projective space \mathbb{P}^{\ell(D)-1}(\mathbb{F}_q). ◻

Lemma 3.1 (Finiteness of Effective Divisors of Bounded Degree). For any integer d \ge 0, the set of effective divisors of degree d on X is finite. That is, the set \operatorname{Div}_+^d(X) := \{E \in \operatorname{Div}(X) \mid E \ge 0, \deg(E) = d\}. is a finite set.

Proof. An effective divisor can be uniquely represented as a formal sum E = \sum n_x x, where x \in |X| are closed points, n_x \ge 0, and the degree satisfies \sum n_x \deg(x) = d. Because the degree of any closed point is positive (\deg(x) \ge 1), the points x actively participating in this sum (where n_x > 0) must individually satisfy \deg(x) \le d.

Because X is a projective scheme over k, it admits a closed immersion into some projective space \mathbb{P}^n_k. For any closed point x \in |X| with \deg(x) = r \le d, its residue field \kappa(x) is isomorphic to the finite field \mathbb{F}_{q^r}. This structure gives rise to a naturally defined morphism \operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{F}_{q^r} \to X \hookrightarrow \mathbb{P}^n_k, which corresponds to a specific rational point in \mathbb{P}^n(\mathbb{F}_{q^r}).

Since a projective space over a finite field only has a finite number of rational points, the set \mathbb{P}^n(\mathbb{F}_{q^r}) is finite. Consequently, its closed subset X(\mathbb{F}_{q^r}) is also finite. This implies that the total number of closed points on X whose degree does not exceed d is strictly finite. Since there are only finitely many closed points available to form the divisor E, and their respective coefficients n_x are strictly bounded by the condition \sum n_x \deg(x) = d, there are only finitely many valid linear combinations. Thus, \operatorname{Div}_+^d(X) is a finite set. ◻

Theorem 3.1 (Finiteness of \operatorname{Pic}^d(X) and the Class Number). For any integer d, the set of divisor classes of degree d, denoted \operatorname{Pic}^d(X), is a finite set. In particular, the degree-zero Picard group \operatorname{Pic}^0(X) is a finite Abelian group. Its cardinality is called the class number of the curve X, denoted by h.

Proof. We first prove that \operatorname{Pic}^d(X) is finite for any d \ge 2g. Take an arbitrary divisor class [D] \in \operatorname{Pic}^d(X) with d \ge 2g. By the Riemann-Roch theorem, we must consider the canonical divisor K, which has degree \deg(K) = 2g - 2. We evaluate the degree of K - D: \deg(K - D) = 2g - 2 - d < 0. Because a divisor of negative degree cannot be linearly equivalent to an effective divisor, the space of global sections for K-D is trivial, meaning \ell(K - D) = 0.

Therefore, the Riemann-Roch formula simplifies exactly to: \ell(D) = \deg(D) + 1 - g = d + 1 - g. Since d \ge 2g, it immediately follows that \ell(D) \ge g + 1 \ge 1. This proves that \ell(D) > 0, meaning the complete linear system |D| is non-empty. This establishes a crucial fact: when d \ge 2g, every divisor class in \operatorname{Pic}^d(X) contains at least one effective divisor.

In other words, the natural quotient map mapping effective divisors to their linear equivalence class: \operatorname{Div}_+^d(X) \twoheadrightarrow \operatorname{Pic}^d(X). is surjective. By Lemma , the domain \operatorname{Div}_+^d(X) is a finite set. The surjective image of a finite set is necessarily finite, so \operatorname{Pic}^d(X) must be finite for d \ge 2g.

Now consider an arbitrary degree r. Because the degrees of closed points on X are strictly positive, we can simply pick a sufficiently large number of closed points to construct an effective divisor D_h such that r + \deg(D_h) \ge 2g. Translation by D_h induces a group isomorphism: \operatorname{Pic}^r(X) \xrightarrow{\sim} \operatorname{Pic}^{r + \deg(D_h)}(X), \quad [D] \mapsto [D + D_h]. Because the target set \operatorname{Pic}^{r + \deg(D_h)}(X) has a degree greater than or equal to 2g, we know it is finite. The bijection implies the source set \operatorname{Pic}^r(X) is also finite. Setting r=0 demonstrates that \operatorname{Pic}^0(X) is a finite group, allowing us to formally define the class number h = \#\operatorname{Pic}^0(X). ◻

We continue to fix X as a smooth, projective, geometrically irreducible curve over the finite field k = \mathbb{F}_q, with genus g and class number h. Let A_s denote the number of effective divisors of degree s.

Lemma 3.1 (The Greatest Common Divisor of Divisor Degrees). Let m be the greatest common divisor of the degrees of all closed points on X (equivalently, m is the smallest positive integer such that there exists a divisor of degree m on X). Then m = 1.

Proof. We begin by observing the explicit distribution of the sequence A_s:

  1. If m \nmid s, there are strictly no divisors of degree s on X (as all degrees are multiples of m). Thus, A_s = 0.

  2. If m \mid s and s > 2g - 2, the set \operatorname{Pic}^s(X) is a principal homogeneous space over \operatorname{Pic}^0(X), meaning it contains exactly h distinct divisor classes. For each class [D] \in \operatorname{Pic}^s(X), since s > 2g - 2, the Riemann-Roch theorem dictates \ell(D) = s - g + 1. By Theorem , each of the h classes contains exactly \frac{q^{s-g+1}-1}{q-1} effective divisors. Summing these up yields: A_s = h \frac{q^{s-g+1} - 1}{q - 1}.

Using these two rules, we can expand the formal series of the Zeta function and re-index it using the variable t^m: \begin{align*} Z(X, t) &= \sum_{s=0}^\infty A_s t^s = \sum_{r=0}^\infty A_{rm} t^{rm} \\ &= \sum_{rm \le 2g-2} A_{rm} t^{rm} + \frac{h}{q-1} \sum_{rm > 2g-2} \left( q^{rm-g+1} - 1 \right) t^{rm} \end{align*} The first summation is simply a polynomial in t^m. The second summation is the difference of two geometric series, which can be evaluated explicitly: Z(X, t) = \left( \text{Polynomial in } t^m \right) + \frac{h}{q-1} \left( \frac{q^{1-g} (qt)^{r_0 m}}{1 - q^m t^m} - \frac{t^{r_0 m}}{1 - t^m} \right). where r_0 is the smallest integer such that r_0 m > 2g - 2.

This explicit formula shows that Z(X, t) is a rational function, a quotient of two polynomials in the variable t^m. If we view Z(X, t) as a meromorphic function on the complex plane \mathbb{C}, the presence of the term \frac{1}{1 - t^m} (whose numerator coefficient is non-zero) forces Z(X, t) to have a simple (first-order) pole at t = 1.

Now, let M_j be the number of closed points of degree j on X. Because M_j = 0 whenever m \nmid j, we can rewrite the Euler product formula of the Zeta function strictly in terms of multiples of m: Z(X, t) = \prod_{s=1}^\infty (1 - t^{ms})^{-M_{ms}}. Define an auxiliary power series f(t) = \prod_{s=1}^\infty (1 - t^s)^{-M_{ms}}, so that the relationship is exactly Z(X, t) = f(t^m). Since we established that Z(X, t) has a simple pole at t = 1, it immediately follows that f(t) itself must have a simple pole at t = 1.

We now employ a base change argument. Let k_m = \mathbb{F}_{q^m}, and consider the base-changed curve X_{k_m} = X \times_k k_m. Let its Zeta function be denoted Z(X_{k_m}, t) = \prod_{s=1}^\infty (1 - t^s)^{-M_s^*}. For any closed point x on X, its degree [\kappa(x) : k] must be a multiple of m. This implies its residue field \kappa(x) contains the subfield k_m. Thus, the field extension degrees satisfy the tower law: [\kappa(x) : k] = m \cdot [\kappa(x) : k_m]. Geometrically, this implies that a single closed point on X of degree ms, upon base change to k_m, splits completely into exactly m distinct closed points, each of degree s. This gives us a direct counting correspondence: M_s^* = m \cdot M_{ms}.

Substitute this correspondence back into the Euler product for X_{k_m}: Z(X_{k_m}, t) = \prod_{s=1}^\infty (1 - t^s)^{-m M_{ms}} = \left( \prod_{s=1}^\infty (1 - t^s)^{-M_{ms}} \right)^m = f(t)^m.

On the other hand, X_{k_m} is itself a smooth, geometrically irreducible curve defined over the finite field k_m. The greatest common divisor of its closed point degrees is naturally 1 (as we factoUniBlue out m). Applying the exact same analytic derivation we used for X to the curve X_{k_m}, we conclude that Z(X_{k_m}, t) as a whole must have a simple pole at t = 1.

Theorem 3.1 (Rationality of the Zeta Function). Let X be a smooth, projective, geometrically irreducible curve of genus g over a finite field \mathbb{F}_q. The local Zeta function of X is a rational function of the specific form: Z(X, t) = \frac{P(t)}{(1 - t)(1 - qt)}. where P(t) \in \mathbb{Z}[t] is a polynomial with integer coefficients satisfying P(0) = 1 and \deg P(t) = 2g. Furthermore, the leading term of P(t) is exactly q^g t^{2g}. Consequently, P(t) factors over the complex numbers as \prod_{i=1}^{2g} (1 - \alpha_i t), where the reciprocal roots \alpha_i are algebraic integers.

Proof. We first address the special case where g = 0. For a curve of genus zero, the Riemann-Roch theorem simplifies significantly. For any divisor D of degree s \ge 0, we have: \ell(D) = s + 1 - g = s + 1. Therefore, by Theorem , every divisor class of degree s contains exactly \frac{q^{s+1} - 1}{q - 1} effective divisors. Since m=1 (Lemma ), there are exactly h divisor classes of degree s. Summing over all classes yields the total number of effective divisors of degree s: A_s = h \left( \frac{q^{s+1} - 1}{q - 1} \right), \quad \forall s \ge 0. Taking s = 0, the only effective divisor of degree zero is the zero divisor itself, so A_0 = 1. This immediately forces the class number h = A_0 = 1. The Zeta function then becomes a direct evaluation of geometric series: Z(X, t) = \sum_{s=0}^\infty \left( \frac{q^{s+1} - 1}{q - 1} \right) t^s = \frac{1}{q-1} \left( q \sum_{s=0}^\infty (qt)^s - \sum_{s=0}^\infty t^s \right). Z(X, t) = \frac{1}{q-1} \left( \frac{q}{1 - qt} - \frac{1}{1 - t} \right) = \frac{1}{(1 - t)(1 - qt)}. This perfectly matches the theorem’s claim with P(t) = 1, which satisfies \deg P(t) = 2(0) = 0 and P(0) = 1.

For the general case where g \ge 1, we already established that m=1, meaning \operatorname{Pic}^s(X) always contains exactly h divisor classes for any integer s. By the Riemann-Roch theorem, when s > 2g - 2, any divisor D of degree s satisfies \ell(K - D) = 0, which dictates that \ell(D) = s - g + 1. Thus, every class of degree s contains identically \frac{q^{s-g+1} - 1}{q - 1} effective divisors. Summing over the h classes gives the exact count for s > 2g - 2: A_s = h \frac{q^{s-g+1} - 1}{q - 1}. To leverage this stable asymptotic behavior, we define an “expected” number of effective divisors for all s \ge 0: A_s^* = \frac{h}{q-1} (q^{s-g+1} - 1). We can then split the full Zeta function series into a finite polynomial part (the deviations from the expected count) and a pUniBlueictable infinite series part: Z(X, t) = \sum_{s=0}^{2g-2} (A_s - A_s^*) t^s + \sum_{s=0}^\infty A_s^* t^s. We explicitly compute the infinite sum of geometric series on the right: \begin{align*} \sum_{s=0}^\infty A_s^* t^s &= \frac{h}{q-1} \sum_{s=0}^\infty \left( q^{1-g}(qt)^s - t^s \right) \\ &= \frac{h}{q-1} \left( \frac{q^{1-g}}{1 - qt} - \frac{1}{1 - t} \right) \end{align*} The left-hand sum is a finite polynomial, which we will define as Q(t) = \sum_{s=0}^{2g-2} (A_s - A_s^*) t^s. Substituting this back yields: Z(X, t) = Q(t) + \frac{h}{q-1} \left( \frac{q^{1-g}}{1 - qt} - \frac{1}{1 - t} \right). By finding a common denominator, we obtain: Z(X, t) = \frac{Q(t)(1 - t)(1 - qt) + \frac{h}{q-1} \left( q^{1-g}(1-t) - (1-qt) \right)}{(1 - t)(1 - qt)}. This proves that Z(X, t) is a rational function with the denominator (1-t)(1-qt). Let its numerator be denoted P(t). Because the original power series definition dictates Z(X, 0) = A_0 = 1, evaluating the rational function at t=0 forces P(0) = 1.

It remains to compute the highest degree term of P(t). Since the correction term in the numerator has degree at most 1, the leading term of P(t) is entirely determined by the leading term of Q(t)(1-t)(1-qt). We must therefore find the highest degree term of Q(t). We look at the coefficient at s = 2g - 2.

In \operatorname{Pic}^{2g-2}(X), there are exactly h divisor classes. Exactly one of these is the canonical divisor class [K]. For [K], the dimension of global sections is \ell(K) = g. Thus, the canonical class contains \frac{q^g - 1}{q - 1} effective divisors.

For the remaining h - 1 divisor classes [D] \neq [K] of degree 2g-2, the fact that D \not\sim K forces \ell(K - D) = 0. Using Riemann-Roch, \ell(D) = (2g - 2) + 1 - g = g - 1. Each of these h-1 classes contains \frac{q^{g-1} - 1}{q - 1} effective divisors.

Summing these contributions gives the true exact count A_{2g-2}: \begin{align*} A_{2g-2} &= \frac{q^g - 1}{q - 1} + (h - 1)\frac{q^{g-1} - 1}{q - 1} \\ &= h \frac{q^{g-1} - 1}{q - 1} + \frac{q^g - q^{g-1}}{q - 1} \\ &= A_{2g-2}^* + q^{g-1} \end{align*} This reveals that the deviation exactly at s = 2g-2 is A_{2g-2} - A_{2g-2}^* = q^{g-1} \neq 0. Because all terms s > 2g-2 have identically zero deviation, the polynomial Q(t) has exactly degree 2g-2, and its leading term is precisely q^{g-1} t^{2g-2}.

We now substitute this back into the structural expression for P(t): P(t) = Q(t)(1 - t)(1 - qt) + (\text{lower degree terms}). Expanding the leading terms: \left( q^{g-1} t^{2g-2} \right) \cdot (-t) \cdot (-qt) = q^g t^{2g}. This decisively proves that \deg P(t) = 2g, and its leading term is q^g t^{2g}.

Finally, because P(t) \in \mathbb{Z}[t] and its constant term is P(0) = 1, the reciprocal polynomial t^{2g} P(1/t) is a monic polynomial with integer coefficients. The roots of this monic polynomial are precisely \alpha_i. Because they are roots of a monic polynomial in \mathbb{Z}[t], the numbers \alpha_i are by definition algebraic integers. ◻

To establish the functional equation, it is highly convenient to rewrite our rational Zeta function using a slightly different algebraic grouping. Recall that the number of effective divisors in any divisor class C is exactly \frac{q^{\ell(C)} - 1}{q - 1}. If we define a new summation notation L_d = \sum_{C \in \operatorname{Pic}^d(X)} q^{\ell(C)}, then the total number of effective divisors of degree d is A_d = \frac{L_d - h}{q - 1}. Substituting this into our power series naturally rearranges the Zeta function into the following compact form: Z(X, t) = \frac{1}{q-1} \left( \sum_{d=0}^{2g-2} L_d t^d + \frac{h q^g t^{2g-1}}{1 - qt} - \frac{h}{1 - t} \right).

Proposition 3.1 (Functional Equation of the Zeta Function). Let X be a smooth, projective, geometrically irreducible curve of genus g over a finite field \mathbb{F}_q. Its local Zeta function satisfies the following symmetric functional equation: Z\left(X, \frac{1}{qt}\right) = q^{1-g} t^{2-2g} Z(X, t).

Proof. We first establish a symmetry property for the sequence L_d. In the Picard group \operatorname{Pic}(X), the map C \mapsto K - C (where K is the canonical divisor) provides a canonical bijection between \operatorname{Pic}^d(X) and \operatorname{Pic}^{2g-2-d}(X).

Using this bijection, we can rewrite the Riemann-Roch formula \ell(C) - \ell(K - C) = d + 1 - g as \ell(K - C) = \ell(C) - d - 1 + g. This allows us to directly relate L_{2g-2-d} to L_d: \begin{align*} L_{2g-2-d} &= \sum_{D \in \operatorname{Pic}^{2g-2-d}(X)} q^{\ell(D)} \\ &= \sum_{C \in \operatorname{Pic}^d(X)} q^{\ell(K-C)} \\ &= \sum_{C \in \operatorname{Pic}^d(X)} q^{\ell(C) - d - 1 + g} \\ &= q^{g-1-d} \sum_{C \in \operatorname{Pic}^d(X)} q^{\ell(C)} \\ &= q^{g-1-d} L_d \end{align*}

Now, we substitute \frac{1}{qt} into the rearranged formula for Z(X, t): Z\left(X, \frac{1}{qt}\right) = \frac{1}{q-1} \left( \sum_{d=0}^{2g-2} L_d (qt)^{-d} + \frac{h q^g (qt)^{1-2g}}{1 - q(qt)^{-1}} - \frac{h}{1 - (qt)^{-1}} \right).

We will factor out q^{1-g}t^{2-2g} from each of the three terms inside the parenthesis to show they perfectly match the terms of the original Z(X, t).

First Term (The Finite Sum): We apply the substitution k = 2g - 2 - d. As d ranges from 0 to 2g-2, k also ranges from 2g-2 down to 0. Utilizing the symmetry L_{2g-2-k} = q^{g-1-k} L_k, we compute: \begin{align*} \sum_{d=0}^{2g-2} L_d (qt)^{-d} &= \sum_{k=0}^{2g-2} L_{2g-2-k} q^{-(2g-2-k)} t^{-(2g-2-k)} \\ &= \sum_{k=0}^{2g-2} (q^{g-1-k} L_k) q^{-2g+2+k} t^{-2g+2+k} \\ &= \sum_{k=0}^{2g-2} q^{1-g} L_k t^{2-2g+k} \\ &= q^{1-g} t^{2-2g} \sum_{k=0}^{2g-2} L_k t^k \end{align*} This exactly matches q^{1-g}t^{2-2g} times the first term of Z(X, t).

Second Term: We simplify the algebraic fraction: \begin{align*} \frac{h q^g (qt)^{1-2g}}{1 - q(qt)^{-1}} &= \frac{h q^g q^{1-2g} t^{1-2g}}{1 - t^{-1}} \\ &= \frac{h q^{1-g} t^{1-2g}}{(t - 1)/t} \\ &= \frac{h q^{1-g} t^{2-2g}}{t - 1} \\ &= -q^{1-g} t^{2-2g} \left( \frac{h}{1 - t} \right) \end{align*} This exactly matches q^{1-g}t^{2-2g} times the third term of Z(X, t).

Third Term: We simplify the final algebraic fraction: \begin{align*} - \frac{h}{1 - (qt)^{-1}} &= - \frac{h}{(qt - 1)/qt} \\ &= - \frac{h qt}{qt - 1} \\ &= \frac{h qt}{1 - qt} \\ &= q^{1-g} t^{2-2g} \left( \frac{h q^g t^{2g-1}}{1 - qt} \right) \end{align*} This exactly matches q^{1-g}t^{2-2g} times the second term of Z(X, t).

In conclusion, all three transformed terms precisely match the original terms of Z(X, t) multiplied by the scaling factor. We therefore obtain: Z\left(X, \frac{1}{qt}\right) = q^{1-g} t^{2-2g} Z(X, t). The functional equation is proved. ◻

To estimate the lower bounds of sums of powers of complex numbers, which will be crucial for connecting the point counts to the roots of the Zeta function, we first require a foundational analytic lemma based on Dirichlet’s simultaneous approximation theorem.

Lemma 3.1 (Integer Multiple Approximation of Phases). Let \lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_r be complex numbers with absolute value exactly equal to 1. For any given real number \epsilon > 0, there exist infinitely many positive integers m such that for all j = 1, \dots, r, we have: \operatorname{Re}(\lambda_j^m) > 1 - \epsilon.

Proof. We can write the complex numbers in polar form as \lambda_j = e^{2\pi i \theta_j}, where \theta_j \in \mathbb{R}. According to Dirichlet’s simultaneous approximation theorem, the sequence of fractional parts \left( \{m\theta_1\}, \dots, \{m\theta_r\} \right)_{m=1}^\infty must have an accumulation point in the r-dimensional torus (\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^r.

Because this is a translation on a compact group, the origin (0, \dots, 0) itself is an accumulation point. Therefore, for any arbitrarily small \delta > 0, there exist infinitely many positive integers m such that for all j, the phase angle difference between \lambda_j^m and 1 on the unit circle is strictly less than 2\pi\delta.

If we choose \delta to be sufficiently small such that \cos(2\pi\delta) > 1 - \epsilon, then by the geometry of the complex plane, we immediately obtain: \operatorname{Re}(\lambda_j^m) = \cos(2\pi m \theta_j) > 1 - \epsilon. Since there are infinitely many such m for any \delta, the lemma is proved. ◻

Lemma 3.1 (Non-Degenerate Lower Bound for Power Sums). Let \alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_t be a finite sequence of non-zero complex numbers. Then there exist infinitely many positive integers m such that: \left| \sum_{j=1}^t \alpha_j^m \right| \ge \max_{1 \le j \le t} |\alpha_j|^m.

Proof. Without loss of generality, we may assume the complex numbers are sorted in descending order of their absolute values: |\alpha_1| = \dots = |\alpha_r| > |\alpha_{r+1}| \ge \dots \ge |\alpha_t|. Let this maximum absolute value be denoted by A = |\alpha_1|. We factor out the dominant magnitude A^m from the sum: \sum_{j=1}^t \alpha_j^m = A^m \left( \sum_{j=1}^r \left(\frac{\alpha_j}{A}\right)^m + \sum_{j=r+1}^t \left(\frac{\alpha_j}{A}\right)^m \right). Define normalized complex numbers \lambda_j = \alpha_j / A. By our ordering, for 1 \le j \le r, we have |\lambda_j| = 1; and for j > r, we have |\lambda_j| < 1.

Now, consider the real part of the normalized sum: \operatorname{Re}\left( \frac{1}{A^m} \sum_{j=1}^t \alpha_j^m \right) = \sum_{j=1}^r \operatorname{Re}(\lambda_j^m) + \sum_{j=r+1}^t \operatorname{Re}(\lambda_j^m). By Lemma , for any \epsilon > 0 (choose \epsilon < 1/r), there exist infinitely many positive integers m such that the first sum strictly satisfies: \sum_{j=1}^r \operatorname{Re}(\lambda_j^m) > r(1 - \epsilon). For the second term, because |\lambda_j| < 1 for all j > r, the geometric progression forces \sum_{j=r+1}^t \operatorname{Re}(\lambda_j^m) \to 0 as m \to \infty.

Therefore, if we restrict our attention to the infinitely many chosen integers m, and take m sufficiently large, we must have: \operatorname{Re}\left( \frac{1}{A^m} \sum_{j=1}^t \alpha_j^m \right) \ge r(1 - \epsilon) - o(1) \ge 1. Because the real part of this complex number is greater than or equal to 1, its absolute value must naturally be greater than or equal to 1 as well. This yields: \frac{1}{A^m} \left| \sum_{j=1}^t \alpha_j^m \right| \ge 1 \implies \left| \sum_{j=1}^t \alpha_j^m \right| \ge A^m = |\alpha_1|^m. This holds for infinitely many m, concluding the proof. ◻

Proposition 3.1 (Equivalence of the Riemann Hypothesis and Hasse-Weil Bound). Let X be a smooth projective curve of genus g over a finite field \mathbb{F}_q. We have shown its Zeta function takes the form: Z(X, t) = \frac{\prod_{i=1}^{2g} (1 - \alpha_i t)}{(1 - t)(1 - qt)}. Let N_s denote the number of \mathbb{F}_{q^s}-rational points on X. Then the following two assertions are strictly equivalent:

Consequently, the deviation of the point count from the expected q^s + 1 is governed perfectly by the roots: |N_s - q^s - 1| = \left| \sum_{i=1}^{2g} \alpha_i^s \right|.

Because this inequality is satisfied for an infinite sequence of s tending to infinity, we can pass to the limit as s \to \infty. Lemma 3.1 (Local Parameters of the Tensor Product). Let p = (x, y) be a closed point on S. Let t_x \in \mathfrak{m}_x and t_y \in \mathfrak{m}_y be the local parameters of C at the points x and y, respectively. Then in the local ring \mathcal{O}_{S, p} of the product space, the maximal ideal \mathfrak{m}_p is generated precisely by (t_x, t_y).

Proof. Without loss of generality, assume that U = \operatorname{Spec}A and V = \operatorname{Spec}B are affine open neighborhoods of the closed points x and y on the curve C, respectively. According to the correspondence between morphisms and prime ideals, the fact that the point p maps to the point x is algebraically equivalent to saying that the preimage of the maximal ideal \mathfrak{m}_p under the homomorphism \varphi_A is \mathfrak{m}_A, i.e., \varphi_A^{-1}(\mathfrak{m}_p) = \mathfrak{m}_A. From the basic properties of the preimage of an ideal, this necessarily implies that \varphi_A(\mathfrak{m}_A) \subseteq \mathfrak{m}_p, which means: \mathfrak{m}_A \otimes 1 \subseteq \mathfrak{m}_p. Similarly, from p_2(p) = y, we obtain \varphi_B^{-1}(\mathfrak{m}_p) = \mathfrak{m}_B, which in turn implies: 1 \otimes \mathfrak{m}_B \subseteq \mathfrak{m}_p.

Since \mathfrak{m}_p is an ideal in A \otimes_k B and is closed under addition, it must contain the ideal generated jointly by the two sets above. We denote this ideal as I: I = \mathfrak{m}_A \otimes_k B + A \otimes_k \mathfrak{m}_B \subseteq \mathfrak{m}_p.

Next, we verify the maximality of the ideal I itself. Examining the quotient ring (A \otimes_k B) / I and utilizing the isomorphism properties of the tensor product, we have: \frac{A \otimes_k B}{I} = \frac{A \otimes_k B}{\mathfrak{m}_A \otimes_k B + A \otimes_k \mathfrak{m}_B} \cong \left( \frac{A}{\mathfrak{m}_A} \right) \otimes_k \left( \frac{B}{\mathfrak{m}_B} \right). Finally, it is known that C is a one-dimensional smooth curve, so its local rings \mathcal{O}_{C,x} and \mathcal{O}_{C,y} at x and y are both discrete valuation rings, whose maximal ideals are generated by the corresponding local parameters, denoted as \mathfrak{m}_x = (t_x) and \mathfrak{m}_y = (t_y). Therefore, the maximal ideal of the local ring \mathcal{O}_{S,p} is generated precisely by (t_x, t_y). ◻

Lemma 3.1 (Castelnuovo Inequality). Let D be any divisor on the surface S = C \times_k C. Define its intersection numbers with the horizontal and vertical fibers as: d_1(D) = (D \cdot l), \quad d_2(D) = (D \cdot m). Then the self-intersection number of D satisfies the following inequality: D^2 \le 2 d_1(D) d_2(D).

Proof. Let V be the 3-dimensional vector space over the field of rational numbers \mathbb{Q} spanned by the three divisor classes l, m, and D. The intersection pairing defines a quadratic form on V. According to the previous Propositions , we have already computed the intersection numbers among the basis elements: l^2 = 0, m^2 = 0, and (l \cdot m) = 1. Therefore, the matrix M of this quadratic form with respect to the basis \{l, m, D\} is: M = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & d_1(D) \\ 1 & 0 & d_2(D) \\ d_1(D) & d_2(D) & D^2 \end{pmatrix} Computing the determinant of this matrix: \det M = 0 - 1 \cdot (1 \cdot D^2 - d_1(D) d_2(D)) + d_1(D) \cdot (\operatorname{d}_2(D) - 0) = 2d_1(D)d_2(D) - D^2.

We assert that \det M \ge 0. Suppose that \det M < 0. We select an orthogonal basis E_1, E_2, E_3 in V with respect to this quadratic form. Let their corresponding diagonal entries of the quadratic form be a_i = (E_i \cdot E_i). The sign of the determinant is invariant under a change of basis, so a_1 a_2 a_3 < 0. On the other hand, since the top-left 2 \times 2 submatrix of M is \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, its eigenvalues are 1 and -1, which indicates that the quadratic form on V is indefinite and it is impossible for all eigenvalues to be negative. Since the three eigenvalues cannot all be negative and their product is negative, the only possibility is that there are two positive eigenvalues and one negative eigenvalue (i.e., the signature is (+, +, -)). Without loss of generality, assume a_1 > 0 and a_2 > 0. This implies that there exists a 2-dimensional positive definite subspace \operatorname{span}(E_1, E_2) in V.

Let H = l + m. Since \operatorname{H}^2 = 2 > 0, and the intersection numbers of H with both the horizontal and vertical fibers on S are strictly positive, H is an ample divisor on the surface S according to the Nakai-Moishezon criterion.

Let W \subset \operatorname{Num}(S) \otimes \mathbb{Q} be the 2-dimensional rational vector space spanned by E_1 and E_2. On W, the intersection quadratic form is strictly positive definite. Now consider the linear intersection map induced by the ample divisor H: W \to \mathbb{Q}, \quad E \mapsto (E \cdot H). Since the target space is 1-dimensional and the domain W is 2-dimensional, according to the Rank-Nullity Theorem in linear algebra, the dimension of the kernel of this map is at least \dim W - 1 = 1. This means that in the positive definite subspace W, there must exist a non-zero rational vector E_{\mathbb{Q}} satisfying the orthogonality condition (E_{\mathbb{Q}} \cdot H) = 0.

We multiply E_{\mathbb{Q}} by a suitable scalar to clear the denominators, thereby obtaining an integral divisor class E. Since scalar multiplication does not alter the orthogonality or the sign of the quadratic form, this divisor E still satisfies:

  1. E is a numerically non-trivial integral divisor (E \not\equiv 0);

  2. E is orthogonal to the ample divisor, i.e., (E \cdot H) = 0.

At this point, we observe the self-intersection number of E. On one hand, because E is a non-zero element in the positive definite subspace W, its self-intersection number must be strictly positive: E^2 > 0. On the other hand, because H is an ample divisor and the divisor E \not\equiv 0 satisfies (E \cdot H) = 0, its self-intersection number must be strictly negative according to the Hodge Index Theorem: E^2 < 0. This yields a contradiction.

Therefore, the initial assumption \det M < 0 is false, and we must have \det M \ge 0. Substituting the explicit expression for the determinant gives: 2d_1(D)d_2(D) - D^2 \ge 0 \implies D^2 \le 2d_1(D)d_2(D). The lemma is proved. ◻

Based on the aforementioned Castelnuovo-Severi inequality, we define a symmetric bilinear form on the product surface S = C \times_k C. For any two divisors D, E on S, we define: [D, E] = d_1(D)d_2(E) + d_1(E)d_2(D) - (D \cdot E). According to Lemma (Castelnuovo Inequality), we have [D, D] = 2d_1(D)d_2(D) - D^2 \ge 0. Therefore, this bilinear form is positive semi-definite on \operatorname{Num}(S) \otimes \mathbb{Q}. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for positive semi-definite bilinear forms, we must have: |[D, E]| \le \sqrt{[D, D] \cdot [E, E]} \quad \text{--- (Eq. 1)}.

Theorem 3.1 (Intersection Inequality for Graph of Morphisms). Let \varphi: C \to C be any finite morphism on C with degree d = \deg(\varphi). Let \Gamma_\varphi be its graph divisor and \Delta be the diagonal divisor. Then: |(\Gamma_\varphi \cdot \Delta) - 1 - d| \le 2g \sqrt{d}.

Proof. We substitute D = \Gamma_\varphi and E = \Delta into Eq. 1. We need to compute each value under this bilinear form.

By Proposition , the diagonal intersects both the horizontal fiber l and the vertical fiber m transversely at a single point, so d_1(\Delta) = 1 and d_2(\Delta) = 1. According to Proposition , \Delta^2 = 2 - 2g. Therefore: [\Delta, \Delta] = 2(1)(1) - (2 - 2g) = 2g.

The graph divisor \Gamma_\varphi is the closure of the point set \{(x, \varphi(x))\}. By Proposition , its intersection with the vertical fiber m = \{x\} \times C occurs at exactly one place, namely (x, \varphi(x)), so d_2(\Gamma_\varphi) = 1. Its intersection with the horizontal fiber l = C \times \{y\} corresponds to the number of solutions to the equation \varphi(x) = y, which means d_1(\Gamma_\varphi) = \deg(\varphi) = d.

Combining this with the result of Proposition , we can calculate the semi-positive norm of \Gamma_\varphi: [\Gamma_\varphi, \Gamma_\varphi] = 2 d_1(\Gamma_\varphi) d_2(\Gamma_\varphi) - \Gamma_\varphi^2 = 2(d)(1) - d(2 - 2g) = 2dg.

Computing the cross term of the bilinear form: [\Gamma_\varphi, \Delta] = d_1(\Gamma_\varphi)d_2(\Delta) + d_1(\Delta)d_2(\Gamma_\varphi) - (\Gamma_\varphi \cdot \Delta) = d(1) + 1(1) - (\Gamma_\varphi \cdot \Delta). Substituting all the above results into the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality |[\Gamma_\varphi, \Delta]| \le \sqrt{[\Gamma_\varphi, \Gamma_\varphi] \cdot [\Delta, \Delta]}: |d + 1 - (\Gamma_\varphi \cdot \Delta)| \le \sqrt{(2dg) \cdot (2g)} = 2g \sqrt{d}. Rearranging the signs inside the absolute value slightly yields the desiUniBlue result: |(\Gamma_\varphi \cdot \Delta) - 1 - d| \le 2g \sqrt{d}. ◻

Theorem 3.1 (Weil Conjectures: Riemann Hypothesis for Curves). For a smooth projective curve C of genus g defined over \mathbb{F}_q, the number N_s of its \mathbb{F}_{q^s}-rational points satisfies the celebrated Hasse-Weil bound: |N_s - q^s - 1| \le 2g q^{s/2}.

Proof. We take the morphism \varphi to be the s-th iterate of the absolute Frobenius morphism, i.e., \varphi = F^s: C \to C. In local coordinates, F^s is given by the ring homomorphism a \mapsto a^{q^s}. Since C is a smooth one-dimensional curve, the degree of the field extension of function fields [K(C) : F^{s*} K(C)] is clearly q^s. This implies that the degree of the morphism F^s is: d = \deg(F^s) = q^s. On the other hand, according to our conclusion preceding Proposition , the Frobenius graph \Gamma_{F^s} and the diagonal \Delta intersect transversely, and the intersection number is exactly identical to the number of rational points: (\Gamma_{F^s} \cdot \Delta) = N_s. Directly substituting d = q^s and (\Gamma_{F^s} \cdot \Delta) = N_s into the inequality of Theorem , we arrive at the magnificent inequality: |N_s - q^s - 1| \le 2g \sqrt{q^s} = 2g q^{s/2}. According to the equivalence proven in our earlier lemmas, this is equivalent to stating that the absolute values of all roots \alpha_i of the numerator polynomial \prod_{i=1}^{2g}(1-\alpha_i t) of its Zeta function strictly satisfy |\alpha_i| = \sqrt{q}.

The Weil Conjectures for curves are now completely proved. ◻

Non-geometrically irreducible case

Any irreducible curve defined over \mathbb{F}_q is essentially a geometrically irreducible curve over some larger finite field \mathbb{F}_{q^d}. We call the field k' the field of global sections of the curve X. Through this perspective, we can directly lift the base field of the Zeta function to k'.

Proposition 3.1 (Global Sections). Let k be a field. Let X be an irreducible, smooth, and projective curve (1-dimensional scheme) over k. Let k' = \Gamma(X, \mathcal{O}_X) be the ring of global regular functions on X. Then:

  1. k' is a finite field extension of k.

  2. The structure morphism X \to \operatorname{Spec}k naturally factors as X \to \operatorname{Spec}k' \to \operatorname{Spec}k, and X, when viewed as a scheme defined over k', is geometrically irreducible.

Proof. (1) Because X is a projective scheme over k, it is proper over k. According to the finiteness theorem for coherent sheaf cohomology on proper schemes, the ring of global sections k' = \Gamma(X, \mathcal{O}_X) is a finite-dimensional k-vector space, making it a finite k-algebra. Furthermore, because X is irreducible and smooth over k, it is an integral scheme. The ring of global regular functions on an integral scheme contains no zero divisors, so k' is an integral domain. A finite-dimensional integral domain over a field is necessarily a field. Therefore, k' is a finite field extension of k.

(2) Since k' = \Gamma(X, \mathcal{O}_X), the structure sheaf \mathcal{O}_X is naturally equipped with the structure of a sheaf of k'-algebras. This is categorically equivalent to providing a natural morphism of schemes X \to \operatorname{Spec}k', which endows X with the structure of a k'-scheme. The composition of this morphism with the canonical projection \operatorname{Spec}k' \to \operatorname{Spec}k recovers the original structure morphism X \to \operatorname{Spec}k.

Now, we consider X as a scheme defined over the new base field k'. By construction, its ring of global functions over this new base field is exactly k' itself; that is, \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X) = k'. Because X is smooth over k, it is geometrically regular over k. Consequently, X \otimes_k \overline{k} is a regular scheme. Since the algebraic closure \overline{k'} is isomorphic to \overline{k}, the base change X \otimes_{k'} \overline{k'} is also a regular scheme. This implies that X is geometrically regular, and thus geometrically normal, over k'.

By Lemma , for a proper geometrically normal scheme over a field, the condition \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X) = k' is strictly equivalent to the scheme being geometrically irreducible. Therefore, X as a curve defined over k' is geometrically irreducible. ◻

Since X, viewed as a scheme over k', is geometrically irreducible, we next establish the connection between the Zeta function of X over the base field k = \mathbb{F}_q and its Zeta function over the field of global sections k' = \mathbb{F}_{q^d}.

Proposition 3.1 (Base Field Lifting of the Zeta Function). Let X be an irreducible smooth projective curve defined over k = \mathbb{F}_q, whose field of global sections is k' = \mathbb{F}_{q^d}. Then its local Zeta functions over the two different base fields satisfy the following identity: Z(X/k, t) = Z(X/k', t^d).

Proof. We will prove this using the Euler product formula of the Zeta function. Recall the product definition of the Zeta function: Z(X/k, t) = \prod_{x \in |X|} (1 - t^{\deg_k(x)})^{-1}. where |X| is the topological space of closed points of X, and \deg_k(x) = [\kappa(x) : k] is the degree of the closed point x over the base field k.

As mentioned previously, the structure morphism can be factoUniBlue as X \to \operatorname{Spec}k' \to \operatorname{Spec}k. Therefore, for any closed point x \in |XBase|, its residue field \kappa(x) must contain the field of global sections k'. According to the tower formula for field extensions: [\kappa(x) : k] = [\kappa(x) : k'] \cdot [k' : k]. which means \deg_k(x) = d \cdot \deg_{k'}(x).

Substituting this degree relation into the Euler product formula for X/k yields: \begin{align*} Z(X/k, t) &= \prod_{x \in |X|} (1 - t^{d \cdot \deg_{k'}(x)})^{-1} \\ &= \prod_{x \in |X|} (1 - (t^d)^{\deg_{k'}(x)})^{-1} \end{align*} Observing the right-hand side of the equation, this is precisely the Euler product formula for X as a k'-scheme, with the formal variable replaced by t^d. Therefore, we obtain the equality: Z(X/k, t) = Z(X/k', t^d). The proposition is proved. ◻

By virtue of the above proposition, and since X is strictly geometrically irreducible over k', we can apply the previously established conclusions to naturally deduce the Weil Conjectures for any irreducible curve.

Theorem 3.1 (Weil Conjectures for Any Irreducible Curve). Let X be an irreducible smooth projective curve over a finite field k = \mathbb{F}_q with genus g and the field of global sections k' = \mathbb{F}_{q^d}. Then the Zeta function of X/k satisfies:

  1. Rationality: Z(X/k, t) can be written as: Z(X/k, t) = \frac{P(t^d)}{(1-t^d)(1-(qt)^d)}. where P(u) \in \mathbb{Z}[u] and \deg_u P = 2g (the degree of the polynomial expanded in terms of t is 2dg).

  2. Functional Equation: Z\left(X/k, \frac{1}{qt}\right) = q^{d(1-g)} t^{2d(1-g)} Z(X/k, t).

  3. Riemann Hypothesis: After expanding the numerator polynomial into a product of linear factors in terms of t as \prod (1 - \alpha t), all reciprocal roots \alpha satisfy the complex absolute value condition: |\alpha| = \sqrt{q}.

Proof. By Proposition , it is known that Z(X/k, t) = Z(X/k', u), where we use the substitution u = t^d. Since X is a geometrically irreducible curve of genus g over k', and the cardinality of k' is q' = q^d, we can directly apply the conclusions of the Weil Conjectures for geometrically irreducible curves to Z(X/k', u).

(1) Rationality: According to the prior rationality conclusion, Z(X/k', u) can be expressed as: Z(X/k', u) = \frac{P(u)}{(1-u)(1-q'u)}. where P(u) is a polynomial with integral coefficients and \deg_u P = 2g. Substituting u = t^d and q' = q^d into the equation, we immediately obtain: Z(X/k, t) = \frac{P(t^d)}{(1-t^d)(1-q^dt^d)} = \frac{P(t^d)}{(1-t^d)(1-(qt)^d)}. Rationality is proved.

(2) Functional Equation: According to the functional equation in the geometrically irreducible case, substituting the variable u and the cardinality q' gives: Z\left(X/k', \frac{1}{q'u}\right) = (q')^{1-g} u^{2-2g} Z(X/k', u). Now we calculate the term to be verified, Z\left(X/k, \frac{1}{qt}\right). Utilizing Proposition : Z\left(X/k, \frac{1}{qt}\right) = Z\left(X/k', \left(\frac{1}{qt}\right)^d\right) = Z\left(X/k', \frac{1}{q^dt^d}\right) = Z\left(X/k', \frac{1}{q'u}\right). Substituting the functional equation of X/k' above and restoring u back to t^d: \begin{align*} Z\left(X/k, \frac{1}{qt}\right) &= (q^d)^{1-g} (t^d)^{2-2g} Z(X/k', t^d) \\ &= q^{d(1-g)} t^{2d(1-g)} Z(X/k, t) \end{align*} The functional equation is proved.

(3) Riemann Hypothesis: According to the Riemann Hypothesis in the geometrically irreducible case, the polynomial P(u) can be factoUniBlue into \prod_{i=1}^{2g} (1 - \gamma_i u), and the absolute values of the roots corresponding to the Frobenius action satisfy: |\gamma_i| = \sqrt{q'} = q^{d/2}. Now we examine the expanded polynomial in terms of t, P(t^d) = \prod_{i=1}^{2g} (1 - \gamma_i t^d). For each factor 1 - \gamma_i t^d, it can be further decomposed over the complex field into a product of d linear terms: 1 - \gamma_i t^d = \prod_{j=1}^d (1 - \alpha_{ij} t). where \alpha_{ij} is clearly a d-th root of \gamma_i, satisfying \alpha_{ij}^d = \gamma_i. Taking the complex absolute value on both sides of this equation: |\alpha_{ij}|^d = |\gamma_i| = q^{d/2}. Since the absolute value is a positive real number, taking the d-th root on both sides immediately yields the absolute value of all roots \alpha_{ij} in the new numerator: |\alpha_{ij}| = q^{1/2} = \sqrt{q}. This matches the assertion of the Riemann Hypothesis. The Weil Conjectures for any irreducible curve are thus completely proved. ◻

The case with singularities

We now extend our investigation of Zeta functions to algebraic curves that are permitted to have singularities. Let X be an irreducible, projective algebraic curve defined over the finite field \mathbb{F}_q.

To study the geometric properties of a singular curve, the standard algebraic technique is to relate it to its normalization. We denote by \tilde{X} the normalization of X. Geometrically, \tilde{X} is a smooth projective curve, and there is a natural normalization map: \nu \colon \tilde{X} \to X. This map is a birational, surjective, finite morphism. For any closed point P \in X, its degree over the base field \mathbb{F}_q is given by the degree of the field extension of its residue field: d_P = [\mathbb{F}_q(P) : \mathbb{F}_q].

Theorem 3.1 (Zeta Function of a Singular Curve). Let S be the finite set of singular points of X. The Zeta function of X is a rational function of the form: Z_X(T) = \frac{P_X(T)}{(1 - T)(1 - qT)}. where the numerator polynomial P_X(T) is explicitly related to the numerator polynomial P_{\tilde{X}}(T) of the normalized smooth curve \tilde{X} by the formula: P_X(T) = P_{\tilde{X}}(T) \prod_{P \in S} \left( \frac{\prod_{Q \in \nu^{-1}(P)} (1 - T^{d_Q})}{1 - T^{d_P}} \right).

Proof. By definition, the Zeta function is constructed from the number of rational points over finite extensions. We analyze the quotient of the Zeta functions of X and \tilde{X} by expressing them via their exponential generating functions: \begin{align*} \frac{Z_X(T)}{Z_{\tilde{X}}(T)} &= \exp\left( \sum_{n=1}^\infty \left( \#X(\mathbb{F}_{q^n}) - \#\tilde{X}(\mathbb{F}_{q^n}) \right) \frac{T^n}{n} \right) \end{align*} The normalization map \nu \colon \tilde{X} \to X is an isomorphism outside the singular locus S. Therefore, any discrepancy in the point counts must originate exclusively from points lying over S.

Let \alpha_P(n) denote the number of points on \tilde{X} that lie over P and are rational over \mathbb{F}_{q^n}. For the base point P itself, it contributes to \#X(\mathbb{F}_{q^n}) if and only if it is rational over \mathbb{F}_{q^n}, which occurs precisely when its degree d_P divides n. We encode this using an indicator function: let \delta_{m \mid n} = 1 if m divides n, and 0 otherwise. The difference in point counts can then be localized to sums over the singular points P \in S: \frac{Z_X(T)}{Z_{\tilde{X}}(T)} = \prod_{P \in S} \exp\left( -\sum_{n=1}^\infty (\alpha_P(n) - d_P)\delta_{d_P \mid n} \frac{T^n}{n} \right). We can simplify the exponent by re-indexing the summation. Since non-zero terms only occur when n is a multiple of d_P, we substitute n = m d_P: \frac{Z_X(T)}{Z_{\tilde{X}}(T)} = \prod_{P \in S} \exp\left( -\sum_{m=1}^\infty (\alpha_P(m d_P) - d_P) \frac{T^{m d_P}}{m d_P} \right).

Now we must evaluate \alpha_P(m d_P). The points on \tilde{X} lying over P are exactly the elements Q \in \nu^{-1}(P). Similar to P, a point Q contributes to the rational point count over \mathbb{F}_{q^{m d_P}} if and only if its degree d_Q divides the extension degree m d_P. Summing the contributions of these conjugate points yields: \alpha_P(m d_P) = \sum_{Q \in \nu^{-1}(P)} d_Q \delta_{d_Q \mid m d_P}. Substituting this explicit sum back into the exponential expression, we get: \begin{align*} \frac{Z_X(T)}{Z_{\tilde{X}}(T)} &= \prod_{P \in S} \left( \frac{\prod_{Q \in \nu^{-1}(P)} \exp\left( -\sum_{m=1}^\infty d_Q \delta_{d_Q \mid m d_P} \frac{T^{m d_P}}{m d_P} \right)}{\exp\left( -\sum_{m=1}^\infty d_P \frac{T^{m d_P}}{m d_P} \right)} \right) \end{align*} The denominator in this product evaluates cleanly using the Taylor series for the logarith: \exp(-\sum_{m=1}^\infty \frac{(T^{d_P})^m}{m}) = \exp(\log(1 - T^{d_P})) = 1 - T^{d_P}.

For the numerator, we evaluate the sum for each fixed Q. The non-zero terms occur when d_Q \mid m d_P, so we can substitute the index \ell = m d_P / d_Q: \begin{align*} \exp\left( -\sum_{m=1}^\infty d_Q \delta_{d_Q \mid m d_P} \frac{T^{m d_P}}{m d_P} \right) &= \exp\left( -\sum_{\ell=1}^\infty d_Q \frac{T^{\ell d_Q}}{\ell d_Q} \right) \\ &= \exp\left( -\sum_{\ell=1}^\infty \frac{(T^{d_Q})^\ell}{\ell} \right) \\ &= 1 - T^{d_Q} \end{align*} Assembling these evaluated sums confirms the theorem’s formula: \frac{Z_X(T)}{Z_{\tilde{X}}(T)} = \prod_{P \in S} \left( \frac{\prod_{Q \in \nu^{-1}(P)} (1 - T^{d_Q})}{1 - T^{d_P}} \right). Because Z_{\tilde{X}}(T) = P_{\tilde{X}}(T) / ((1-T)(1-qT)), we simply multiply both sides by P_{\tilde{X}}(T) to solve for P_X(T). ◻

Theorem demonstrates that the Zeta function of a singular curve X is the product of the numerator polynomial of its smooth normalization (which has degree 2g_{\tilde{X}}) multiplied by a correction polynomial. The degree of this correction polynomial is precisely defined as: \Delta_X = \#\{\tilde{X}(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_q) \setminus X(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_q)\}. where \overline{\mathbb{F}}_q is an algebraic closure of \mathbb{F}_q. The quantity \Delta_X is well-defined because the singular locus S is a finite subset of X. In the context of generalized Jacobians, \Delta_X represents the dimension of the toric component of the generalized Jacobian of X.

To further understand the difference between a singular point and its preimages on the normalization, we must analyze their local rings. Let P \in X(\mathbb{F}_q) be an \mathbb{F}_q-rational singular point. Following the notation of Theorem , let \alpha_P(1) = \alpha be the number of \mathbb{F}_q-rational points of \tilde{X} lying over P, and let \alpha_P(\infty) be the total number of points on \tilde{X} lying over P over the algebraic closure.

Let \mathcal{O}_P be the local ring of the point P on X, and let \overline{\mathcal{O}_P} be its integral closure within the function field \mathbb{F}_q(X). Because the curve is a one-dimensional Noetherian scheme, the quotient \overline{\mathcal{O}_P} / \mathcal{O}_P is a finite-dimensional vector space over \mathbb{F}_q. We define its dimension as the delta invariant of the singularity: \delta_P = \dim_{\mathbb{F}_q} (\overline{\mathcal{O}_P} / \mathcal{O}_P).

Lemma 3.1 (Lower Bound for the Delta Invariant). Let P be an \mathbb{F}_q-rational singular point of X. Then the number of rational branches over P is bounded by the delta invariant: \alpha_P(1) - 1 \le \delta_P.

Proof. Let Q_1, \dots, Q_{\alpha_P(\infty)} be all the points of \tilde{X}(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_q) lying over P. By definition, the first \alpha = \alpha_P(1) points are exactly the \mathbb{F}_q-rational ones.

The integral closure \overline{\mathcal{O}_P} consists of all rational functions that are regular at every branch over P. That is, \overline{\mathcal{O}_P} = \bigcap_{i} \mathcal{O}_{Q_i}. Evaluating these regular functions at the rational branches defines a natural \mathbb{F}_q-linear evaluation map: \begin{align*} \phi \colon \overline{\mathcal{O}_P} &\to \mathbb{F}_q^\alpha \\ f &\mapsto (f(Q_1), \dots, f(Q_\alpha)) \end{align*}

We first prove that \phi is surjective. Let (x_1, \dots, x_\alpha) \in \mathbb{F}_q^\alpha be an arbitrary target vector. Since \mathbb{F}_q \subset \mathbb{F}_q(X), we can set constant functions f_i = x_i for 1 \le i \le \alpha. For the non-rational branches i \ge \alpha + 1, we simply set f_i = 0. The points Q_i are distinct valuations on the function field. By the Weak Approximation Theorem for valuations, there exists a rational function g \in \mathbb{F}_q(X) that simultaneously approximates all these targets. Specifically, we can find a g such that the valuation v_{Q_i}(g - f_i) \ge 1 for all 1 \le i \le \alpha_P(\infty).

This condition immediately implies two things. First, because f_i are constants and v_{Q_i}(g - f_i) \ge 1 \ge 0, the function g must have no poles at any Q_i, meaning g \in \bigcap \mathcal{O}_{Q_i} = \overline{\mathcal{O}_P}. Second, evaluating g at the rational branches gives exactly g(Q_i) = f_i(Q_i) = x_i for all 1 \le i \le \alpha. Therefore, \phi(g) = (x_1, \dots, x_\alpha), proving that the linear map \phi is strictly surjective onto \mathbb{F}_q^\alpha.

Next, we analyze the restriction of \phi to the original local ring \mathcal{O}_P. If a function f actually belongs to \mathcal{O}_P, it is defined at the singular point P itself. Its value at all branches lying over P must simply be the value of the function evaluated at P. Therefore, for any f \in \mathcal{O}_P: f(Q_1) = f(Q_2) = \dots = f(Q_\alpha) = f(P). This demonstrates that the image of \mathcal{O}_P under \phi is restricted entirely to the one-dimensional diagonal subspace L \subset \mathbb{F}_q^\alpha defined by elements of the form (x, x, \dots, x).

Because \phi(\mathcal{O}_P) \subseteq L, the surjective linear map \phi canonically factors through the quotient space to induce a new surjective linear map: \tilde{\phi} \colon \overline{\mathcal{O}_P} / \mathcal{O}_P \twoheadrightarrow \mathbb{F}_q^\alpha / L. Because \tilde{\phi} is surjective, the dimension of the domain must be greater than or equal to the dimension of the target space. The dimension of the domain is \delta_P. The dimension of \mathbb{F}_q^\alpha is \alpha, and the dimension of the diagonal line L is 1, so the quotient space has dimension \alpha - 1. This directly establishes the inequality \delta_P \ge \alpha - 1, proving the lemma. ◻

Proposition 3.1 (Bound on the Discrepancy of Rational Points). Let X be an absolutely irreducible projective curve over \mathbb{F}_q with arithmetic genus \pi, and let \tilde{X} be its normalization with geometric genus g. The difference between the number of rational points on the smooth normalization and the singular curve is bounded by: |\#\tilde{X}(\mathbb{F}_q) - \#X(\mathbb{F}_q)| \le \pi - g.

Proof. Let P \in X be a closed point and Q \in \tilde{X} be a point lying over P under the normalization map \nu \colon \tilde{X} \to X. Because the normalization map is defined over the base field \mathbb{F}_q, if Q is an \mathbb{F}_q-rational point, its image P = \nu(Q) must also be an \mathbb{F}_q-rational point. Therefore, when counting rational points, we only need to compare the rational points lying over the rational singular locus.

Using the notation established previously, the difference in point counts is exactly the sum of the surplus branches over each rational singular point: \#\tilde{X}(\mathbb{F}_q) - \#X(\mathbb{F}_q) = \sum_{P \in \operatorname{Sing} X(\mathbb{F}_q)} (\alpha_P(1) - 1). By Lemma , we know that for every rational singular point, \alpha_P(1) - 1 \le \delta_P. A fundamental theorem of algebraic curves relates the arithmetic genus \pi and the geometric genus g via the sum of the delta invariants over all singular points (over the algebraic closure): \pi - g = \sum_{P \in \operatorname{Sing} X(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_q)} \delta_P. Since \delta_P \ge 0 for all points, restricting the sum to strictly the \mathbb{F}_q-rational singular points gives the upper bound: \sum_{P \in \operatorname{Sing} X(\mathbb{F}_q)} (\alpha_P(1) - 1) \le \sum_{P \in \operatorname{Sing} X(\mathbb{F}_q)} \delta_P \le \pi - g.

To establish the lower bound, we observe that the number of rational branches \alpha_P(1) is inherently non-negative (\alpha_P(1) \ge 0). Therefore, \alpha_P(1) - 1 \ge -1. Summing this over the rational singular points yields: \sum_{P \in \operatorname{Sing} X(\mathbb{F}_q)} (\alpha_P(1) - 1) \ge -\#\operatorname{Sing} X(\mathbb{F}_q). Furthermore, because every singular point P must have a strictly positive delta invariant (\delta_P \ge 1), the total number of singular points over the algebraic closure is bounded by the total sum of their delta invariants: -\#\operatorname{Sing} X(\mathbb{F}_q) \ge -\#\operatorname{Sing} X(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_q) \ge -\sum_{P \in \operatorname{Sing} X(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_q)} \delta_P = -(\pi - g). Combining the upper and lower bounds confirms the absolute value inequality |\#\tilde{X}(\mathbb{F}_q) - \#X(\mathbb{F}_q)| \le \pi - g. ◻

Remark 3.1 (Structure of the Numerator Polynomial). Theorem showed that the Zeta function Z_X(T) is formed by multiplying the smooth Zeta function Z_{\tilde{X}}(T) by correction factors of the form (1 - T^{d_Q}) / (1 - T^{d_P}). Because the roots of 1 - T^k = 0 are exactly the k-th roots of unity, these correction factors contribute polynomial terms whose inverse roots have a modulus of exactly 1.

Thus, the numerator of the Zeta function of X is a polynomial P_X(T) with integer coefficients of degree 2g + \Delta_X, where \Delta_X = \#\{\tilde{X}(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_q) \setminus X(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_q)\}. The total degree lies in the range \{2g, \dots, \pi + g\}. The inverse roots of P_X(T) fall strictly into two categories:

  • 2g roots coming from the smooth normalization, which have modulus \sqrt{q} (by the Riemann Hypothesis).

  • \Delta_X cyclotomic roots coming from the singularities, which have modulus 1.

Corollary 3.1 (Hasse-Weil Bound for Singular Curves). Let \omega_1, \dots, \omega_{2g} be the inverse roots of P_{\tilde{X}}(T), and let \beta_1, \dots, \beta_{\Delta_X} be the inverse roots of the cyclotomic part of P_X(T). Then, for all integers n \ge 1, the exact point count is: \#X(\mathbb{F}_{q^n}) = q^n + 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{2g} \omega_i^n - \sum_{j=1}^{\Delta_X} \beta_j^n. In particular, applying the triangle inequality yields the generalized bound for the base field (n=1): |\#X(\mathbb{F}_q) - (q + 1)| \le 2g\sqrt{q} + \Delta_X \le 2g\sqrt{q} + \pi - g \le 2\pi\sqrt{q}. Furthermore, this implies that the dimension of the first compactly supported étale cohomology group is: \dim_{\mathbb{Q}_\ell} H_c^1(X, \mathbb{Q}_\ell) = 2g + \Delta_X.

Proof. The exact point counting formula follows directly by taking the logarithmic derivative of the expanded Zeta function, exactly as we did in the smooth case, but now accounting for the extra \Delta_X cyclotomic roots.

To establish the inequality, we apply the triangle inequality and substitute the known moduli (|\omega_i| = \sqrt{q} and |\beta_j| = 1): \left| \sum_{i=1}^{2g} \omega_i + \sum_{j=1}^{\Delta_X} \beta_j \right| \le \sum_{i=1}^{2g} \sqrt{q} + \sum_{j=1}^{\Delta_X} 1 = 2g\sqrt{q} + \Delta_X. By definition, \Delta_X \le \sum \delta_P = \pi - g, giving the second inequality. For the final step, since q \ge 1 (it is a prime power), we know \sqrt{q} \ge 1. We can therefore loosely bound the constants: 2g\sqrt{q} + (\pi - g) \le 2g\sqrt{q} + (\pi - g)\sqrt{q} = (\pi + g)\sqrt{q}. Since the geometric genus is always less than or equal to the arithmetic genus (g \le \pi), we have (\pi + g)\sqrt{q} \le 2\pi\sqrt{q}, concluding the proof of the bounds. ◻

Corollary 3.1 (Bound for Complete Intersections). If X is an absolutely irreducible curve which is a complete intersection in \mathbb{P}^{n+1} of n hypersurfaces of degrees d_1, \dots, d_n, and if we set d = \prod_{i=1}^n d_i, then: |\#X(\mathbb{F}_q) - (q + 1)| \le (d - 1)(d - 2)\sqrt{q}. In particular, this precise inequality holds for any absolutely irreducible plane curve of degree d.

Proof. The arithmetic genus \pi_X of a complete intersection curve in \mathbb{P}^{n+1} defined by hypersurfaces of degrees d_1, \dots, d_n is classically given by the formula: 2\pi_X = d\left(\sum_{i=1}^n d_i - n - 2\right) + 2. It is a purely algebraic exercise to verify that for any sequence of degrees d_i \ge 1, this quantity is bounded from above: 2\pi_X \le (d - 1)(d - 2). By Corollary , the rational points are bounded by 2\pi_X\sqrt{q}, which is immediately bounded by (d - 1)(d - 2)\sqrt{q}.

The second assertion concerns plane curves, which is the specific case where n = 1. A plane curve is a complete intersection of a single hypersurface (a curve in \mathbb{P}^2) of degree d_1 = d. Substituting n=1 and d_1=d into the arithmetic genus formula gives: 2\pi_X = d(d - 1 - 2) + 2 = d^2 - 3d + 2 = (d - 1)(d - 2). Thus, the arithmetic genus of a plane curve of degree d is exactly \pi_X = \frac{(d-1)(d-2)}{2}. Applying the bound 2\pi_X\sqrt{q} directly yields (d-1)(d-2)\sqrt{q}. ◻
