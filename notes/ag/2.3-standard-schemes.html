Dimension Theory of Standard Schemes

Recollections from Commutative Algebra

Before proceeding further into the geometry of schemes, it is necessary to recall several pivotal finiteness and dimensional conditions from commutative algebra. These ring-theoretic properties impose strict topological and algebraic regularity on the corresponding affine schemes, and they form the bedrock of the dimension theory of schemes.

We begin with the algebraic framework underlying Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz.

Definition 3.1 (Jacobson Ring). A commutative ring A is called a Jacobson ring if every prime ideal in A is the intersection of the maximal ideals containing it. Equivalently, the Jacobson radical of any quotient ring A/\mathfrak{p} (where \mathfrak{p} is a prime ideal) coincides with its nilradical, which is precisely the zero ideal (0).

The most crucial feature of Jacobson rings is their robust stability, which allows us to perform standard geometric constructions (such as taking products of affine spaces) without leaving this well-behaved class of rings.

Proposition 3.1 (Stability of Jacobson Rings). Let A be a Jacobson ring. If B is a finitely generated A-algebra, then B is also a Jacobson ring.

Proof. This is a standard algebraic generalization of Zariski’s Lemma and Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz. By induction on the number of generators, it suffices to prove the statement for a polynomial ring A[x] and for a quotient A/I. Quotients of Jacobson rings are trivially Jacobson by the correspondence theorem for ideals. The fact that A[x] is Jacobson when A is Jacobson is precisely the content of the generalized Nullstellensatz. ◻

Remark 3.1 (Geometric Implication). Geometrically, a scheme X is called Jacobson if its underlying topological space is a Jacobson space (meaning the closed points are very dense in every closed subset). Proposition implies that any scheme locally of finite type over a Jacobson scheme (such as \operatorname{Spec}k for a field k, or \operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{Z}) is automatically a Jacobson scheme.

Next, we introduce conditions that ensure the dimension theory of our schemes is well-behaved, precluding pathological chains of irreducible closed subsets.

Definition 3.2 (Catenary and Universally Catenary Rings). Let A be a commutative ring.

  1. A is catenary if, for any pair of prime ideals \mathfrak{p} \subsetneq \mathfrak{q} in A, all maximal chains of prime ideals \mathfrak{p} = \mathfrak{p}_0 \subsetneq \mathfrak{p}_1 \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq \mathfrak{p}_n = \mathfrak{q} strictly between them have the same finite length n.

  2. A Noetherian ring A is universally catenary if every finitely generated A-algebra is a catenary ring.

While catenary rings prevent local dimensional anomalies, we often require a stronger smoothness condition to perform local calculus on schemes. This is formalized by the concept of regular rings.

Definition 3.3 (Regular Ring). A Noetherian local ring (A, \mathfrak{m}, k) of Krull dimension d is a regular local ring if its maximal ideal \mathfrak{m} can be generated by exactly d elements. (Equivalently, \dim_k(\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2) = d).

A general Noetherian ring A is called a regular ring if all of its localizations A_{\mathfrak{p}} at prime ideals \mathfrak{p} are regular local rings.

Remark 3.1 (Regular implies Universally Catenary). A celebrated theorem in commutative algebra states that every regular local ring is a unique factorization domain (UFD), and every Cohen-Macaulay ring is universally catenary. Because regular rings are Cohen-Macaulay, it follows that any algebra finitely generated over a regular ring (such as a field k or the integers \mathbb{Z}) is automatically universally catenary.

Standard Schemes and Varieties

With the necessary ring-theoretic foundations established, we are now prepared to define the primary objects of study in these notes. We seek to work within a category of schemes that is geometrically rich yet sufficiently tame to avoid the pathological behaviors of arbitrary schemes. To achieve this, we first specify a well-behaved class of base rings.

Definition 3.4 (Standard Base Ring). A commutative ring S is called a standard base ring if it satisfies the following three conditions:

  1. S is a Noetherian integral domain.

  2. S is a universally catenary ring.

Prototypical examples of standard base rings include any field k, the ring of integers \mathbb{Z}, and polynomial rings over these bases. By working over a standard base ring, we ensure that the dimension theory of our spaces is well-behaved and that closed points carry sufficient geometric information.

We now define the geometric spaces built over these rings. By enforcing finiteness and separatedness, we obtain spaces that are amenable to both algebraic manipulation and geometric intuition.

Definition 3.5 (Standard Scheme and Standard Variety). Let S be a standard base ring.

  1. A scheme X is called a standard scheme over S if the structure morphism X \to \operatorname{Spec}S is separated and of finite type.

  2. If the standard base ring S is specifically a field k, a standard scheme X over k is called a standard variety (or simply a variety over k).

Remark 3.1 (Conventions on the Definition of a Variety). Our definition of a “variety” is deliberately broad. In the classical literature, conventions vary significantly: some authors (such as Hartshorne) require a variety to be an integral scheme (irreducible and reduced) separated and of finite type over an algebraically closed field. Others require it to be geometrically reduced and geometrically irreducible.

We adopt this more inclusive definition because it is highly practical in modern algebraic geometry. By merely requiring the space to be separated and of finite type over a field, we are free to study reducible spaces, spaces with nilpotent elements (non-reduced schemes, which are crucial for intersection theory and deformation theory), and spaces over non-algebraically closed fields (essential for arithmetic geometry), all under the unified umbrella of “varieties.” When stronger conditions like irreducibility or reducedness are required, they will be stated explicitly.

Remark 3.1 (The Standard Category).

Restricting our attention to this category brings several profound technical advantages that will streamline our subsequent geometric arguments:

  1. A Globally Noetherian World: By definition, X is of finite type over a Noetherian ring S. This implies X can be covered by finitely many affine open subsets \operatorname{Spec}A_i, where each A_i is a finitely generated S-algebra. By Hilbert’s Basis Theorem, each A_i is Noetherian, meaning X is a Noetherian scheme. This guarantees that our topological spaces are quasi-compact, algebraic sets decompose uniquely into irreducible components, and we can safely perform coherent cohomology and polynomial calculus (such as the theory of Kähler differentials) without pathological infinities.

  2. Automatic Finiteness and Separatedness: Any S-morphism f \colon X \to Y between standard schemes over S is automatically separated and of finite type.

    Proof. The morphism f can be factored as the composition of the graph morphism \Gamma_f \colon X \to X \times_S Y and the canonical projection p_2 \colon X \times_S Y \to Y. Because Y is a standard scheme, its structure morphism Y \to \operatorname{Spec}S is separated. By Proposition , this implies the graph \Gamma_f is a closed immersion. Closed immersions are inherently separated and of finite type. Furthermore, the projection p_2 is the base change of the finite type structure morphism X \to \operatorname{Spec}S, meaning p_2 is of finite type (and separated, as base changes of separated morphisms are separated). Since separatedness and finite type are stable under composition, the composition f = p_2 \circ \Gamma_f is separated and of finite type. ◻

  3. Robust Dimension Theory: Because S is universally catenary and Jacobson, and X is of finite type over S, all our standard schemes will inherit these incredibly strong topological and dimensional properties. This sets the perfect stage for the global dimension theory we will develop in the subsequent sections.

To demonstrate the utility of our global convention, we now formally verify that the most fundamental geometric spaces and categorical constructions remain securely within the category of standard schemes.

Proposition 3.1 (Examples and Stability of Standard Schemes). Let S be a standard base ring.

  1. Standard Spaces: For any integer n \ge 0, the affine space \mathbb{A}^n_S and the projective space \mathbb{P}^n_S are standard schemes over S.

  2. Subschemes: Any open or closed subscheme of a standard scheme over S is itself a standard scheme over S.

  3. Fiber Products: If X and Y are standard schemes over S, their fiber product X \times_S Y is a standard scheme over S.

Proof. For (1), the affine space \mathbb{A}^n_S = \operatorname{Spec}S[x_1, \dots, x_n] is an affine scheme over S, hence separated over S. It is of finite type because S[x_1, \dots, x_n] is manifestly a finitely generated S-algebra. The projective space \mathbb{P}^n_S = \operatorname{Proj}S[x_0, \dots, x_n] is covered by n+1 affine open patches, each isomorphic to \mathbb{A}^n_S. This proves it is locally of finite type and quasi-compact, hence of finite type. The separatedness of \mathbb{P}^n_S over S is a classical result; one can verify it using the affine intersection criterion (Proposition ), as the intersection of any two basic affine open sets D_+(x_i) \cap D_+(x_j) is affine, and the corresponding tensor product of coordinate rings is surjective.

For (2), let X be a standard scheme over S, which means its structure morphism p_X \colon X \to \operatorname{Spec}S is separated and of finite type. Let i \colon Z \hookrightarrow X be an immersion (either open or closed). Immersions are always separated. Furthermore, a closed immersion is finite, hence of finite type; an open immersion into a Noetherian scheme (which X is, by our global convention) is quasi-compact, and since it is locally of finite type, it is globally of finite type. Thus, i is separated and of finite type. By the stability of these properties under composition (Proposition and Proposition ), the structure morphism for Z, given by p_X \circ i \colon Z \to \operatorname{Spec}S, is separated and of finite type.

For (3), we utilize the stability of separatedness and finite type under base change and composition. The structure morphism of the fiber product X \times_S Y \to \operatorname{Spec}S naturally factors as: X \times_S Y \xrightarrow{p_2} Y \xrightarrow{p_Y} \operatorname{Spec}S The canonical projection p_2 is the base change of the structure morphism p_X \colon X \to \operatorname{Spec}S along p_Y. Because p_X is separated and of finite type, its base change p_2 strictly inherits both properties. Since p_Y is also separated and of finite type, the composed morphism p_Y \circ p_2 is separated and of finite type. Therefore, X \times_S Y is a standard scheme over S. ◻

Remark 3.1 (Standard Varieties). Recall that standard varieties are defined simply as standard schemes where the base ring is a field k. Because the category of varieties is just a specific slice of the category of standard schemes, all the stability properties established in Proposition apply identically. Consequently, affine spaces over k, projective spaces over k, subvarieties, and products of varieties all naturally form standard varieties. We never need to worry about accidentally leaving the category during standard geometric constructions.

Properties of Dimension

We now develop the dimension theory of schemes. The topological dimension (or Krull dimension) of a scheme X, denoted \dim X, is defined as the supremum of the lengths n of all strict chains of irreducible closed subsets Z_0 \subsetneq Z_1 \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq Z_n in the underlying topological space of X.

Because of our Global Convention (Note ), our schemes are Noetherian and of finite type over a standard base ring. Consequently, these dimensions are always finite, and suprema are achieved as maxima.

Proposition 3.1 (Basic Properties of Scheme Dimension). Let X be a standard scheme. The dimension of X satisfies the following foundational properties:

  1. (Affine Schemes): If X = \operatorname{Spec}A is an affine scheme, the topological dimension of X is exactly the Krull dimension of the ring A: \dim X = \dim A.

  2. (Topological Invariance): The dimension of a scheme depends only on its underlying topological space. In particular, passing to the reduced induced subscheme does not change the dimension: \dim X = \dim X_{red}.

  3. (Open Covers): If X = \bigcup_{\alpha \in I} U_\alpha is any open cover of X, the dimension of X is the maximum of the dimensions of its open patches: \dim X = \max_{\alpha \in I} \dim U_\alpha.

  4. (Local Rings): The dimension of X can be computed microscopically by taking the maximum of the Krull dimensions of the local stalk rings at all points x \in X: \dim X = \max_{x \in X} \dim \mathcal{O}_{X,x}.

Proof. We prove these properties sequentially:

(1) By definition, the topological dimension of X = \operatorname{Spec}A is the supremum of the lengths of strict chains of irreducible closed subsets. In the Zariski topology of an affine scheme, every irreducible closed subset is of the form V(\mathfrak{p}) for some prime ideal \mathfrak{p} \subset A. Furthermore, the inclusion relation is order-reversing: V(\mathfrak{p}) \subsetneq V(\mathfrak{q}) if and only if \mathfrak{p} \supsetneq \mathfrak{q}. Thus, strict chains of irreducible closed subsets correspond bijectively to strict chains of prime ideals in A. The supremum of the lengths of such prime ideal chains is precisely the Krull dimension of A.

(2) By the construction of the reduced induced subscheme, the canonical closed immersion i \colon X_{red} \hookrightarrow X induces a strict homeomorphism between their underlying topological spaces. Since the definition of topological dimension \dim X relies exclusively on the poset structure of closed subsets in the topological space, identical topological spaces inherently possess identical dimensions.

(3) First, since each U_\alpha \subseteq X is a topological subspace, any chain of irreducible closed subsets in U_\alpha can be closed in X (by taking Zariski closures) to form a chain of irreducible closed subsets in X. Thus, \dim U_\alpha \le \dim X for all \alpha, which implies \sup \dim U_\alpha \le \dim X.

Conversely, let Z_0 \subsetneq Z_1 \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq Z_n be a strict chain of irreducible closed subsets in X. Because the chain is strict and non-empty, we can select a point x \in Z_0. This point must be covered by some open patch U_{\alpha_0} in our cover. Intersecting the entire chain with this patch yields Z_i \cap U_{\alpha_0}, which are non-empty irreducible closed subsets of U_{\alpha_0} because x \in Z_i \cap U_{\alpha_0} for all i. Furthermore, because Z_i \cap U_{\alpha_0} is dense in Z_i, the strict nested inclusions are perfectly preserved: Z_0 \cap U_{\alpha_0} \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq Z_n \cap U_{\alpha_0}. This forms a chain of length n in U_{\alpha_0}, showing n \le \dim U_{\alpha_0} \le \max \dim U_\alpha. Hence, \dim X \le \max \dim U_\alpha, establishing equality.

(4) Using property (3), we can compute the dimension locally by choosing a finite affine open cover X = \bigcup \operatorname{Spec}A_i. Thus, it suffices to prove the statement for an affine scheme X = \operatorname{Spec}A.

By property (1), \dim X = \dim A. In commutative algebra, the Krull dimension of a ring A is the supremum of the heights of all its prime ideals \mathfrak{p}. The local ring at the point x = [\mathfrak{p}] is precisely the localization A_{\mathfrak{p}}, and the Krull dimension of A_{\mathfrak{p}} is exactly the height of \mathfrak{p}. Therefore: \dim X = \dim A = \sup_{\mathfrak{p} \in \operatorname{Spec}A} \operatorname{ht}(\mathfrak{p}) = \sup_{x \in X} \dim \mathcal{O}_{X,x}. Because X is a standard scheme over S, it is Noetherian, so this dimension is finite and the supremum is achieved as a maximum at some closed point. ◻

The Dimension Function Induced from the Base

Let f\colon X\to B=\operatorname{Spec}S be the structure morphism. For a point s\in B, write \operatorname{ht}_S(s) for the height of the corresponding prime ideal.

Definition 3.6 (The Standard Dimension Function). Define \delta_B(s):=-\operatorname{ht}_S(s), \qquad \delta_{X/S}(x):=\delta_B(f(x))+ \operatorname{trdeg}_{\kappa(f(x))}\kappa(x).

Proposition 3.1 (Dimension Function and Exact Altitude Formula). The function \delta_{X/S} is a dimension function on X: if y is an immediate specialization of x, then \delta_{X/S}(x)=\delta_{X/S}(y)+1. More explicitly, suppose that X is integral with generic point \eta, that s_0=f(\eta), and set t_X:=\operatorname{trdeg}_{\kappa(s_0)}K(X). For x\in X, put s=f(x) and r_x:=\operatorname{trdeg}_{\kappa(s)}\kappa(x). Then \begin{equation} {\dim\mathcal{O}_{X,x} =\operatorname{codim}_{\overline{\{s_0\}}} \overline{\{s\}}+t_X-r_x.} \tag{*} \end{equation} Equivalently, \operatorname{codim}_X\overline{\{x\}} =\delta_{X/S}(\eta)-\delta_{X/S}(x).

Proof. The closed integral subscheme B_0=\overline{\{s_0\}} is the spectrum of a quotient of the universally catenary ring S, hence is universally catenary. The morphism X\to B factors dominantly through B_0. Applying the dimension formula for a finite type dominant morphism of integral schemes gives \dim\mathcal{O}_{X,x} =\dim\mathcal{O}_{B_0,s}+t_X-r_x. The first local dimension is the codimension of \overline{\{s\}} in B_0, which proves (*). The dimension-function assertion is the same equality applied to an immediate specialization. ◻

This formula is the principal replacement for all arguments that previously used Jacobson closed points. It computes codimension exactly from the height traversed in the base and the loss of residue-field transcendence degree.

Global Dimension and the Base-Access Defect

The generic relative transcendence degree does not by itself determine the global Krull dimension once no hypothesis forces the scheme to meet the deepest strata of the closure of its generic image. The missing quantity records how much of that base dimension is actually reached by points of the scheme.

Definition 3.7 (Base-Access Index and Defect). Let Z be an integral standard scheme, let g\colon Z\to B, let \eta_Z be its generic point, and put b_Z=g(\eta_Z) and r_Z=\operatorname{trdeg}_{\kappa(b_Z)}K(Z). Define the base-access index a_S(Z):= \max_{z\in Z} \left\{ \operatorname{codim}_{\overline{\{b_Z\}}} \overline{\{g(z)\}} -\operatorname{trdeg}_{\kappa(g(z))}\kappa(z) \right\}. Define also the base-access defect \varepsilon_S(Z):= \dim\overline{\{b_Z\}}-a_S(Z)\ge 0. In fact, 0\le a_S(Z)\le\dim\overline{\{b_Z\}}. The upper bound is immediate from the definition. For the lower bound, choose a point of some non-empty fiber of Z\to B that is closed in that fiber; its residue field is algebraic over the residue field of the base point, so the corresponding term in the maximum is non-negative.

Proposition 3.1 (Fundamental Dimension Formulas). Let X be an integral standard scheme over S.

  1. Exact global formula: {\dim X=t_X+a_S(X) =t_X+\dim\overline{\{s_0\}}-\varepsilon_S(X).}

  2. Let x\in X and Z=\overline{\{x\}} with its reduced structure. Define the chain defect at x by \chi_X(x):= \dim X-\dim\mathcal{O}_{X,x}-\dim Z. Then \chi_X(x)\ge0 and {\dim\mathcal{O}_{X,x}+\dim\overline{\{x\}} =\dim X-\chi_X(x).} Moreover, writing s=f(x), {\chi_X(x)=a_S(X)- \operatorname{codim}_{\overline{\{s_0\}}}\overline{\{s\}} -a_S(Z).}

  3. For every irreducible closed subscheme Y\subseteq X, with generic point y, {\dim Y+\operatorname{codim}_X Y =\dim X-\chi_X(y).}

Proof. By Proposition , for every z\in X, \dim\mathcal{O}_{X,z}=t_X+ \operatorname{codim}_{\overline{\{s_0\}}}\overline{\{f(z)\}} -\operatorname{trdeg}_{\kappa(f(z))}\kappa(z). Taking the maximum over z and using Proposition  gives (1). For (2), concatenate a maximal chain from X to Z with a maximal chain inside Z; this proves \chi_X(x)\ge0. Applying (1) to X and to Z, and using the exact altitude formula for \dim\mathcal{O}_{X,x}, gives the displayed expression for \chi_X(x). Part (3) is (2) at the generic point of Y. ◻

Proposition 3.1 (Componentwise Dimension Profile). Let f\colon X\to B=\operatorname{Spec}S be a standard scheme, and let X_1,\ldots,X_m be its irreducible components with their reduced induced structures. Write \eta_i for the generic point of X_i, s_i=f(\eta_i), and t_i:=\operatorname{trdeg}_{\kappa(s_i)}\kappa(\eta_i). For x\in X, put s=f(x) and I(x):=\{i\mid x\in X_i\}. Then

  1. the local dimension is the maximum of the branch dimensions: {\dim\mathcal{O}_{X,x} =\max_{i\in I(x)} \left\{ \operatorname{codim}_{\overline{\{s_i\}}}\overline{\{s\}} +t_i-\operatorname{trdeg}_{\kappa(s)}\kappa(x) \right\};}

  2. the global dimension is the maximum of the component profiles: {\dim X=\max_{1\le i\le m} \left(t_i+a_S(X_i)\right);}

  3. if Z=\overline{\{x\}} carries its reduced structure, then {\dim Z= \operatorname{trdeg}_{\kappa(s)}\kappa(x)+a_S(Z).}

Nilpotent structure affects none of these quantities. At a point lying on several components, however, the local dimension is governed by the largest branch, so one must not replace the maximum in (1) by a formula involving only \dim X.

Proof. Passing to X_{\mathrm{red}} does not change the underlying prime chains or any local Krull dimension. In the Noetherian local ring \mathcal{O}_{X,x}, the minimal primes correspond to the irreducible components X_i containing x, and \dim\mathcal{O}_{X,x} =\max_{i\in I(x)}\dim\mathcal{O}_{X_i,x}. Applying Proposition  to each integral branch proves (1). Since a Noetherian scheme has finitely many irreducible components and its dimension is the maximum of their dimensions, (2) follows by applying Proposition (1) to every X_i. Finally, Z is integral and its generic point is x, so (3) is the same formula applied to Z. ◻

Definition 3.8 (Chain-Pure Integral Scheme). An integral Noetherian finite-dimensional scheme X is called chain-pure if \chi_X(x)=0 for every x\in X. Equivalently, every irreducible closed subset satisfies \dim Y+\operatorname{codim}_X Y=\dim X.

Remark 3.1 (When the Classical Formulas Return). Integral standard varieties are chain-pure. Hence, over a field, \dim X=\operatorname{trdeg}_kK(X),\qquad \dim\mathcal{O}_{X,x}+\dim\overline{\{x\}}=\dim X. These conclusions come from the special dimension theory of finite type algebras over a field, not from the definition of a standard scheme. More generally, the same formulas hold on any chain-pure standard scheme.

Example 3.1 (A Base-Access Defect over a DVR). Let (S,\pi) be a discrete valuation ring, let K=\operatorname{Frac}(S), and consider the standard open subscheme X=D(\pi)=\operatorname{Spec}K\longrightarrow\operatorname{Spec}S. The generic image is the generic point of \operatorname{Spec}S, and t_X=0. The only point of X also maps to that generic point, so a_S(X)=0. Consequently \dim X=0+0=0, \qquad \varepsilon_S(X)=\dim S-a_S(X)=1. Thus the former formula \dim X=t_X+\dim S fails by exactly one, and the defect \varepsilon_S(X) records the entire failure. Geometrically, X misses the codimension-one closed point of the base.

Example 3.1 (A Positive Chain Defect on a Regular Semilocal Base). Let A=k[u,v], let \mathfrak p=(u),\qquad \mathfrak q=(u-1,v),\qquad T=A\setminus(\mathfrak p\cup\mathfrak q), and put S=T^{-1}A. The primes \mathfrak p and \mathfrak q are incomparable, and prime avoidance shows that the maximal ideals of S are precisely \mathfrak pS and \mathfrak qS. Thus S is a regular Noetherian integral domain of dimension two, with closed points of heights one and two. Take X=\operatorname{Spec}S as a standard scheme over itself.

Let x be the closed point defined by \mathfrak pS. Then \dim X=2, \qquad \dim\mathcal{O}_{X,x}=1, \qquad \dim\overline{\{x\}}=0, and hence {\chi_X(x)=1.} At the other closed point x' defined by \mathfrak qS, one has \dim\mathcal{O}_{X,x'}=2 and therefore \chi_X(x')=0.

This example separates the two correction terms. Since the identity morphism reaches the height-two point, a_S(X)=2 and \varepsilon_S(X)=0; nevertheless, the height-one closed point has positive chain defect. Thus the base-access defect measures failure of the global dimension formula, whereas \chi_X(x) measures failure of local and closure dimensions to complement each other at a specified point.

Krull’s Principal Ideal Theorem for One Equation

Theorem 3.1 (Krull’s Principal Ideal Theorem (Algebraic)). Let A be a Noetherian ring and let f\in A be a non-unit non-zero-divisor. If \mathfrak p is minimal over (f), then \operatorname{ht}(\mathfrak p)=1.

Proposition 3.1 (Geometric Krull’s PIT with the Exact Defect). Let X be an integral standard scheme and let 0\ne f\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal{O}_X) be a non-unit. For every irreducible component Y of V(f), with generic point y, one has \operatorname{codim}_X Y=1 and the exact global dimension formula is {\dim Y=\dim X-1-\chi_X(y).} In particular, one equation cuts the global dimension by exactly one precisely when \chi_X(y)=0; this holds, for example, when X is chain-pure, and hence for integral standard varieties.

Proof. Choose an affine neighbourhood U=\operatorname{Spec}A of y. Since X is integral, A is a Noetherian domain, and the prime corresponding to y is minimal over the restriction of (f). Krull’s principal ideal theorem gives height one, which is exactly \operatorname{codim}_X Y. Proposition (3) then gives the displayed equality. ◻

Krull’s Height Theorem for Several Equations

Theorem 3.1 (Krull’s Height Theorem (Algebraic)). Let A be a Noetherian ring and let I=(f_1,\ldots,f_r). If \mathfrak p is minimal over I, then \operatorname{ht}(\mathfrak p)\le r.

Proposition 3.1 (Geometric Krull Height Formula with Two Exact Corrections). Let X be an integral standard scheme, let f_1,\ldots,f_r be global regular functions, and let Y be an irreducible component of V(f_1,\ldots,f_r). Let y be the generic point of Y, let s_0 be the image of the generic point of X, and let s be the image of y. Put t_X=\operatorname{trdeg}_{\kappa(s_0)}K(X), \qquad r_y=\operatorname{trdeg}_{\kappa(s)}\kappa(y). Then c_Y:=\operatorname{codim}_X Y =\operatorname{codim}_{\overline{\{s_0\}}}\overline{\{s\}} +t_X-r_y\le r. Define the equation-redundancy index along Y by \rho_Y:=r-c_Y\ge0. Then one has the two exact identities {\dim Y=\dim X-c_Y-\chi_X(y)} and {\dim Y-(\dim X-r)=\rho_Y-\chi_X(y).} Consequently, the classical lower bound \dim Y\ge\dim X-r holds along Y if and only if \chi_X(y)\le\rho_Y. It fails by exactly \chi_X(y)-\rho_Y when this number is positive. In particular:

  1. if the equations have the expected height c_Y=r, then the entire dimension loss beyond r is exactly the chain defect \chi_X(y);

  2. if X is chain-pure, then the classical lower bound always holds, and its surplus is exactly the redundancy index \rho_Y.

Proof. On an affine neighbourhood of y, the corresponding prime is minimal over an ideal generated by r elements, so c_Y\le r by Krull’s height theorem. The displayed expression for c_Y is the exact altitude formula from Proposition . The first dimension identity follows from Proposition (3). Subtracting \dim X-r and using \rho_Y=r-c_Y gives \dim Y-(\dim X-r) =r-c_Y-\chi_X(y)=\rho_Y-\chi_X(y), from which all remaining assertions follow. ◻

Example 3.1 (A Corrected Arithmetic Example). Let X=\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb Z[x,y,z],\qquad \dim X=4, and let I=(xy,xz). Its minimal primes are (x) and (y,z). Hence Y_1=V(x),\qquad \dim Y_1=\dim\mathbb Z[y,z]=3, \qquad \operatorname{codim}_X Y_1=1, while Y_2=V(y,z),\qquad \dim Y_2=\dim\mathbb Z[x]=2, \qquad \operatorname{codim}_X Y_2=2. Here X is chain-pure, so the chain defects vanish. The two equations are redundant on Y_1 but have the expected height on Y_2. Notice that the ambient dimension is 4, not 3: the arithmetic base contributes one dimension.

Dimension of a Point

Definition 3.9 (Dimension of a Point). For a point x of a scheme X, define \dim(x):=\dim\overline{\{x\}}, where the closure carries its reduced induced structure.

Proposition 3.1 (Dimension and Prime Ideals). Let X=\operatorname{Spec}A and let x correspond to \mathfrak p\in\operatorname{Spec}A.

  1. Always, \dim(x)=\dim(A/\mathfrak p).

  2. If X is integral of dimension n, then {\dim(x)=n-\operatorname{ht}(\mathfrak p)-\chi_X(x).}

  3. If X is an integral standard scheme over S, s=f(x), and Z=\overline{\{x\}}, then {\dim(x)= \operatorname{trdeg}_{\kappa(s)}\kappa(x)+a_S(Z).}

Proof. The first assertion is the identification \overline{\{x\}}=\operatorname{Spec}(A/\mathfrak p). The second is the definition of the chain defect together with \dim\mathcal{O}_{X,x}=\operatorname{ht}(\mathfrak p). The third is Proposition (1) applied to Z. ◻

Density of Dimension Strata

For d\ge0, put X_{(d)}:=\{x\in X\mid\dim(x)=d\}. After the Jacobson hypothesis is removed, standardness alone gives no density statement for these strata.

Definition 3.10 (The d-Dimensional Avoidance Locus). Define J_d(X):=X\setminus\overline{X_{(d)}}. This is the largest open subset of X containing no d-dimensional point.

Proposition 3.1 (Density of d-Dimensional Points). Let X be an integral standard scheme of dimension n.

  1. The stratum X_{(d)} is dense if and only if J_d(X)=\varnothing.

  2. No such density is automatic for standard schemes. If S is a DVR and X=\operatorname{Spec}S, then X_{(0)} is the unique closed point and is not dense.

  3. If X is an integral standard variety over a field, then J_d(X)=\varnothing\qquad(0\le d\le n).

  4. More generally, the same conclusion holds when X is chain-pure and its closed points are dense.

Proof. The first assertion is the definition. The DVR example proves (2). For (4), let U\subseteq X be non-empty and choose a closed point u\in U. Chain-purity gives a saturated chain from the generic point to u of length n, and the generic points of the intermediate irreducible closed subsets have dimensions n,n-1,\ldots,0. Since open sets are stable under generalization, all these points belong to U. Thus U meets every X_{(d)}. Part (3) is the special case in which X is finite type over a field: closed points are dense and the standard dimension formula over a field makes X chain-pure. ◻

Topological Filtration

Definition 3.11 (Topological Filtration by Dimension). Let X be a standard scheme of dimension n. Define X_{\le d}:=\{x\in X\mid\dim(x)\le d\} =\bigcup_{i=0}^dX_{(i)}. Then X_{\le0}\subseteq X_{\le1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq X_{\le n}=X. Some inclusions may be equal when the corresponding stratum is empty.

Proposition 3.1 (Exact Dimension Formula in a Fiber Product). Let X and Y be standard varieties over an arbitrary field k. For z\in X\times_kY, let x and y be its projections and define the transcendence overlap \omega_k(z;x,y):= \operatorname{trdeg}_k\kappa(x)+ \operatorname{trdeg}_k\kappa(y)- \operatorname{trdeg}_k\kappa(z). Then \omega_k(z;x,y)\ge0 and {\dim(z)=\dim(x)+\dim(y)-\omega_k(z;x,y).} Consequently, \bigcup_{i+j\le d}X_{\le i}\times Y_{\le j} \subseteq (X\times_kY)_{\le d}, and this inclusion may be strict. For d=0 one has the exact equality (X\times_kY)_{\le0}=X_{\le0}\times Y_{\le0}.

Proof. The field \kappa(z) is the fraction field of a prime quotient of \kappa(x)\otimes_k\kappa(y), hence is generated by the images of the two residue fields. This proves non-negativity of \omega_k and the displayed equality. The inclusion of filtrations follows immediately.

If z is closed, then \kappa(z) is finite over k. The injections \kappa(x),\kappa(y)\hookrightarrow\kappa(z) show that x and y are closed. Conversely, if x and y are closed, then \kappa(x)\otimes_k\kappa(y) is finite-dimensional over k, so every residue field of a prime quotient is finite over k and every point above (x,y) is closed. This proves the equality for d=0. ◻

Example 3.1 (The Diagonal Measures the Overlap). For X=Y=\mathbb A^1_k, let z be the generic point of the diagonal. Both projections are the generic point of \mathbb A^1_k, so their dimensions are one, while \dim(z)=1. Hence \omega_k(z;x,y)=1+1-1=1. This single unit of transcendence overlap explains exactly why z belongs to (X\times_kY)_{\le1} but not to \bigcup_{i+j\le1}X_{\le i}\times Y_{\le j}.

Remark 3.1 (Arithmetic Dimension Strata). In X=\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb Z[t], one has \dim X=2. The generic point has dimension two. Height-one primes give one-dimensional points: primes (p) are generic points of vertical fibers, while primitive irreducible polynomials define horizontal curves. Maximal ideals (p,\overline f(t)) give zero-dimensional closed points. In this particular arithmetic example the chain defect vanishes, but this is an additional feature of \mathbb Z[t], not a consequence of the new definition alone.

Topology within a Dimension Stratum

Proposition 3.1 (Topology within a Dimension Stratum). Let X be a standard scheme. Each stratum X_{(d)}, with its subspace topology, is a T_1 space.

Proof. If y\in\overline{\{x\}} and y\ne x, then \overline{\{y\}}\subsetneq\overline{\{x\}}. Both are finite-dimensional irreducible closed subsets, so \dim\overline{\{y\}}<\dim\overline{\{x\}}. Thus no distinct specialization of x lies in the same dimension stratum. ◻

Remark 3.1 (Point Dimension Does Not Determine Stalk Dimension). If X is integral of dimension n and x\in X_{(d)}, then the correct formula is {\dim\mathcal{O}_{X,x}=n-d-\chi_X(x).} Hence the topological filtration by d agrees with a reverse filtration by local Krull dimension exactly on the chain-pure locus \{x\mid\chi_X(x)=0\}. After the Jacobson condition is removed, the former identity \dim\mathcal{O}_{X,x}=n-d must not be used without this additional hypothesis.

Proposition 3.1 (When Skeletons Fail to be Locally Closed). Let X be an integral standard scheme of dimension n>0.

  1. If X_{(0)} is dense, then X_{\le d} is dense and not locally closed for every d<n.

  2. If X_{(d)} is dense and d<n, then X_{(d)} is not locally closed.

  3. In particular, both statements apply to integral standard varieties.

Without the density hypothesis the conclusion is false: for a DVR, X_{\le0} is its closed point and is a closed subscheme.

Proof. A dense locally closed subset is open in its closure, hence open in X. Every non-empty open subset of the irreducible space X contains the generic point, whose dimension is n. Therefore neither X_{\le d} nor X_{(d)} can be open when d<n. The final example is immediate. ◻

Behaves of Dimension under Morphisms

The topological properties of schemes, combined with the sobriety of their underlying spaces (where every irreducible closed subset has a unique generic point), allow us to track how dimensions behave under various well-behaved morphisms.

Proposition 3.1 (Dimension Bounds under Morphisms). Let X and Y be standard schemes over S, and let f \colon X \to Y be a morphism of schemes.

  1. Injective Morphisms: If f is topologically injective, then \dim X \le \dim Y.

  2. Open Morphisms: If f is an open morphism (i.e., the image of every open set is open), then \dim X \ge \dim f(X), where f(X) is endowed with the subspace topology.

  3. Closed Surjective Morphisms: If f is closed and surjective, then \dim X \ge \dim Y.

  4. Isomorphisms via Dimension: If f is a closed immersion, X is an integral scheme, Y is an integral scheme, and \dim X = \dim Y, then f is an isomorphism.

Proof. We leverage the correspondence between strict chains of irreducible closed subsets and strict sequences of specializations of points. A sequence x_0 \rightsquigarrow x_1 \rightsquigarrow \cdots \rightsquigarrow x_n is a strict specialization chain if x_i \in \overline{\{x_{i+1}\}} and x_i \neq x_{i+1}.

(1) Assume f is injective, and let x_0 \rightsquigarrow x_1 \rightsquigarrow \cdots \rightsquigarrow x_n be a strict specialization chain of length n in X. By the continuity of f, we have f(\overline{\{x_{i+1}\}}) \subseteq \overline{\{f(x_{i+1})\}}. Therefore, x_i \in \overline{\{x_{i+1}\}} implies f(x_i) \in \overline{\{f(x_{i+1})\}}. Because f is strictly injective and x_i \neq x_{i+1}, it forces f(x_i) \neq f(x_{i+1}). Thus, the image yields a strict specialization chain f(x_0) \rightsquigarrow f(x_1) \rightsquigarrow \cdots \rightsquigarrow f(x_n) of length n in Y. Consequently, any chain in X injects into a chain in Y, yielding \dim X \le \dim Y.

(2) Assume f is open. A fundamental topological property of open continuous maps between sober spaces is that they satisfy the “Going-Down” theorem for specializations. Let y_0 \rightsquigarrow y_1 \rightsquigarrow \cdots \rightsquigarrow y_n be a strict chain of specializations in the subspace f(X). If y_n = f(x_n) for some x_n \in X, the openness of f guarantees that the specialization y_{n-1} \in \overline{\{y_n\}} can be lifted: there exists x_{n-1} \in \overline{\{x_n\}} such that f(x_{n-1}) = y_{n-1}. Inductively, the entire chain in f(X) lifts to a strict chain of the same length in X. Taking the supremum over all such chains gives \dim X \ge \dim f(X).

(3) Assume f is closed and surjective. Let Z_0 \subsetneq Z_1 \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq Z_n be a strict chain of irreducible closed subsets in Y. Because f is surjective and closed, the preimage f^{-1}(Z_n) is a closed subset of X that surjects onto Z_n. By Noetherianity, we can select an irreducible component W_n \subseteq f^{-1}(Z_n) that still surjects onto Z_n. The restriction f|_{W_n} \colon W_n \to Z_n remains closed and surjective. We can then find an irreducible component W_{n-1} \subseteq f^{-1}(Z_{n-1}) \cap W_n surjecting onto Z_{n-1}. Proceeding inductively, we construct a strict chain of irreducible closed subsets W_0 \subsetneq W_1 \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq W_n in X. Thus, \dim X \ge \dim Y.

(4) Assume f \colon X \hookrightarrow Y is a closed immersion between two integral standard schemes of the same dimension n. Because X is a closed subscheme, its topological image is exactly V(\mathcal{I}) for some quasi-coherent ideal sheaf \mathcal{I} \subseteq \mathcal{O}_Y. Since Y is an integral standard scheme, it is catenary; therefore, any proper closed subset of Y must strictly omit the generic point \eta_Y and thus has dimension strictly less than n = \dim Y. Because \dim X = n, its topological image cannot be a proper subset, meaning topologically X \cong Y.

Algebraically, this implies the ideal sheaf \mathcal{I} is supported nowhere, meaning its sections are nilpotent. However, Y is integral and therefore geometrically reduced (its nilradical is zero). Thus, \mathcal{I} = 0, meaning the closed immersion f is an exact isomorphism of schemes. ◻

Remark 3.1 (The Necessity of an Integral Target). In the fourth statement of Proposition , it is a common geometric oversight to assume that only the source space X needs to be integral. If we drop the integrality requirement on Y, the statement becomes false.

For example, let Y = \operatorname{Spec}k[x, y, z] / (xy, xz) be the union of the yoz-plane and the x-axis, which has global dimension 2. Let X = \operatorname{Spec}k[y, z] be the yoz-plane itself. The natural inclusion X \hookrightarrow Y is a closed immersion, X is integral, and \dim X = 2 = \dim Y. However, X is clearly not isomorphic to Y, as it fails to capture the x-axis component. By demanding that Y is also integral, we ensure the ambient space cannot hide distinct components of identical or smaller dimensions.

Zero-Dimensional Schemes

We now examine standard schemes of dimension zero. Because our standard schemes are inherently Noetherian spaces (Note ), vanishing dimension forces the topological space to be perfectly discrete and finite, while algebraically reducing the structure to the classical theory of Artinian rings.

Proposition 3.1 (Characterizations of Zero-dimensional Schemes). Let X be a standard scheme over S. The following conditions are strictly equivalent:

  1. The dimension of the scheme is zero: \dim X = 0.

  2. Every point x \in X is a closed point.

  3. The underlying topological space of X is discrete.

  4. The underlying topological space of X is discrete and finite.

  5. The underlying topological space of X is Hausdorff.

  6. For every point x \in X, the local stalk ring \mathcal{O}_{X,x} is an Artinian ring.

  7. The scheme X is isomorphic to the finite disjoint union of the spectra of its local rings: X \cong \coprod_{x \in X} \operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{O}_{X,x}

Furthermore, if S = k is a field (so X is a zero-dimensional standard variety), these conditions are equivalent to the statement that the global section ring \Gamma(X, \mathcal{O}_X) is a finite-dimensional vector space over k.

Proof. We will establish a cycle of implications leveraging the Noetherian property of X.

(1) \implies (2): If \dim X = 0, the supremum of the lengths of strict chains of irreducible closed subsets is zero. For any x \in X, its topological closure \overline{\{x\}} is an irreducible closed subset. If it contained any other distinct point y, we would have a strict inclusion \overline{\{y\}} \subsetneq \overline{\{x\}}, forming a chain of length at least 1, which contradicts \dim X = 0. Thus, \overline{\{x\}} = \{x\}, meaning every point is closed.

(2) \implies (4): Assume every point in X is closed. Because X is a Noetherian topological space, it can be expressed uniquely as a finite union of irreducible closed components. The only irreducible closed subsets in a space where every point is closed are the singleton points themselves. Therefore, X must be a finite set of points. Because every subset of a finite space of closed points is a finite union of closed sets, every subset is closed. Thus, every subset is also open, meaning the topology is finite and discrete.

(4) \implies (3) \implies (5): A finite discrete space is trivially discrete. Furthermore, any discrete topology is totally separated, hence strictly Hausdorff.

(5) \implies (1): If X is Hausdorff, every irreducible closed subset must consist of exactly one point. (If an irreducible subset contained two distinct points, they could be separated by disjoint open sets, immediately breaking the irreducibility). Since all irreducible closed subsets are points, there can be no strict chains of inclusions among them. Thus, \dim X = 0.

(1) \iff (6): By Proposition , the global dimension of X is the maximum of the Krull dimensions of its local rings \mathcal{O}_{X,x}. Thus, \dim X = 0 if and only if \dim \mathcal{O}_{X,x} = 0 for all x. A fundamental theorem of commutative algebra dictates that a Noetherian local ring has Krull dimension zero if and only if it is an Artinian ring.

(4) and (6) \implies (7): By (4), X is a finite discrete set of points. We can trivially cover X by disjoint open neighborhoods, each containing exactly one point. An open subset of a standard scheme is itself a standard scheme, and a scheme consisting of a single point x with a local ring \mathcal{O}_{X,x} is canonically isomorphic to the affine scheme \operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{O}_{X,x}. Because X is the finite disjoint union of these open singletons, it is exactly the categorical coproduct: X \cong \coprod_{x \in X} \operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{O}_{X,x} Since any finite disjoint union of affine schemes is affine, X is isomorphic to \operatorname{Spec}(\prod_{x \in X} \mathcal{O}_{X,x}).

(7) \implies (1): If X \cong \coprod \operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{O}_{X,x}, the space is a disjoint union of prime spectra of local rings. Since the spectra correspond to the points of X, each local ring must have exactly one prime ideal (its maximal ideal), meaning \dim \mathcal{O}_{X,x} = 0. Thus, \dim X = 0.

The Standard Variety Case: Finally, assume S = k is a field, meaning X is a standard variety over k. By the above equivalences, X is an affine scheme X = \operatorname{Spec}A where A = \Gamma(X, \mathcal{O}_X) is an Artinian ring. By our global convention, A is a finitely generated k-algebra. An Artinian ring that is a finitely generated algebra over a field is necessarily a finite-dimensional vector space over that field. Conversely, if A is a finite-dimensional k-vector space, it satisfies the descending chain condition on ideals, making it an Artinian ring, which immediately forces \dim X = 0. ◻

Remark 3.1 (Algebraic Translation). Because the scheme-theoretic disjoint union (coproduct) corresponds to the direct product in the category of rings, the geometric isomorphism in condition (5) translates to a purely algebraic statement for affine schemes. If X = \operatorname{Spec}A is a Noetherian affine scheme of dimension zero, the proposition implies that the ring A uniquely decomposes as a finite direct product of Artinian local rings: A \cong \prod_{i=1}^n \mathcal{O}_{X,x_i} This is exactly the classical structure theorem for Artinian rings.

Geometric Properties of Standard Varieties

When studying standard varieties over a field k, a recurring complication is that a topological or algebraic property might hold for a variety X over k, but fail catastrophically when we extend the base field. For instance, the scheme \operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1)) is a single closed point over \mathbb{R}, hence trivially irreducible and connected. However, upon extending the scalars to \mathbb{C}, the polynomial splits, and the scheme becomes two distinct disjoint points.

To isolate the intrinsic geometric nature of a space from the arithmetic quirks of its field of definition, Grothendieck introduced the prefix “geometrically.” A property holds geometrically if it survives base change to the algebraic closure.

Definition 3.12 (Geometric Properties). Let X be a standard variety over a field k, and let \bar{k} denote a fixed algebraic closure of k. Let \mathbf{P} be a property of schemes. We say that X is geometrically \mathbf{P} over k if the base changed scheme X_{\bar{k}} = X \times_{\operatorname{Spec}k} \operatorname{Spec}\bar{k} satisfies property \mathcal{P}.

Specifically, we highlight the following five foundational geometric properties:

  1. Geometrically Connected: X_{\bar{k}} is a connected topological space.

  2. Geometrically Irreducible: X_{\bar{k}} is an irreducible topological space.

  3. Geometrically Reduced: The structure sheaf of X_{\bar{k}} has no non-zero nilpotent sections.

  4. Geometrically Integral: X_{\bar{k}} is both irreducible and reduced (i.e., it is an integral scheme).

  5. Geometrically Regular: All local stalk rings of X_{\bar{k}} are regular local rings. (In the category of varieties, a geometrically regular variety is often called a smooth variety over k).

  6. Geometrically Normal: X_{\bar{k}} is a normal scheme (i.e., all local stalk rings of X_{\bar{k}} are integrally closed domains).

Remark 3.1 (Dependence on the Base Field). It is crucial to emphasize that being “geometrically \mathbf{P}” is a relative property depending strictly on the structure morphism X \to \operatorname{Spec}k. A scheme might be geometrically integral over one field but not over another. For example, L = \mathbb{C} is a geometrically integral variety when viewed over k = \mathbb{C}, but it is not geometrically integral when viewed as a variety over k = \mathbb{R}, because \mathbb{C} \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C}, which is not an integral domain.

The true power of defining these properties via the algebraic closure is that they become unconditionally absolute. If a property holds over the algebraic closure, it holds over any field extension whatsoever.

Proposition 3.1 (Absolute Nature of Geometric Properties). Let X be a standard variety over a field k, and let \mathbf{P} represent any of the five properties: connected, irreducible, reduced, integral, or regular. The following conditions are strictly equivalent:

  1. X is geometrically \mathbf{P} over k.

  2. For every field extension K/k, the base changed variety X_K = X \times_{\operatorname{Spec}k} \operatorname{Spec}K satisfies property \mathcal{P}.

  3. There exists some algebraically closed field extension \Omega/k such that X_\Omega = X \times_{\operatorname{Spec}k} \operatorname{Spec}\Omega satisfies property \mathcal{P}.

Proof. The implication (2) \implies (1) is trivial by setting K = \bar{k}. The implication (1) \implies (3) is equally trivial by setting \Omega = \bar{k}. The substantial mathematical content lies in proving that (3) implies (2).

Assume X_\Omega has property \mathcal{P} for some algebraically closed extension \Omega/k. Let K/k be any arbitrary field extension. We can always construct a composite field L that contains both K and \Omega.

The proof relies on two descent steps utilizing the morphisms X_L \to X_K and X_L \to X_\Omega:

  • Ascent from \Omega to L: The extension L/\Omega is an extension of an algebraically closed field. Because \Omega is algebraically closed, any standard variety over \Omega inherently possesses the same geometric and topological structure regardless of further field extensions (algebraically, \Omega has no non-trivial algebraic extensions, so tensoring with L only adds transcendental variables which preserve connectivity, irreducibility, reducedness, and regularity). Thus, because X_\Omega has \mathbf{P}, X_L also has \mathbf{P}.

  • Descent from L to K: The canonical projection X_L \to X_K is universally open, universally faithfully flat, and surjective. If X_L is connected, irreducible, reduced, or regular, these properties descend faithfully along faithfully flat surjective morphisms. Therefore, X_K must also possess property \mathcal{P}.

This completes the cycle of equivalences, demonstrating that geometric properties are immune to any further arithmetic meddling. ◻

Corollary 3.1 (Geometric implies Absolute). If X is a geometrically \mathbf{P} variety over k, then X itself satisfies property \mathcal{P}.

Proof. This is an immediate consequence of Proposition by taking the trivial field extension K = k, which yields X_k = X \times_{\operatorname{Spec}k} \operatorname{Spec}k \cong X. ◻

The objective of this section is to examine a classical topic in algebraic geometry: how an algebraic variety defined over a non-algebraically closed base field behaves under field extensions. This question arises naturally, as illustrated by the example X = \operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1)) over the real numbers \mathbb{R}, which is a single irreducible closed point over \mathbb{R} yet decomposes into two distinct closed points after base change to the complex numbers \mathbb{C}.

To formalize such phenomena, we must introduce several intrinsic geometric properties. Consistent with our earlier treatment of separatedness, we only discuss absolute properties for schemes over a field here; the relative properties for general morphisms will be systematically investigated in later parts of this text.

Definition 3.13 (Absolute Properties of Schemes over a Field). Let X be a scheme over a field k, equipped with the canonical structure morphism \pi \colon X \to \operatorname{Spec}k. We define the following absolute properties for X:

  1. Flatness: The scheme X is said to be flat over k if for every point x \in X, the local stalk \mathcal{O}_{X,x} is a flat k-module.

    (Remark: Since every module over a field is a vector space, and vector spaces are inherently free and therefore flat, every scheme over a field k is trivially flat over k. However, formulating this absolute definition is a conceptual prerequisite for understanding field extensions via flat base change.)

  2. Properness: The scheme X is proper over k if the structure morphism \pi is separated, of finite type, and universally closed. The universally closed condition dictates that for any other scheme Y over k, the canonical projection from the fiber product X \times_k Y \to Y is a strictly closed map in the Zariski topology.

    (Remark: By our Global Convention, if X is a standard variety over k, it is automatically separated and of finite type over k. Therefore, for standard varieties, properness reduces precisely to being universally closed.)

These absolute properties endow the underlying topological spaces and cohomology groups of our varieties with powerful finiteness constraints.

Proposition 3.1 (Topological and Cohomological Finiteness). Let X be a scheme over a field k. The following profound properties hold unconditionally:

  1. Universally Open: If X is flat and of finite type over k (which is automatically true for any standard variety over k), then the structure morphism X \to \operatorname{Spec}k is universally open. Consequently, for any arbitrary field extension K/k, the canonical projection X \times_k K \to \operatorname{Spec}K is an open map.

  2. Finiteness of Cohomology: If X is a proper scheme over k, then X exhibits finite cohomological dimension. Specifically, for any coherent sheaf \mathcal{F} on X, the cohomology groups H^i(X, \mathcal{F}) are finite-dimensional k-vector spaces, and they vanish completely for all degrees i > \dim X.

Remark 3.1 (Deferred Proofs). The two propositions stated above constitute remarkably heavy algebraic machinery. Proving that flat morphisms of finite presentation are universally open requires a delicate analysis of constructible sets and Chevalley’s Theorem. Proving the finiteness of coherent cohomology for proper schemes demands the full power of derived functors, Čech cohomology techniques, and the theorem on formal functions.

At this preliminary stage, the reader is only asked to accept these two propositions as “black boxes.” They form the necessary topological and homological foundation for studying the dynamics of varieties under field extensions, and we will provide rigorous, step-by-step proofs for both in later chapters dedicated to cohomology and flat descent.

Geometrically Connected

Our previous observation that absolute geometric properties can be checked on any algebraically closed field significantly streamlines our proofs. We now formalize the characterizations of geometric connectedness, leveraging both topological arguments and finite field extensions.

Proposition 3.1 (Characterizations of Geometrically Connected Schemes). Let X be a standard scheme over a field k. The following assertions are strictly equivalent:

  1. X is geometrically connected.

  2. For every connected scheme Y over k, the fiber product X \times_k Y is a connected topological space.

  3. There exists a separably closed field extension \Omega of k such that X_\Omega is connected.

  4. For every finite separable field extension K of k, the base change X_K is connected.

Proof. The implications (2) \implies (1) \implies (3) and (1) \implies (4) are immediate from the definition of geometric connectedness (by testing against specific extensions such as \bar{k} or general k-schemes).

(1) \implies (2): Consider the canonical projection q \colon X \times_k Y \to Y. Because the structure morphism X \to \operatorname{Spec}k is flat (as all modules over a field are flat), base change preserves this, making q a flat morphism of finite presentation. By Proposition , q is universally open. Since it is also surjective, it suffices to show that the fibers of q are connected. For any point y \in Y, the fiber is q^{-1}(y) \cong X \times_k \operatorname{Spec}\kappa(y). Because X is geometrically connected, its base change to any field extension, including \kappa(y), remains connected. Since the target space Y is connected and all fibers of the open surjective map q are connected, the total space X \times_k Y must be connected.

(3) \implies (1): Let K be an arbitrary field extension of k; we must prove X_K is connected. Let L be a field extension containing both K and \Omega (for instance, an algebraic closure of their compositum). The canonical morphism X_L \to X_K is surjective. Since the continuous image of a connected space is connected, it suffices to show that X_L is connected.

Consider the projection p \colon X_L \to X_\Omega. This map is obtained by base-changing the field extension L/\Omega, so p is again flat, open, and surjective. The fibers of p over any point x \in X_\Omega are given by \operatorname{Spec}(\kappa(x) \otimes_\Omega L). Because \Omega is separably closed, it is separably closed in L. A standard result in commutative algebra states that tensoring a field with an extension over a separably closed subfield yields a ring with a connected spectrum. Since X_\Omega is connected by assumption and the fibers of p are connected, X_L is connected.

(4) \implies (3): We utilize the fact that X is a standard scheme, and hence of finite type. Let \Omega be a separable closure of k. Assume for the sake of contradiction that X_\Omega is disconnected, meaning X_\Omega = Y \sqcup Z where Y and Z are closed, non-empty, disjoint subsets.

Because X is of finite type over k, it admits a finite affine covering U_i = \operatorname{Spec}A_i, where each A_i is a finitely generated k-algebra. The subsets Y and Z restrict to closed sets in U_i \otimes_k \Omega. Thus, Y \cap (U_i \otimes_k \Omega) = V(\mathfrak{a}_i) for some ideal \mathfrak{a}_i. Since A_i \otimes_k \Omega is finitely generated, \mathfrak{a}_i is generated by finitely many polynomials. Similarly, the intersection with Z is defined by finitely many elements.

Because there are only finitely many such coefficients across a finite number of affine patches, all the coefficients involved lie in \Omega. Any finite collection of elements in a separable closure generates a finite separable subextension k' of k. Therefore, we can descend the defining ideals to A_i \otimes_k k', allowing us to define closed subschemes Y' and Z' inside X_{k'}. By construction, the base change of X_{k'} = Y' \sqcup Z' to \Omega recovers our original disjoint union. Thus, Y' and Z' must be disjoint and non-empty in X_{k'}. This proves X_{k'} is not connected, which directly contradicts (4) since k' is a finite separable extension. ◻

Proposition 3.1 (Geometric Connectedness and Global Sections). Let X be a standard scheme over k that is proper and connected. Let A = \Gamma(X, \mathcal{O}_X) be the ring of global sections.

  1. The ring A is a local Artinian k-algebra (and thus has a unique maximal ideal \mathfrak{m}).

  2. X is geometrically connected over k if and only if the residue field A/\mathfrak{m} is a purely inseparable field extension of k.

Proof. By Proposition , because X is proper over k, its global section ring A = H^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X) is a finite-dimensional k-vector space. Because X is connected, the structure sheaf has no non-trivial idempotents, which implies A has no non-trivial idempotents. A finite-dimensional k-algebra with no non-trivial idempotents is necessarily a local Artinian ring, proving (1).

For (2), let k' = A/\mathfrak{m} be the residue field, which is a finite extension of k. Base changing to the algebraic closure \bar{k}, the ring of global sections becomes A \otimes_k \bar{k}. The scheme X_{\bar{k}} is connected if and only if A \otimes_k \bar{k} has no non-trivial idempotents.

Because A is a local Artinian ring, its nilradical absorbs the structural complexity, and A \otimes_k \bar{k} has idempotents if and only if the semi-simple quotient k' \otimes_k \bar{k} has idempotents. By the structure of tensor products of fields, k' \otimes_k \bar{k} is a local ring (i.e., has no idempotents) if and only if k'/k is a purely inseparable field extension. Thus, geometric connectedness is exactly equivalent to the pure inseparability of k' over k. ◻

Remark 3.1 (Varieties over Perfect Fields). If the base field k is a perfect field (such as \mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{C}, or finite fields), every algebraic extension is separable. Consequently, the only purely inseparable extension of k is k itself. Therefore, for a proper, connected standard variety over a perfect field, it is geometrically connected if and only if its reduced ring of constants is exactly the base field (i.e., \Gamma(X, \mathcal{O}_X)_{red} = k).

Geometrically Irreducible

Following our analysis of geometric connectedness, we naturally transition to geometric irreducibility. Just as connectedness is governed by the absence of non-trivial idempotents in the field of constants, geometric irreducibility is controlled entirely by the algebraic behavior of the rational function field.

Proposition 3.1 (Characterizations of Geometrically Irreducible Schemes). Let X be a standard scheme over a field k. The following assertions are strictly equivalent:

  1. X is geometrically irreducible over k.

  2. For every irreducible scheme Y over k, the fiber product X \times_k Y is irreducible.

  3. X is irreducible, and if \eta is its unique generic point, then k is separably closed in the rational function field \kappa(\eta).

  4. There exists a separably closed field extension \Omega of k such that the base change X_\Omega is irreducible.

  5. For every finite separable field extension K of k, the base change X_K is irreducible.

Proof. If any of the assertions hold, taking trivial field extensions or Y = \operatorname{Spec}k immediately implies that X itself is irreducible. We may therefore assume X is an irreducible standard scheme, and let \eta be its unique generic point.

For any scheme Z over k, consider the canonical projection p \colon X \times_k Z \to X. Because the structure morphism Z \to \operatorname{Spec}k is flat, its base change p is a flat, open, and surjective morphism. Topologically, the preimage of a dense set under an open surjective map remains dense. Thus, X \times_k Z is irreducible if and only if the fiber over the generic point \eta of X is irreducible. The scheme-theoretic fiber is exactly: p^{-1}(\eta) = \operatorname{Spec}(\kappa(\eta) \otimes_k Z)

If we apply this to the case where Z = \operatorname{Spec}L for a field extension L/k, we conclude that X_L is irreducible if and only if the ring \kappa(\eta) \otimes_k L has a unique minimal prime ideal (i.e., its prime spectrum is an irreducible topological space).

We must show that \kappa(\eta) \otimes_k L has a unique minimal prime ideal for all field extensions L if and only if k is separably closed in \kappa(\eta).

Suppose k is not separably closed in \kappa(\eta). Then there exists a non-trivial finite separable subextension k' \subseteq \kappa(\eta). By the primitive element theorem, k' \cong k[x]/(f(x)) for some irreducible separable polynomial f \in k[x]. If we choose L to be a field extension over which f splits completely (such as a separable closure of k), then f(x) factors into distinct linear terms in L[x]. Consequently: k' \otimes_k L \cong L[x]/(f(x)) \cong L \times \dots \times L This means k' \otimes_k L contains non-trivial idempotents, and therefore \kappa(\eta) \otimes_k L also contains non-trivial idempotents. A ring with non-trivial idempotents has a disconnected spectrum, so it strictly fails to be irreducible.

Conversely, if k is separably closed in \kappa(\eta), then \kappa(\eta)/k is a primary field extension. A fundamental result in commutative algebra dictates that tensoring a primary field extension with any field L preserves the topological irreducibility of the spectrum (the resulting ring may have non-zero nilpotents, but it possesses a unique minimal prime ideal). Because the failure of irreducibility is driven entirely by separable algebraic subextensions, testing against finite separable extensions (or a single separable closure \Omega) is mathematically sufficient. This rigorously establishes the equivalence of (1), (3), (4), and (5).

Finally, to show that (3) implies (2), let Y be an arbitrary irreducible scheme over k with unique generic point \theta. Applying our earlier topological observation to the canonical projection q \colon \operatorname{Spec}\kappa(\eta) \times_k Y \to Y, we see that this fiber product is irreducible if and only if its generic fiber over \theta is irreducible. This generic fiber is exactly: \operatorname{Spec}(\kappa(\eta) \otimes_k \kappa(\theta)) Since k is separably closed in \kappa(\eta), this tensor product of fields has a unique minimal prime ideal, meaning its spectrum is rigorously irreducible. ◻

Geometrically Reduced

We complete our triad of foundational geometric properties by examining geometrically reduced schemes. While geometric connectedness and irreducibility are tested against separable extensions, geometric reducedness is orthogonally tested against purely inseparable extensions.

Proposition 3.1 (Characterizations of Geometrically Reduced Schemes). Let X be a standard scheme over a field k. The following assertions are strictly equivalent:

  1. X is geometrically reduced over k.

  2. For every reduced scheme Y over k, the fiber product X \times_k Y is reduced.

  3. X is reduced, and for every generic point \eta of an irreducible component of X, the residue field \kappa(\eta) is a separable field extension of k.

  4. There exists a perfect field extension \Omega of k such that the base change X_\Omega is reduced.

  5. For every finite purely inseparable field extension K of k, the base change X_K is reduced.

Proof. Because being reduced is fundamentally a local property, we may assume without loss of generality that X = \operatorname{Spec}A is an affine standard scheme, where A is a finitely generated k-algebra. If any of the assertions hold, taking Y = \operatorname{Spec}k or K=k trivially implies X is reduced, so we may assume A itself is a reduced ring.

Let \{\eta_i\}_{i \in I} be the set of generic points of the irreducible components of X. Because X is a Noetherian standard scheme, I is a finite set. Since A is reduced, its nilradical is zero, meaning the intersection of its finitely many minimal prime ideals is zero. The localizations at these minimal prime ideals correspond precisely to the residue fields \kappa(\eta_i). Therefore, the canonical ring homomorphism into the direct product of these fields is strictly injective: A \hookrightarrow \prod_{i \in I} \kappa(\eta_i)

Let L be an arbitrary field extension of k. Because modules over fields are free, L is inherently flat over k. Tensoring the injective map above with L preserves injectivity. Crucially, because the index set I is finite, the tensor product perfectly distributes over the direct product, yielding the injection: A \otimes_k L \hookrightarrow \left( \prod_{i \in I} \kappa(\eta_i) \right) \otimes_k L \cong \prod_{i \in I} (\kappa(\eta_i) \otimes_k L) A subring of a reduced ring is reduced. Therefore, A \otimes_k L is reduced if and only if \kappa(\eta_i) \otimes_k L is reduced for all i \in I.

This reduces the geometric problem to the purely field-theoretic property of separability. A field extension E/k has the property that E \otimes_k L is reduced for all extensions L if and only if E is a separable extension of k. To see the necessity, suppose E/k is not separable. Then in characteristic p > 0, there exists an element x \in E \setminus k such that x^p = a \in k. If we choose L = k(a^{1/p}), then in the tensor product E \otimes_k L, the element u = x \otimes 1 - 1 \otimes a^{1/p} is non-zero. However, computing its p-th power yields: u^p = x^p \otimes 1 - 1 \otimes a = a \otimes 1 - 1 \otimes a = 0 Thus, E \otimes_k L contains non-zero nilpotent elements.

Because nilpotents under base change inevitably arise from purely inseparable algebraic elements, testing whether E \otimes_k L is reduced only requires testing against finite purely inseparable extensions, or against the perfect closure \Omega of k (which inherently contains all p-th roots). This algebraic fact immediately establishes that condition (3) is strictly equivalent to (1), (4), and (5).

To finish the proof, we show (3) implies (2) (the converse is trivial by setting Y = \operatorname{Spec}L). Let Y = \operatorname{Spec}B be any reduced affine scheme over k. By the exact same logic utilized for A, B embeds injectively into the finite product of its residue fields L_j at its maximal points. Taking the tensor product of these two injective maps over k yields: A \otimes_k B \hookrightarrow \left( \prod_{i \in I} \kappa(\eta_i) \right) \otimes_k \left( \prod_{j \in J} L_j \right) \cong \prod_{i \in I, j \in J} (\kappa(\eta_i) \otimes_k L_j) Because we assumed condition (3), each field \kappa(\eta_i) is separable over k. By the fundamental definition of field separability, every tensor product \kappa(\eta_i) \otimes_k L_j is a reduced ring. Since A \otimes_k B embeds injectively into a direct product of reduced rings, it must itself be entirely free of nilpotents. ◻

Geometrically Integral

By synthesizing the geometric conditions for being reduced (Proposition ) and being irreducible (Proposition ), we immediately deduce the corresponding criteria for a scheme to be geometrically integral. This establishes the exact conditions under which the rational function field of a variety behaves perfectly under arbitrary base changes.

Proposition 3.1 (Characterizations of Geometrically Integral Schemes). Let X be a standard scheme over a field k. The following assertions are strictly equivalent:

  1. X is geometrically integral over k.

  2. For every integral scheme Y over k, the fiber product X \times_k Y is integral.

  3. X is integral, and if \eta is the unique generic point of X, then k is algebraically closed in the rational function field \kappa(\eta), and \kappa(\eta) is a separable field extension of k.

  4. There exists an algebraically closed field extension \Omega of k such that the base change X_\Omega is integral.

  5. For every finite field extension K of k, the base change X_K is integral.

Proof. A scheme is integral if and only if it is simultaneously reduced and irreducible. Therefore, X is geometrically integral if and only if it satisfies the structural conditions of both Proposition and Proposition .

Combining condition (3) of Proposition and condition (3) of Proposition , we observe that X is geometrically integral if and only if it is an integral scheme, its rational function field \kappa(\eta) is a separable extension of k, and k is separably closed in \kappa(\eta).

However, any element in \kappa(\eta) that is algebraic over k must inherently be separable over k (since the entire global extension \kappa(\eta)/k is separable). Therefore, under the umbrella assumption of separability, stating that k is separably closed in \kappa(\eta) is strictly equivalent to stating that k is algebraically closed in \kappa(\eta). This precisely yields condition (3).

The remaining equivalences follow immediately by systematically intersecting the respective field extension conditions established in the previous two proofs. For instance, testing against all finite field extensions in (5) covers both the finite separable extensions required for irreducibility and the finite purely inseparable extensions required for reducedness. ◻

Geometrically Regular

We conclude our suite of geometric properties with geometric regularity. In the category of standard varieties over a field, geometric regularity is synonymous with smoothness. Strikingly, just as with geometric reducedness, the regularity of a scheme is entirely insensitive to separable base changes—any failure of geometric regularity is strictly governed by purely inseparable field extensions.

Proposition 3.1 (Characterizations of Geometrically Regular Schemes). Let X be a standard scheme over a field k. The following assertions are strictly equivalent:

  1. X is geometrically regular over k.

  2. The base changed scheme X_{k'} is regular for every finitely generated field extension k'/k.

  3. The base changed scheme X_{k'} is regular for every finite purely inseparable field extension k'/k.

  4. For every affine open subscheme U \subset X, the coordinate ring \mathcal{O}_X(U) is a geometrically regular k-algebra.

  5. There exists an affine open covering X = \bigcup U_i such that each coordinate ring \mathcal{O}_X(U_i) is a geometrically regular k-algebra.

Proof. (1) \implies (2): By the definition of geometric regularity, X_K is a regular scheme for all field extensions K/k. Thus, for every finitely generated field extension k'/k and every point x' \in X_{k'}, the local stalk ring \mathcal{O}_{X_{k'}, x'} is a regular local ring. This exactly means X_{k'} is a regular scheme.

(2) \implies (3): This implication is tautological. Any finite purely inseparable field extension of k is, by definition, a finite algebraic extension, and thus generated by finitely many elements (specifically, roots of purely inseparable polynomials). Therefore, it is a finitely generated field extension.

(3) \implies (4): Assume (3) holds, and let U \subset X be an arbitrary affine open subscheme. Let k'/k be any finite purely inseparable field extension. Because X_{k'} is assumed to be a regular scheme, its open subscheme U_{k'} must also be regular. Since U_{k'} \cong \operatorname{Spec}(k' \otimes_k \mathcal{O}_X(U)), this structurally implies that the ring k' \otimes_k \mathcal{O}_X(U) is regular. Because this holds for all finite purely inseparable extensions of k, the standard commutative algebra definition dictates that \mathcal{O}_X(U) is a geometrically regular k-algebra.

(4) \implies (5): This implication is trivial, as we can simply select any finite affine open covering of the standard scheme X, and (4) guarantees each patch satisfies the algebraic condition.

(5) \implies (1): Let X = \bigcup U_i be a finite affine open covering where each A_i = \mathcal{O}_X(U_i) is a geometrically regular k-algebra. Let K/k be an arbitrary field extension. The base changed scheme X_K is constructed by gluing the affine spectra of the rings A_i \otimes_k K. Because each A_i is geometrically regular over k, the tensor product A_i \otimes_k K is a regular ring for all i. Since regularity is an inherently local property that can be checked on any affine cover, X_K is uniformly a regular scheme. As K/k was arbitrary, this strictly proves X is geometrically regular over k. ◻

Remark 3.1 (Smoothness over Perfect Fields). An immediate and powerful corollary of Proposition is that over a perfect field k (such as \mathbb{C}, \mathbb{R}, \mathbb{Q}, or finite fields \mathbb{F}_q), every regular standard variety is automatically geometrically regular (i.e., smooth). This is because perfect fields admit no non-trivial purely inseparable extensions, causing condition (3) to be vacuously satisfied by the base scheme X itself.

Geometrically Normal

By synthesizing our previous results, we can observe a remarkable collapse of geometric properties for standard schemes that are geometrically normal. Because normality imposes strict algebraic regularity on the local rings, it topologically separates intersecting components, forcing connected normal schemes to be highly rigid.

Proposition 3.1 (Geometric Normality and Global Sections). Let X be a proper, geometrically normal standard scheme over a field k. The following assertions are strictly equivalent:

  1. The ring of global sections is exactly the base field: H^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X) = k.

  2. X is geometrically connected over k.

  3. X is geometrically irreducible over k.

  4. X is geometrically integral over k.

Proof. (1) \iff (2): By Proposition , because X is proper, its global section ring A = H^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X) is a finite-dimensional k-algebra. Furthermore, X is geometrically connected if and only if A is a local ring whose residue field k' is a purely inseparable extension of k.

Because X is assumed to be geometrically normal, it is inherently geometrically reduced. Consequently, the global section ring A must be geometrically reduced over k. A reduced local Artinian ring is exactly a field, so A = k'. However, because A is geometrically reduced, the tensor product k' \otimes_k \bar{k} must be a reduced ring. A purely inseparable field extension k'/k always introduces nilpotents upon base change to the algebraic closure unless the extension is trivial. Therefore, the geometric reducedness forces k' = k, yielding exactly H^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X) = k.

(2) \iff (3): Consider the base change X_{\bar{k}} to the algebraic closure. Because X is geometrically normal, X_{\bar{k}} is a normal standard scheme (and hence locally Noetherian). A profound topological property of locally Noetherian normal schemes is that their connected components and irreducible components strictly coincide. This occurs because the local stalk rings of a normal scheme are integrally closed domains (and thus strictly domains), which physically prevents distinct irreducible components from intersecting at any point. Thus, the space X_{\bar{k}} is connected if and only if it is irreducible.

(3) \iff (4): By standard commutative algebra, every normal local ring is an integral domain, and therefore a reduced ring. Since this property is stable under arbitrary base change by the hypothesis of geometric normality, X is automatically geometrically reduced. By definition, a scheme is geometrically integral if and only if it is simultaneously geometrically reduced and geometrically irreducible. Since the reducedness is already unconditionally guaranteed, geometric irreducibility and geometric integrality are strictly equivalent in this context. ◻

Absolute Stability of Skeletons under Base Change

We now return to our discussion of the topological skeletons of standard varieties. A significant and striking property emerges: the topological skeleton of a standard variety remains perfectly invariant under arbitrary field extensions.

Proposition 3.1 (Absolute Stability of Skeletons under Base Change). Let X be a standard variety over a field k, and let K/k be an arbitrary field extension. Consider the base change X_K = X \times_{\operatorname{Spec}k} \operatorname{Spec}K and the canonical projection morphism \pi \colon X_K \to X. Let F_d(X) := X_{\le d} denote the d-th topological skeleton of X. Then the skeleton filtration is absolutely stable under field extensions, meaning that for every integer d \ge 0, we have the strict equality: \pi^{-1}(F_d(X)) = F_d(X_K) In terms of the individual dimension strata, this is equivalent to \pi^{-1}(X_{(d)}) = (X_K)_{(d)} for all d \ge 0.

Proof. By the set-theoretic definition of the preimage, a point z \in X_K belongs to \pi^{-1}(F_d(X)) if and only if its image x = \pi(z) satisfies \dim(x) \le d. On the other hand, z \in F_d(X_K) if and only if \dim(z) \le d. Therefore, the topological identity \pi^{-1}(F_d(X)) = F_d(X_K) reduces precisely to showing that the dimension of any point is strictly invariant under the canonical projection, i.e., \dim(z) = \dim(\pi(z)) for every point z \in X_K.

Recall that for any standard variety over a field F (which is inherently a Jacobson scheme of finite type), the dimension of a point y (defined as the Krull dimension of its Zariski closure \overline{\{y\}}) can be computed internally via the algebraic transcendence degree of its residue field \kappa(y) over the base field F: \dim(y) = \operatorname{tr.deg}_F \kappa(y) Applying this dictionary to X over k and X_K over K, we obtain: \dim(x) = \operatorname{tr.deg}_k \kappa(x) \quad \text{and} \quad \dim(z) = \operatorname{tr.deg}_K \kappa(z)

The point z \in X_K maps to x \in X under \pi, which dictates a canonical inclusion of fields k \hookrightarrow \kappa(x) \hookrightarrow \kappa(z) and k \hookrightarrow K \hookrightarrow \kappa(z). By the universal property of the fiber product, the residue field \kappa(z) is obtained as the residue field of a prime ideal \mathfrak{p} in the tensor product ring \kappa(x) \otimes_k K. That is, \kappa(z) \cong \operatorname{Frac}((\kappa(x) \otimes_k K)/\mathfrak{p}).

A fundamental theorem in commutative algebra dictates that if E is a field extension of k with finite transcendence degree, then for any arbitrary field extension K/k and any prime ideal \mathfrak{p} \subset E \otimes_k K, the fraction field \Omega = \operatorname{Frac}((E \otimes_k K)/\mathfrak{p}) preserves the transcendence degree strictly when changing the base field from k to K: \operatorname{tr.deg}_K \Omega = \operatorname{tr.deg}_k E Setting E = \kappa(x) and \Omega = \kappa(z), this algebraic identity directly yields: \operatorname{tr.deg}_K \kappa(z) = \operatorname{tr.deg}_k \kappa(x) Consequently, \dim(z) = \dim(x). This shows that the dimension of a point is an absolute geometric invariant independent of the ground field coefficients, thereby establishing the rigorous equality \pi^{-1}(F_d(X)) = F_d(X_K). ◻

Remark 3.1 (Geometric Interpretation of Absolute Stability). This stability property reveals a mathematically beautiful picture: when we perform a base extension to a larger field K (such as transitioning from \mathbb{R} to \mathbb{C}), a single closed point x \in X with a non-trivial separable residue field will typically “shatter” into a finite collection of points in X_K. However, this theorem geometrically guarantees that all these newborn points in X_K are strictly forced to remain closed points (\dim = 0).

More generally, no irreducible subvariety can collapse or expand its dimension level during a field extension; a curve strictly maps to a curve, and a surface strictly maps to a surface. The topological skeletons F_d(X) act as an immutable, rigid coordinate framework that absolutely controls the dimension of geometric objects across the entire dynamics of base change.

Birational Geometry of Standard Schemes

Throughout this section, let S be a standard base ring in the sense of Definition , and write B:=\operatorname{Spec}S. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, all schemes are standard schemes over S, and all morphisms are morphisms over B, in accordance with Note . In particular, every scheme considered below is Noetherian and separated over B.

Generic Geometry Beyond the Integral Case

Remark 3.1 (Corrections to the Classical Integral Language). The classical language of birational geometry is usually introduced for integral varieties. Several statements from that setting cease to be correct if one merely deletes the words “irreducible” and “reduced.” The required replacements are as follows.

  1. A non-empty open subset need not meet every irreducible component, and on a non-reduced scheme even a topologically dense open subset may erase nilpotent or embedded information. The correct notion is a schematically dense open subscheme.

  2. The ring of rational functions is generally a ring, not a field. It becomes the usual function field only when the source is integral.

  3. The existence of a unique maximal domain of definition uses separatedness of the target. This hypothesis is automatic in the standard category, but it must be stated in the general theory.

  4. Arbitrary rational maps cannot always be composed. Dominance guarantees composability in the integral case, but not for an arbitrary reducible or non-reduced scheme.

  5. For general schemes, birationality should be defined directly by an isomorphism between schematically dense open subschemes, rather than by formally postulating inverse rational maps whose compositions may not exist.

Schematically Dense Open Subschemes

Definition 3.14 (Schematically Dense Open Subscheme). Let X be a scheme and let j\colon U\hookrightarrow X be an open immersion. We say that U is schematically dense in X if the canonical morphism of sheaves j^{\sharp}\colon \mathcal{O}_X\longrightarrow j_*\mathcal{O}_U is injective.

Equivalently, no non-zero local section of \mathcal{O}_X becomes zero after restriction to U. The open immersion j is then also called schematically dominant.

The adjective “schematically” is essential: ordinary topological density detects the support of a scheme, whereas schematic density also detects nilpotents and embedded associated components.

Definition 3.15 (Associated Points). Let A be a Noetherian ring. A prime ideal \mathfrak p\subseteq A is associated if \mathfrak p=\operatorname{Ann}(a) for some a\in A. The set of associated prime ideals is denoted by \operatorname{Ass}(A).

Let X be a locally Noetherian scheme. A point x\in X is an associated point if the maximal ideal of \mathcal{O}_{X,x} is an associated prime of \mathcal{O}_{X,x}. The set of associated points is denoted by \operatorname{Ass}(X). On an affine open \operatorname{Spec}A\subseteq X, this definition agrees with \operatorname{Ass}(A). An associated point is called embedded if it is not the generic point of an irreducible component.

Proposition 3.1 (Equivalent Forms of Schematic Density). Let j\colon U\hookrightarrow X be an open immersion. The following conditions are equivalent.

  1. U is schematically dense in X.

  2. For every open subscheme V\subseteq X, the only closed subscheme of V through which U\cap V\hookrightarrow V factors is V itself.

  3. For every open subscheme V\subseteq X, every separated B-scheme Y, and every pair of B-morphisms f,g\colon V\to Y, the equality f|_{U\cap V}=g|_{U\cap V} implies f=g.

Proof. The equivalence of (1) and (2) is local on X. If a closed subscheme Z\hookrightarrow V is defined by an ideal sheaf \mathcal I\subseteq\mathcal{O}_V and contains U\cap V scheme-theoretically, then every section of \mathcal I restricts to zero on U\cap V. Injectivity of \mathcal{O}_V\to j_*\mathcal{O}_{U\cap V} therefore forces \mathcal I=0. Conversely, the kernel of \mathcal{O}_V\to j_*\mathcal{O}_{U\cap V} defines a closed subscheme containing U\cap V.

Assume (2). Since Y is separated over B, the equalizer of f and g is the inverse image of the diagonal \Delta_{Y/B} and is therefore a closed subscheme of V. It contains U\cap V, hence equals V by (2). Thus (3) holds.

Finally, apply (3) to Y=\mathbb A^1_B. Morphisms from an open subscheme V to \mathbb A^1_B are exactly sections of \mathcal{O}_X(V), so (3) gives the injectivity in (1). ◻

Proposition 3.1 (Associated-Point Criterion). Let X be a standard scheme and let U\subseteq X be open. Then U\text{ is schematically dense in }X \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \operatorname{Ass}(X)\subseteq U. Consequently, the intersection of finitely many schematically dense open subschemes is again schematically dense.

Proof. The assertion is local on X. Write X=\operatorname{Spec}A, where A is Noetherian. The set of zero divisors of A is the union of the associated prime ideals of A. If U=X\setminus V(\mathfrak a), then U contains \operatorname{Ass}(A) exactly when \mathfrak a contains an element t which is not a zero divisor. In that case D(t)\subseteq U, and the injectivity of A\to A_t shows that D(t), hence U, is schematically dense. Conversely, if U misses an associated prime \mathfrak p=\operatorname{Ann}(a) with a\neq 0, then a restricts to zero on U, contradicting schematic density.

The final assertion follows either from the associated-point criterion or directly from the injectivity of the composite restriction morphism. ◻

Corollary 3.1 (Reduced Schemes). Let X be a reduced standard scheme. For an open subscheme U\subseteq X, the following are equivalent.

  1. U is schematically dense in X.

  2. U is topologically dense in X.

  3. U contains the generic point of every irreducible component of X.

If X is integral, these are further equivalent to U\neq\varnothing.

Proof. A reduced Noetherian scheme has no embedded associated points, so \operatorname{Ass}(X) is exactly the finite set of generic points of the irreducible components. The result now follows from Proposition . ◻

Example 3.1 (A Dense Open Which is Not Schematically Dense). Let A=k[x,\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2,x\varepsilon), \qquad X=\operatorname{Spec}A, \qquad U=D(x). The reduced scheme X_{\mathrm{red}} is \mathbb A^1_k, so U is topologically dense in X. However, x is invertible on U, and the relation x\varepsilon=0 forces \varepsilon|_U=0. Since \varepsilon\neq 0 in A, the map A\to A_x is not injective. Hence U is not schematically dense.

Algebraically, \operatorname{Ass}(A)=\{(\varepsilon),(x,\varepsilon)\}. The open set D(x) contains the generic point (\varepsilon) but misses the embedded associated point (x,\varepsilon). This is precisely the infinitesimal information that topological density fails to see.

Remark 3.1 (The Correct Generic Skeleton). For an integral scheme, the entire birational theory is concentrated at one generic point. For a reduced reducible scheme, it is concentrated at the finitely many generic points of its irreducible components. For a non-reduced scheme, this is still insufficient: every schematically dense open subscheme must also contain the embedded associated points. Thus the appropriate “generic skeleton” for strict birational geometry is \operatorname{Ass}(X), not merely the highest-dimensional topological stratum.

Rational Maps on Standard Schemes

Definition 3.16 (Rational Map). Let X and Y be standard schemes. A rational map f\colon X\dashrightarrow Y is an equivalence class of pairs (U,\widetilde f), where

  1. U\subseteq X is an open schematically dense subscheme;

  2. \widetilde f\colon U\to Y is a morphism over B.

Two representatives (U,\widetilde f) and (V,\widetilde g) are equivalent if there exists an open schematically dense subscheme W\subseteq U\cap V such that \widetilde f|_W=\widetilde g|_W. The set of rational maps is denoted by \operatorname{Rat}_B(X,Y).

Remark 3.1 (Terminology). This is the scheme-theoretically dense version of the notion of a rational map. It is also called a strict rational map or a pseudo-morphism in parts of the literature. On a reduced standard scheme it agrees with the usual definition using topologically dense open subsets.

Because every standard scheme is separated over B, equivalence of representatives has a particularly strong form.

Proposition 3.1 (Agreement on the Full Intersection). Let (U,\widetilde f) and (V,\widetilde g) be representatives of rational maps from X to Y. They represent the same rational map if and only if \widetilde f|_{U\cap V}=\widetilde g|_{U\cap V}.

Proof. Only the forward implication requires proof. By definition the two maps agree on a schematically dense open W\subseteq U\cap V. The target Y is separated over B, so Proposition applied to U\cap V implies equality on all of U\cap V. ◻

Proposition 3.1 (Maximal Domain of Definition). Let f\colon X\dashrightarrow Y be a rational map. There exists a unique largest open schematically dense subscheme \operatorname{dom}(f)\subseteq X and a unique morphism f_{\max}\colon \operatorname{dom}(f)\longrightarrow Y representing f. Every representative of f is obtained by restricting f_{\max}.

Proof. Take the union of the domains of all representatives. By Proposition , the representative morphisms agree on pairwise intersections and therefore glue. The union contains a schematically dense open subscheme, hence is itself schematically dense. Maximality and uniqueness are immediate. ◻

Remark 3.1 (A Rational Map is Not a Set-Theoretic Map). The symbol \dashrightarrow records that a rational map is an equivalence class of partially defined morphisms. A value f(x) is meaningful only for x\in\operatorname{dom}(f). The maximal representative exists here because the target is separated; without separatedness, representatives need not glue uniquely.

The Ring of Rational Functions

Definition 3.17 (Rational Functions). Let X be a standard scheme. A rational function on X is a rational map X\dashrightarrow \mathbb A^1_B. The set of rational functions is denoted by R(X). Addition and multiplication on \mathbb A^1_B make R(X) a commutative S-algebra, and R(X)=\varinjlim_{U}\Gamma(U,\mathcal{O}_X), where U ranges over the directed system of open schematically dense subschemes of X.

Proposition 3.1 (Affine Description by the Total Quotient Ring). Let X=\operatorname{Spec}A be an affine standard scheme, and let T=A\setminus\bigcup_{\mathfrak p\in\operatorname{Ass}(A)}\mathfrak p be the multiplicative set of non-zero-divisors of A. Then R(X)\cong Q(A):=T^{-1}A.

Proof. For every t\in T, the principal open D(t) is schematically dense and \Gamma(D(t),\mathcal{O}_X)=A_t. Conversely, every schematically dense open subset of the Noetherian affine scheme X contains some D(t) with t\in T, by the proof of Proposition . The direct limit defining R(X) is therefore exactly \varinjlim_{t\in T}A_t=T^{-1}A. ◻

Corollary 3.1 (Integral and Reduced Specializations). Let X be a standard scheme.

  1. If X is integral with generic point \eta, then R(X)=\mathcal{O}_{X,\eta}=\kappa(\eta)=:K(X), the usual function field.

  2. If X is reduced with irreducible components X_1,\dots,X_r and generic points \eta_1,\dots,\eta_r, then R(X)\cong\prod_{i=1}^r \kappa(\eta_i) =\prod_{i=1}^r K(X_i).

Proof. In the integral case every non-empty open subset contains \eta, so the direct limit of regular functions is the stalk \mathcal{O}_{X,\eta}, which is a field.

If X is reduced, the sheaf of rational functions is the finite product \mathcal K_X\cong \prod_{i=1}^r (j_i)_*\kappa(\eta_i), \qquad j_i\colon\operatorname{Spec}\kappa(\eta_i)\to X. Taking global sections gives the stated product decomposition. ◻

Example 3.1 (A Reducible Variety). Let X=\operatorname{Spec}k[x,y]/(xy), the union of the two coordinate axes. Then X is reduced with two irreducible components, and R(X)\cong k(x)\times k(y). Thus a rational function on X is a pair of rational functions, one on each component; no compatibility is imposed at the intersection point, because that point can be removed while retaining a schematically dense open subset.

Example 3.1 (A Generically Non-Reduced Variety). Let X=\operatorname{Spec}k[x,\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2). The non-zero-divisors are precisely the elements whose image in k[x] is non-zero. Hence R(X)\cong k(x)[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2). The nilpotent direction survives in the rational function ring. Therefore replacing X by X_{\mathrm{red}} changes its strict birational data: R(X_{\mathrm{red}})=k(x) \neq k(x)[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)=R(X).

Composition and Dominance

Example 3.1 (Failure of Composition). Let X=\mathbb A^1_k,\qquad Y=\mathbb A^2_k,\qquad Z=\mathbb A^1_k. Define a morphism f\colon X\to Y by t\mapsto(t,0), and let g\colon Y\dashrightarrow Z be represented on D(y) by the regular function x/y. Since f(X) is the x-axis, one has f^{-1}(D(y))=\varnothing. Thus there is no schematically dense open subset of X on which the expression g\circ f can be formed.

Proposition 3.1 (Criterion for Composing Rational Maps). Let f\colon X\dashrightarrow Y, \qquad g\colon Y\dashrightarrow Z be rational maps. Choose representatives \widetilde f\colon U\to Y and \widetilde g\colon V\to Z. If U\cap\widetilde f^{-1}(V) is schematically dense in X, then \widetilde g\circ \widetilde f|_{U\cap\widetilde f^{-1}(V)} defines a rational map g\circ f\colon X\dashrightarrow Z. The resulting rational map is independent of the chosen representatives.

Proof. Replace any two choices by their intersections. All relevant representatives agree on these intersections by Proposition . Intersecting once more with the two assumed schematically dense composition domains gives a schematically dense open subset on which the two candidate compositions coincide. Hence they define the same rational map. ◻

Definition 3.18 (Dominant Rational Map). A rational map f\colon X\dashrightarrow Y is called dominant if the image of one, equivalently every, representative is topologically dense in Y.

Remark 3.1 (Dominance Does Not Solve the General Composition Problem). On integral schemes, a dominant rational map sends the generic point of the source to the generic point of the target, and therefore pulls every non-empty open subset of the target back to a non-empty open subset of the source. This makes composition possible.

For a reducible or non-reduced scheme, ordinary topological dominance does not control every irreducible component and does not control embedded associated points. Hence it does not by itself imply the schematic-density hypothesis in Proposition .

For reduced schemes there is a useful componentwise replacement. Let X^0 denote the finite set of generic points of the irreducible components of a reduced standard scheme X.

Definition 3.19 (Componentwise Dominant Rational Map). Let X and Y be reduced standard schemes. A rational map f\colon X\dashrightarrow Y is componentwise dominant if its maximal representative satisfies f(X^0)=Y^0.

Proposition 3.1 (Composition in the Reduced Case). Componentwise dominant rational maps between reduced standard schemes can be composed, and their composition is again componentwise dominant. Thus reduced standard schemes with componentwise dominant rational maps form a category.

Proof. Let f\colon X\dashrightarrow Y be componentwise dominant and let g\colon Y\dashrightarrow Z be represented on a dense open V\subseteq Y. Since Y is reduced, V contains Y^0. Therefore f^{-1}(V) contains X^0, hence is schematically dense in X. The composition criterion applies. The equality on generic points (g\circ f)(X^0)=g(Y^0)=Z^0 proves that the composite is componentwise dominant. ◻

Birational Equivalence of Standard Schemes

Definition 3.20 (Birational Map, Birational Morphism, and Birational Equivalence). Let X and Y be standard schemes.

  1. A rational map f\colon X\dashrightarrow Y is birational if it has a representative \widetilde f\colon U\xrightarrow{\sim}V which is an isomorphism from an open schematically dense subscheme U\subseteq X onto an open schematically dense subscheme V\subseteq Y.

  2. A morphism f\colon X\to Y is birational if there are open schematically dense subschemes U\subseteq X and V\subseteq Y such that f(U)=V and f|_U\colon U\xrightarrow{\sim}V.

  3. The schemes X and Y are birationally equivalent, written X\sim_{\mathrm{bir}}Y, if there exists a birational rational map from X to Y.

Proposition 3.1 (Birational Equivalence is an Equivalence Relation). Birational equivalence is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive on standard schemes.

Proof. Reflexivity and symmetry are immediate. For transitivity, suppose U\xrightarrow{\sim}V, \qquad U'\xrightarrow{\sim}V' exhibit X\sim_{\mathrm{bir}}Y and Y\sim_{\mathrm{bir}}Z, respectively. The open subset V\cap U' is schematically dense in Y. Its inverse image in U is schematically dense in X, and its image in V' is schematically dense in Z. Restricting the two isomorphisms therefore gives an isomorphism between schematically dense open subschemes of X and Z. ◻

Proposition 3.1 (Generic Invariants of Birational Equivalence). If X\sim_{\mathrm{bir}}Y, then:

  1. every chosen birational rational map induces a bijection \operatorname{Ass}(X)\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{Ass}(Y) induced by any birational representative;

  2. corresponding associated points x\in\operatorname{Ass}(X) and y\in\operatorname{Ass}(Y) have isomorphic local rings \mathcal{O}_{X,x}\cong\mathcal{O}_{Y,y} as local S-algebras;

  3. restriction along a birational representative induces an isomorphism R(X)\cong R(Y) of S-algebras.

Proof. Fix a birational representative U\xrightarrow{\sim}V. By schematic density, \operatorname{Ass}(X)\subseteq U and \operatorname{Ass}(Y)\subseteq V. Since associated points localize under open immersions, \operatorname{Ass}(U)=\operatorname{Ass}(X), \qquad \operatorname{Ass}(V)=\operatorname{Ass}(Y). The isomorphism U\cong V therefore gives (1) and (2). Rational functions are unchanged after restriction to a schematically dense open subscheme, yielding (3). ◻

Example 3.1 (Reduction Need Not Preserve Birational Type). For X=\operatorname{Spec}k[x,\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2), the reduction X_{\mathrm{red}}=\mathbb A^1_k is not birational to X in the strict scheme-theoretic sense. Indeed, every schematically dense open subscheme of X retains the non-zero nilpotent \varepsilon, whereas every open subscheme of X_{\mathrm{red}} is reduced. Equivalently, R(X)=k(x)[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2) \not\cong k(x)=R(X_{\mathrm{red}}).

Theorem 3.1 (The Common-Roof Characterization). Let X and Y be standard schemes. The following are equivalent.

  1. X\sim_{\mathrm{bir}}Y.

  2. There exists a standard scheme Z and birational morphisms \pi_X\colon Z\to X, \qquad \pi_Y\colon Z\to Y.

Proof. Suppose U\xrightarrow{\sim}V is an isomorphism of schematically dense open subschemes of X and Y. Let \Gamma\subseteq U\times_B V be its graph, and let Z be the scheme-theoretic closure of \Gamma in X\times_B Y. Since X\times_B Y is standard and Z is closed, Z is standard. The immersion \Gamma\hookrightarrow X\times_B Y is quasi-compact, so \Gamma is an open schematically dense subscheme of Z. The two projections restrict on \Gamma to the isomorphisms \Gamma\cong U and \Gamma\cong V, hence both projections are birational morphisms.

Conversely, let Z\to X and Z\to Y be birational. Choose schematically dense open subsets of Z on which the two morphisms are isomorphisms onto open subschemes of X and Y. Their intersection is schematically dense in Z. Transporting this intersection through the two isomorphisms gives isomorphic schematically dense open subschemes of X and Y. ◻

Reduced and Reducible Standard Varieties

In this section the base ring is a field k, so the objects are standard varieties in the sense of Definition .

Theorem 3.1 (Componentwise Classification of Reduced Varieties). Let X and Y be reduced standard varieties. Write X=X_1\cup\cdots\cup X_r, \qquad Y=Y_1\cup\cdots\cup Y_s for their irreducible-component decompositions. The following are equivalent.

  1. X\sim_{\mathrm{bir}}Y.

  2. r=s and, after a permutation of the components, X_i\sim_{\mathrm{bir}}Y_i \qquad(1\le i\le r).

  3. Their rational function rings are isomorphic as k-algebras: R(X)\cong R(Y).

Proof. If U\subseteq X and V\subseteq Y are dense open subschemes with U\cong V, then the irreducible components of U and V correspond. Since dense opens preserve the generic points of all components, this gives a bijection between the components of X and Y, and corresponding components contain isomorphic non-empty opens. Hence (1) implies (2).

Conversely, assume (2). For each i, remove from X_i its intersections with the other components, and do the same for Y_i. This produces pairwise disjoint non-empty open subsets X_i^{\circ}=X_i\setminus\bigcup_{j\ne i}X_j, \qquad Y_i^{\circ}=Y_i\setminus\bigcup_{j\ne i}Y_j. Shrinking further inside X_i^{\circ} and Y_i^{\circ}, componentwise birationality gives isomorphic non-empty opens U_i\cong V_i. Then \coprod_{i=1}^r U_i\cong\coprod_{i=1}^r V_i is an isomorphism between dense open subschemes of X and Y. Thus (2) implies (1).

Finally, R(X)=\prod_{i=1}^r K(X_i), \qquad R(Y)=\prod_{j=1}^s K(Y_j). An isomorphism between finite products of fields permutes the primitive idempotents and hence the field factors. The integral theorem proved below identifies an isomorphism K(X_i)\cong K(Y_j) with birationality of the corresponding integral components. This proves the equivalence with (3). ◻

Example 3.1 (Separating the Components Birationally). The nodal reducible curve X=\operatorname{Spec}k[x,y]/(xy) is birational to the disjoint union \mathbb A^1_k\amalg\mathbb A^1_k. Indeed, deleting the intersection point from X gives \mathbb G_{m,k}\amalg\mathbb G_{m,k}, which is also a dense open subscheme of the disjoint union. Birational geometry of a reduced scheme therefore forgets how distinct irreducible components meet along proper closed subsets.

The Integral Case and Function Fields

We now recover the classical theory as a specialization of the preceding scheme-theoretic framework.

Remark 3.1 (The Top Skeleton and the Function Field). Let X be an integral standard scheme with generic point \eta_X. Then every non-empty open subscheme is schematically dense, and R(X)=\mathcal{O}_{X,\eta_X}=K(X). Thus in the integral case birational geometry is indeed controlled by the unique generic point. The preceding sections explain exactly why this slogan must be replaced by associated-point data once reducibility or nilpotents are allowed.

Let s_X\in B be the image of \eta_X under the structure morphism. The field K(X) is naturally an extension of the residue field \kappa(s_X). If X is a standard variety over a field k, then s_X is the unique point of \operatorname{Spec}k and K(X)/k is a finitely generated field extension.

Theorem 3.1 (Equivalence of Rational Data). Let X and Y be integral standard schemes with generic points \eta_X and \eta_Y. Write s_X,s_Y\in B for their images. If s_X\ne s_Y, there is no dominant B-rational map X\dashrightarrow Y. If s_X=s_Y=s, there is a natural bijection \left\{ \begin{array}{c} \text{dominant rational maps}\\[-0.2em] f\colon X\dashrightarrow Y \end{array} \right\} \xrightarrow{\sim} \operatorname{Hom}_{\kappa(s)\text{-alg}}\left(K(Y),K(X)\right), \qquad f\longmapsto f^*. Equivalently, for a rational map f\colon X\dashrightarrow Y, the following are equivalent.

  1. f is dominant.

  2. f(\eta_X)=\eta_Y.

  3. The induced local homomorphism f^*\colon\mathcal{O}_{Y,\eta_Y}\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{X,\eta_X} is a \kappa(s)-embedding K(Y)\hookrightarrow K(X).

Proof. Any B-rational map sends \eta_X to a point of Y lying over s_X. A dominant map must send \eta_X to \eta_Y, so necessarily s_X=s_Y.

Assume s_X=s_Y=s. A dominant rational map has a representative on a non-empty open subset of X and sends \eta_X to \eta_Y. Passing to stalks gives a \kappa(s)-algebra homomorphism K(Y)\to K(X), which is injective because its source is a field.

Conversely, a \kappa(s)-embedding K(Y)\hookrightarrow K(X) defines a B-morphism \operatorname{Spec}K(X)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Spec}K(Y)\longrightarrow Y sending the generic point to \eta_Y. Since Y\to B is of finite type over the Noetherian scheme B, it is of finite presentation. Therefore this morphism extends to a morphism from some non-empty open neighbourhood of \eta_X in X to Y. Separatedness of Y makes the resulting rational map unique. Its image contains \eta_Y, hence it is dominant. ◻

Corollary 3.1 (Affine Representatives of Field Embeddings). Under the hypotheses of Theorem , every embedding K(Y)\hookrightarrow K(X) is represented by an injective homomorphism C\hookrightarrow A between the coordinate rings of suitable non-empty affine open subschemes \operatorname{Spec}A\subseteq X and \operatorname{Spec}C\subseteq Y. Conversely, such an injective homomorphism defines a dominant rational map X\dashrightarrow Y.

Proof. Extend the generic-point morphism to a representative U\to Y, shrink Y to an affine neighbourhood \operatorname{Spec}C of \eta_Y, and then shrink the inverse image to a non-empty affine open \operatorname{Spec}A. Dominance and the integrality of C imply that the induced map C\to A has zero kernel. The converse follows from the standard affine criterion for dominance. ◻

Dominant rational maps between integral standard schemes can always be composed, because the inverse image of a non-empty open subset contains the generic point. Hence integral standard schemes with dominant rational maps form a category. In this category, birational maps are precisely the isomorphisms.

The Fundamental Theorem of Birational Geometry

Theorem 3.1 (The Fundamental Theorem of Birational Geometry). Let X and Y be integral standard schemes. Let s_X,s_Y\in B be the images of their generic points. The following conditions are equivalent.

  1. X and Y are birational over B.

  2. There exist non-empty open subschemes U\subseteq X and V\subseteq Y which are isomorphic over B.

  3. One has s_X=s_Y=s, and there is an isomorphism K(X)\cong K(Y) of \kappa(s)-extensions.

  4. One has s_X=s_Y=s, and \mathcal{O}_{X,\eta_X}\cong\mathcal{O}_{Y,\eta_Y} as local \mathcal{O}_{B,s}-algebras.

  5. There exists an integral standard scheme Z and birational morphisms Z\to X, \qquad Z\to Y.

Proof. Conditions (1) and (2) are equivalent by the definition of birationality; for integral schemes every non-empty open is schematically dense. The equivalence of (3) and (4) follows from \mathcal{O}_{X,\eta_X}=K(X), \qquad \mathcal{O}_{Y,\eta_Y}=K(Y). Condition (2) immediately implies (3) by passing to generic-point stalks. Conversely, an isomorphism K(Y)\xrightarrow{\sim}K(X) and its inverse give mutually inverse dominant rational maps by Theorem . After restricting to the common domain on which both compositions are identities, one obtains isomorphic non-empty open subschemes, proving (3) implies (2).

Condition (2) implies (5) by taking the scheme-theoretic closure of the graph of the open isomorphism in X\times_B Y. Since the graph is integral, its scheme-theoretic closure is integral. The two projections are birational morphisms. Finally, (5) implies (3), because a birational morphism induces an isomorphism on the function fields. ◻

Remark 3.1 (The Common Roof is Not Automatically a Resolution). The scheme Z in the common-roof statement is merely a common birational model. It need not be regular, normal, proper, or projective. Resolution of singularities is a much stronger theorem. For integral varieties over a field of characteristic zero, one may often choose a regular birational model after additional work; this statement does not extend unchanged to arbitrary non-reduced standard schemes.

Remark 3.1 (Failure of the Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein Principle). The existence of dominant rational maps in both directions does not imply birationality. Algebraically, embeddings K(Y)\hookrightarrow K(X), \qquad K(X)\hookrightarrow K(Y) need not make the two finitely generated fields isomorphic.

Example 3.1 (Mutually Dominant but Not Birational). Over k=\mathbb C, let E_1=\mathbb C/(\mathbb Z+\mathbb Zi), \qquad E_2=\mathbb C/(\mathbb Z+2\mathbb Zi). The inclusion \mathbb Z+2\mathbb Zi\subset\mathbb Z+\mathbb Zi gives an isogeny E_2\to E_1, while multiplication by 2 gives an isogeny E_1\to E_2. Hence there are dominant morphisms, and therefore dominant rational maps, in both directions.

Nevertheless, j(E_1)=1728 whereas j(E_2)\ne1728, so the two elliptic curves are not isomorphic. Smooth projective curves are birational if and only if they are isomorphic; therefore E_1 and E_2 are not birational.

Realization of Rational Function Fields and Rings

Remark 3.1 (Finite Versus Finitely Generated Extensions). A finite field extension is algebraic and has transcendence degree zero. Function fields of positive-dimensional integral varieties are instead finitely generated field extensions, usually with a non-trivial transcendental part.

Proposition 3.1 (Realization of Function Fields). Let k be a field and let K/k be a field extension. The following are equivalent.

  1. K\cong K(X) for some integral standard variety X over k.

  2. K/k is finitely generated as a field extension.

In this case \dim X=\operatorname{trdeg}_k K.

Proof. If K=K(X), choose a non-empty affine open \operatorname{Spec}A\subseteq X. The ring A is a finitely generated integral k-algebra and K=\operatorname{Frac}(A), so K/k is finitely generated.

Conversely, write K=k(t_1,\dots,t_n) and let A=k[t_1,\dots,t_n]\subseteq K. Then A is a finitely generated integral k-algebra, X=\operatorname{Spec}A is an integral standard variety, and K(X)=\operatorname{Frac}(A)=K. The dimension formula \dim A=\operatorname{trdeg}_k\operatorname{Frac}(A) yields the final assertion. ◻

Proposition 3.1 (Realization for Reduced Standard Varieties). Let k be a field and let R be a k-algebra. The following are equivalent.

  1. R\cong R(X) for some reduced standard variety X over k.

  2. There exist finitely generated field extensions K_1/k,\dots,K_r/k such that R\cong\prod_{i=1}^r K_i as k-algebras.

If X_i denotes the component corresponding to K_i, then \dim X_i=\operatorname{trdeg}_k K_i, \qquad \dim X=\max_i\operatorname{trdeg}_k K_i.

Proof. The forward direction follows from Corollary and the finite number of irreducible components of a standard variety.

Conversely, realize each K_i as the function field of an integral affine standard variety X_i by Proposition , and set X=\coprod_{i=1}^r X_i. This finite disjoint union is a reduced standard variety and R(X)=\prod_iK_i. The dimension statements follow componentwise. ◻

Remark 3.1 (Why There is No Field-Only Classification in the Non-Reduced Case). For a non-reduced affine standard variety X=\operatorname{Spec}A, the rational function ring is the total quotient ring Q(A), which may contain nilpotents and may retain embedded associated-prime information. It need not be a product of fields, or even a product of zero-dimensional local rings when embedded associated primes are present. Thus the correct general invariant is the scheme-theoretic generic algebra together with its associated-point structure, not a single function field.
