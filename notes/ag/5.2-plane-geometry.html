Intersection Numbers

We first define intersection numbers analytically via the Euler-Poincaré characteristic. This establishes a fundamentally topological and homological foundation for the theory.

Definition 2.1 (Intersection Number via Euler Characteristics). Let X be a smooth projective variety of dimension n. Let \mathcal{L}_1, \dots, \mathcal{L}_n be invertible sheaves on X. The intersection number of these sheaves is defined as the alternating sum of the Euler characteristics of their tensor products: (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdot \mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_n) \coloneqq \sum_{I \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}} (-1)^{n - |I|} \chi\Big(X, \bigotimes_{i \in I} \mathcal{L}_i\Big).

To demonstrate that this definition is well-behaved, we must understand the behavior of the Euler characteristic under tensor operations. This brings us to a foundational theorem which asserts that the Euler characteristic of a tensor product of powers of invertible sheaves behaves polynomially.

Theorem 2.1 (Snapper’s Polynomial Theorem). Let X be a smooth projective variety, and let \mathcal{F} be a coherent sheaf on X with s = \dim \operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F}. Let \mathcal{L}_1, \dots, \mathcal{L}_t be t invertible sheaves on X. Then the Euler-Poincaré characteristic \chi(\mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}). is a numerical polynomial in n_1, \dots, n_t of total degree s.

(By a numerical polynomial in n_1, \dots, n_t, we mean a polynomial with rational coefficients which assumes integer values whenever n_1, \dots, n_t are integers.)

Proof. We proceed by induction on s, the dimension of the support of \mathcal{F}.

Base Case: When s = -1, the support is empty, meaning \mathcal{F} = 0. The theorem holds trivially since the Euler characteristic is uniformly 0.

Inductive Step: Assume s \ge 0. By standard dévissage arguments for coherent sheaves on projective schemes, it suffices to prove the theorem for \mathcal{F} = \mathcal{O}_Y, where Y is an irreducible closed subvariety. Since we are focusing on the smooth projective case, we may smoothly reduce to assuming \mathcal{F} = \mathcal{O}_X (so s = \dim X), and we proceed by induction on t, the number of invertible sheaves. When t = 0, the statement is trivial.

Because X is smooth, its local rings are UFDs, allowing us to find a Cartier divisor D such that \mathcal{L}_1 \simeq \mathcal{O}_X(D). We define two coherent sheaves of ideals: \mathcal{I} = \mathcal{O}_X(-D) \cap \mathcal{O}_X \quad \text{and} \quad \mathcal{J} = \mathcal{I} \cdot \mathcal{O}_X(D) \simeq \mathcal{I} \otimes \mathcal{L}_1. From these, we construct the torsion sheaves \mathcal{S} = \mathcal{O}_X / \mathcal{I} and \mathcal{K} = (\mathcal{O}_X / \mathcal{J}) \otimes \mathcal{L}_1. Since \mathcal{S} and \mathcal{K} are supported on proper closed subschemes of X, their support dimensions are strictly less than s. Thus, by our outer induction hypothesis on s, the theorem already holds for \mathcal{S} and \mathcal{K}.

Tensoring the short exact sequences associated with \mathcal{S} and \mathcal{K} by the appropriate powers of our invertible sheaves yields two new exact sequences: 0 \to \mathcal{I} \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t} \to \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t} \to \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t} \to 0 and 0 \to \mathcal{J} \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes (n_1-1)} \otimes \mathcal{L}_2^{\otimes n_2} \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t} \to \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes (n_1-1)} \otimes \mathcal{L}_2^{\otimes n_2} \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t} \to \mathcal{K} \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes (n_1-2)} \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t} \to 0.

Because \mathcal{J} \simeq \mathcal{I} \otimes \mathcal{L}_1, the first term of the second sequence is isomorphic to the first term of the first sequence. The Euler characteristic \chi is additive on short exact sequences, so we subtract the Euler characteristic of the middle term of the second sequence from that of the first sequence: \begin{align*} &\chi(\mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}) - \chi(\mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes (n_1-1)} \otimes \mathcal{L}_2^{\otimes n_2} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}) \\ &= \chi(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}) - \chi(\mathcal{K} \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes (n_1-2)} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}). \end{align*} By our induction hypothesis on s, both terms on the right-hand side are numerical polynomials in n_1, \dots, n_t of total degree less than s = \dim X.

We have shown that the first difference of the function with respect to n_1 is a polynomial of degree strictly less than s. Furthermore, since \chi(\mathcal{L}_2^{\otimes n_2} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}) is a numerical polynomial in n_2, \dots, n_t of degree s by induction on t, the theorem follows immediately from the calculus of finite differences. ◻

We now introduce an alternative, highly useful perspective on intersection numbers formulated by Snapper, which explicitly uses the coefficients of the Euler characteristic polynomial.

Definition 2.2 (Intersection Number via Polynomial Coefficients). Let X be a smooth projective variety, and let \mathcal{L}_1, \dots, \mathcal{L}_t be t invertible sheaves on X (t \ge 0). Let \mathcal{F} be a coherent sheaf on X such that \dim \operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F} \le t. The intersection number (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F}) = (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F})_X is defined as the coefficient of the monomial n_1 \cdots n_t in the polynomial \chi(\mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}).

Before proceeding, it is necessary to rigorously establish that this coefficient definition is completely consistent with our initial definition using the alternating sum of Euler characteristics, specifically in the case where t = n = \dim X and \mathcal{F} = \mathcal{O}_X.

Proposition 2.1 (Equivalence of Intersection Number Definitions). Let X be a smooth projective variety of dimension n, and let \mathcal{L}_1, \dots, \mathcal{L}_n be invertible sheaves on X. The coefficient of n_1 \cdots n_n in the polynomial P(n_1, \dots, n_n) = \chi(X, \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_n^{\otimes n_n}) is exactly the alternating sum: \sum_{I \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}} (-1)^{n - |I|} \chi\Big(X, \bigotimes_{i \in I} \mathcal{L}_i\Big).

Proof. By Snapper’s Polynomial Theorem, P(n_1, \dots, n_n) is a polynomial of total degree at most n. Thus, we may write it as P(n_1, \dots, n_n) = C n_1 n_2 \cdots n_n + Q(n_1, \dots, n_n), where C is the coefficient we wish to extract, and Q consists entirely of monomials of total degree at most n that strictly miss at least one variable n_k (since any monomial of degree n involving all n variables must be exactly n_1 \cdots n_n).

Consider the evaluation of the alternating sum on a general polynomial function F(n_1, \dots, n_n). Let \mathbf{e}_I \in \{0, 1\}^n be the indicator vector of the subset I, meaning its i-th coordinate is 1 if i \in I and 0 otherwise. The alternating sum is exactly \sum_{I} (-1)^{n - |I|} F(\mathbf{e}_I). For the target monomial F = n_1 \cdots n_n, the evaluation F(\mathbf{e}_I) is 1 if and only if I = \{1, \dots, n\}, and 0 otherwise. Thus, the alternating sum yields (-1)^0 \cdot 1 = 1. For any monomial in Q, there is some variable n_k that does not appear. The value of this monomial on \mathbf{e}_I depends only on the coordinates in I \setminus \{k\}. We can partition the subsets of \{1, \dots, n\} into pairs (I, I \cup \{k\}) where k \notin I. For each pair, the monomial evaluates to the same number, but the signs (-1)^{n - |I|} and (-1)^{n - (|I|+1)} are opposite, causing exact cancellation. Therefore, the alternating sum annihilates Q. Applying the alternating sum to P isolates exactly the coefficient C, proving the equivalence. ◻

With consistency established, we now explore the fundamental properties of the intersection number, relying heavily on the structure of numerical polynomials.

Lemma 2.1 (Basis for Numerical Polynomials). A polynomial F(n_1, \dots, n_t) is numerical if and only if it can be written in the form F(n_1, \dots, n_t) = \sum a(i_1, \dots, i_t) \binom{n_1 + i_1}{i_1} \cdots \binom{n_t + i_t}{i_t}. where the coefficients a(i_1, \dots, i_t) are integers, and i_1 \ge 0, \dots, i_t \ge 0. Here, for all n, \binom{n+i}{i} = \frac{(n+i)(n+i-1)\cdots(n+1)}{i(i-1)\cdots 1} if i > 0, = 1 if i = 0, and = 0 if i < 0.

Proof. This is a standard algebraic result. The binomial coefficients \binom{n+i}{i} map integers to integers and form a \mathbb{Q}-basis for polynomials in one variable. Taking tensor products of this basis gives a basis for polynomials in t variables. The integrality of the coefficients a(i_1, \dots, i_t) is both necessary and sufficient for the polynomial to be numerical, which can be shown by straightforward induction on the degree. ◻

An obvious but important fact is that the intersection number should be an integer.

Proposition 2.1 (Integrality of Intersection Numbers). The intersection number (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F}) is an integer.

Proof. By Snapper’s Polynomial Theorem, \chi(\mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}) is a numerical polynomial in n_1, \dots, n_t of degree s = \dim \operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F} \le t. By Lemma , it can be expanded with integer coefficients a(i_1, \dots, i_t). The term n_1 \cdots n_t can only arise from the basis elements where i_1 = 1, \dots, i_t = 1, since any higher index would increase the total degree beyond t, and any lower index would omit a variable. For i_j = 1, \binom{n_j+1}{1} = n_j + 1. Thus, the coefficient of n_1 \cdots n_t is exactly the integer a(1, \dots, 1). ◻

If a sheaf is supported only in very few parts, it is also easy to imagine that the number of intersections with other sheaves should be zero.

Proposition 2.1 (Vanishing for Small Support). (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F}) = 0 if \dim \operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F} < t, and (\mathcal{F}) = h^0(\mathcal{F}) if \dim \operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F} = t = 0.

Proof. If \dim \operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F} < t, then \chi(\mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}) is a polynomial of total degree strictly less than t. Consequently, it cannot contain a monomial of degree t, so the coefficient of n_1 \cdots n_t is identically 0.

If \dim \operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F} = t = 0, the "empty" intersection number (\mathcal{F}) is simply the constant term of the polynomial, which is evaluated at n_i = 0. This gives \chi(\mathcal{F}). Since \mathcal{F} is supported on a zero-dimensional scheme, its higher cohomology vanishes, yielding \chi(\mathcal{F}) = h^0(\mathcal{F}). ◻

The most important and essential property of the intersection number is that it is a bilinear form. This plays an important role in algebraic geometry.

Proposition 2.1 (Linearity and Symmetry). (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F}) is a symmetric t-linear form in the variables \mathcal{L}_1, \dots, \mathcal{L}_t.

Proof. Symmetry is immediate from the definition, as the tensor product of invertible sheaves is commutative, rendering the polynomial symmetric with respect to permutations of the index set. To demonstrate linearity in the first variable (and thus all variables by symmetry), let \mathcal{M} and \mathcal{N} be invertible sheaves. We evaluate the intersection number involving \mathcal{M} \otimes \mathcal{N}^{-1}. Consider the polynomial for the sheaves \mathcal{M}, \mathcal{N}^{-1}, \mathcal{L}_2, \dots, \mathcal{L}_t: \begin{align*} &\chi(\mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{M}^{\otimes m} \otimes (\mathcal{N}^{-1})^{\otimes n} \otimes \mathcal{L}_2^{\otimes n_2} \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}) \\ &= (\mathcal{M} \cdot \mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F}) m n_2 \cdots n_t - (\mathcal{N} \cdot \mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F}) n n_2 \cdots n_t + \cdots \end{align*} This expansion is justified by taking successively n=0 and m=0 to isolate the respective coefficients.

Now, set m = n = n_1 to represent the powers of the combined sheaf \mathcal{M} \otimes \mathcal{N}^{-1}. Substituting this yields: \begin{align*} &\chi(\mathcal{F} \otimes (\mathcal{M} \otimes \mathcal{N}^{-1})^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \mathcal{L}_2^{\otimes n_2} \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}) \\ &= \big( (\mathcal{M} \cdot \mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F}) - (\mathcal{N} \cdot \mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F}) \big) n_1 n_2 \cdots n_t + \cdots \end{align*} The coefficient of n_1 \cdots n_t on the left side is by definition ((\mathcal{M} \otimes \mathcal{N}^{-1}) \cdot \mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F}), confirming the linearity. ◻

Finally, we address the behavior of intersection numbers with respect to the sheaf argument \mathcal{F}.

Proposition 2.1 (Additivity on Exact Sequences). If 0 \to \mathcal{F}' \to \mathcal{F} \to \mathcal{F}'' \to 0 is an exact sequence of coherent sheaves, then (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F}) = (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F}') + (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F}'').

Proof. Because the functor - \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t} is exact (as we are tensoring with locally free sheaves), tensoring the given short exact sequence maintains exactness. The Euler-Poincaré characteristic is additive on short exact sequences, so we have the identity of polynomials: \chi(\mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}) = \chi(\mathcal{F}' \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}) + \chi(\mathcal{F}'' \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}). Extracting the coefficient of n_1 \cdots n_t from both sides of this equation immediately yields the desiUniBlue additivity formula. Note that exactness at stalks ensures that \dim \operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F} \le t if and only if both \dim \operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F}' \le t and \dim \operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F}'' \le t, keeping the definition well-posed. ◻

Remark 2.1 (Notation Convention). Let W \subset X be a closed subscheme of dimension less than or equal to t, and take \mathcal{F} = \mathcal{O}_W. For simplicity, it is common practice to write (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot W) in place of (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{O}_W). Furthermore, if W = X, the notation is abbreviated simply to (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t) to mean (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot X).

An essential geometric computation relates the intersection number involving an invertible sheaf to the intersection number on an effective divisor linearly equivalent to that sheaf.

Proposition 2.1 (Intersection with an Effective Divisor). If \Delta \in |\mathcal{L}_1|, representing the set of all effective divisors \Delta such that \mathcal{O}_X(\Delta) \simeq \mathcal{L}_1, and \Delta \cap \operatorname{Ass} \mathcal{F} = \emptyset, then (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdot \mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F}) = (\mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F}_\Delta). where \mathcal{F}_\Delta = \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{O}_\Delta. In particular, if \dim X \le t, then (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdot \mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t) = (\mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \Delta).

Proof. Because \Delta \cap \operatorname{Ass} \mathcal{F} = \emptyset, any local equation of the effective divisor \Delta at a point x \in X is not a zero divisor in the stalk \mathcal{F}_x. Consequently, tensoring the structural exact sequence of \Delta with \mathcal{F} and the invertible sheaves yields an exact sequence: 0 \to \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes(n_1-1)} \otimes \mathcal{L}_2^{\otimes n_2} \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t} \to \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \mathcal{L}_2^{\otimes n_2} \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t} \to \mathcal{F}_\Delta \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t} \to 0. Taking the Euler-Poincaré characteristics gives: \begin{align*} \chi(\mathcal{F}_\Delta \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}) &= \chi(\mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}) - \chi(\mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes(n_1-1)} \otimes \mathcal{L}_2^{\otimes n_2} \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}) \\ &= (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F})n_1 \cdots n_t + \cdots - (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F})(n_1 - 1)n_2 \cdots n_t + \cdots \\ &= (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F})n_2 \cdots n_t + \cdots \end{align*}. On the other hand, evaluating the characteristic of \mathcal{F}_\Delta explicitly at n_1 = 0 provides: \chi(\mathcal{F}_\Delta \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes 0} \otimes \mathcal{L}_2^{\otimes n_2} \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}) = (\mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F}_\Delta)n_2 \cdots n_t + \cdots. Comparing the coefficients of the monomial n_2 \cdots n_t from both approaches confirms the proposition. ◻

Proposition 2.1 (Restriction to Subschemes). Suppose we have the incidence of closed subschemes Y \subset W \subset X, where Y = \operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F}. Then (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F})_X = (\mathcal{L}_{1|W} \cdots \mathcal{L}_{t|W} \cdot \mathcal{F})_W. In particular, (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot W)_X = (\mathcal{L}_{1|W} \cdots \mathcal{L}_{t|W})_W.

Proof. Because Y is the support of \mathcal{F}, \mathcal{F} may be consideUniBlue an \mathcal{O}_W-module for any W containing Y. For any invertible sheaf \mathcal{L} on X, we have the canonical isomorphism \mathcal{F} \otimes_{\mathcal{O}_X} \mathcal{L} \simeq \mathcal{F} \otimes_{\mathcal{O}_X} \mathcal{O}_W \otimes_{\mathcal{O}_W} \mathcal{L} \simeq \mathcal{F} \otimes_{\mathcal{O}_W} \mathcal{L}_{|W}. Setting \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t} and taking Euler-Poincaré characteristics yields identical polynomials. Extracting the coefficient of n_1 \cdots n_t produces the desiUniBlue equality. ◻

Using the principles of additivity and restriction, intersection numbers can be naturally decomposed over the irreducible components of the support.

Corollary 2.1 (Decomposition over Irreducible Components). Suppose X = \operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F}. Let V_1, \dots, V_k be its irreducible components, and set \mathcal{F}_i = \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{O}_{V_i} for i = 1, \dots, k. Then (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F}) = (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F}_1) + \cdots + (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F}_k).

Proof. The canonical surjections \mathcal{O}_X \to \mathcal{O}_{V_j} give rise to a natural homomorphism of sheaves \mathcal{F} \to \coprod_{j} \mathcal{F}_j. Let \mathcal{K} be the kernel and \mathcal{H} be the cokernel, generating the exact sequence 0 \to \mathcal{K} \to \mathcal{F} \to \coprod_{j} \mathcal{F}_j \to \mathcal{H} \to 0. The supports of both \mathcal{K} and \mathcal{H} are strictly contained in the intersections of the components, namely \bigcup_{i \neq j} (V_i \cap V_j). Since the dimension of these intersections is strictly less than \dim X \le t, their corresponding intersection numbers vanish by Proposition . The result then follows immediately from the additivity of intersection numbers on exact sequences as established in Proposition . ◻

Corollary 2.1 (Multiplicity at the Generic Point). Suppose X is irreducible and \dim X \le t. Let x \in X be the generic point, and set the length l = \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal{O}_x} \mathcal{F}_x. Then (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F}) = l \cdot (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{O}_{X_{\operatorname{UniBlue}}}).

Proof. At the generic point, \mathcal{O}_x is an Artin local ring, and \mathcal{F}_x is a finite \mathcal{O}_x-module, ensuring the length l is well-defined and finite. The corollary is trivially true when \mathcal{F} = \mathcal{O}_{X_{\operatorname{UniBlue}}} (where l=1) and when \dim \operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F} < \dim X \le t (where both the intersection number and the length vanish). We complete the proof by dévissage. Let K' be the category of coherent sheaves \mathcal{F} for which the corollary holds. Because both the intersection number (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F}) and the length \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal{O}_x} \mathcal{F}_x are additive functions on exact sequences of coherent sheaves, K' is closed under extensions, making it an exact subcategory. Since it contains the structure sheaves of all integral closed subschemes, K' contains every coherent sheaf \mathcal{F} on X. ◻

Corollary 2.1 (Divisor on Subscheme). Let W be a t-dimensional closed subscheme. Suppose d \in |\mathcal{L}_{1|W}|. Then (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdot \mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot W) = (\mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot d).

Proof. We sequentially apply Proposition and Proposition : (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdot \mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot W)_X = (\mathcal{L}_{1|W} \cdot \mathcal{L}_{2|W} \cdots \mathcal{L}_{t|W})_W = (\mathcal{L}_{2|W} \cdots \mathcal{L}_{t|W} \cdot d)_W = (\mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot d)_X. This shows that intersection numbers are intrinsically invariant under restriction to the subscheme supporting the effective divisor. ◻

Before stating the next proposition, a brief but rigorous digression is requiUniBlue to construct and understand the concept of the degree of a morphism f: V' \to V between irreducible algebraic schemes.

Let x' \in V' and x \in V be the generic points of these irreducible schemes. Suppose that f(x') = x, meaning the morphism is dominant. Under this assumption, the local ring \mathcal{O}_x maps to \mathcal{O}_{x'} via the natural pullback of functions, endowing \mathcal{O}_{x'} with the structure of an \mathcal{O}_x-algebra. Because of this algebra structure, any \mathcal{O}_{x'}-module M can be consideUniBlue as an \mathcal{O}_x-module simply by restriction of scalars.

The lengths of M over these two respective local rings are intimately related by the degree of the residue field extension. Specifically, we have the length formula: \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal{O}_x} M = [k(x') : k(x)] \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal{O}_{x'}} M. From this formula, it follows that if M is a finite, non-zero \mathcal{O}_{x'}-module, then M is a finite \mathcal{O}_x-module if and only if the dimensions of the schemes are equal, i.e., \dim V' = \dim V. Finally, the topological condition f(x') = x holds if and only if \dim f(V') = \dim V. This allows us to formally define the mapping degree.

Definition 2.3 (Degree of a Morphism). Let f: V' \to V be a morphism of irreducible algebraic schemes with generic points x' and x, respectively. The degree of f is defined as the following rational number: \deg f = \begin{cases} \frac{\operatorname{length}_{\mathcal{O}_x} \mathcal{O}_{x'}}{\operatorname{length}_{\mathcal{O}_x} \mathcal{O}_x} & \text{if } \dim V' = \dim V = \dim f(V') \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}.

To solidify this definition, consider the following three canonical examples:

Example 2.1 (Typical Morphism Degrees).

  1. Let f: V_{\operatorname{UniBlue}} \hookrightarrow V be the canonical inclusion morphism of the reduced induced subscheme. The generic points coincide, so k(x') = k(x) and \mathcal{O}_{x'} = \mathcal{O}_{x, \operatorname{UniBlue}}. Let l = \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal{O}_x} \mathcal{O}_x. The numerator evaluates to 1, yielding \deg f = 1/l.

  2. Let f: V' \to V be a birational morphism; that is, the map induces an isomorphism on the generic local rings, \mathcal{O}_x \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathcal{O}_{x'}. In this case, the lengths are identical, so \deg f = 1. Conversely, if V is reduced (so the denominator is 1), then \deg f = 1 implies f is birational.

  3. Let f: V' \to V with \deg f > 0, and suppose both V' and V are reduced schemes. Since they are reduced, the local rings at the generic points are fields, making \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal{O}_{x'}} \mathcal{O}_{x'} = 1 and \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal{O}_x} \mathcal{O}_x = 1. By the length formula, the numerator becomes [k(x') : k(x)] \cdot 1, and thus \deg f = [k(x') : k(x)].

The degree is multiplicative under composition, provided we are careful about dimensions.

Lemma 2.1 (Multiplicativity of Degree). Let f: V' \to V, g: V'' \to V', and h = f \circ g: V'' \to V be morphisms between irreducible algebraic schemes. Then \deg h = (\deg f)(\deg g) unless \dim V' > \dim V'' = \dim V = \dim h(V'').

Proof. Suppose \deg h > 0. By definition, this requires \dim V'' = \dim V = \dim h(V''). Because h(V'') = f(g(V'')) \subseteq f(V') \subseteq V, we have the dimensional inequalities: \dim V'' = \dim V = \dim h(V'') \le \dim g(V''), \dim f(V') \le \dim V'. Consequently, both \deg f and \deg g must be strictly greater than 0 unless we fall into the pathological case where \dim V' > \dim V'' = \dim V = \dim h(V'). Conversely, suppose both \deg f > 0 and \deg g > 0. This immediately implies \deg h > 0. Letting x'' be the generic point of V'', we can expand the length using the field extensions: \begin{align*} \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal{O}_x} \mathcal{O}_{x''} &= [k(x') : k(x)] \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal{O}_{x'}} \mathcal{O}_{x''} \\ &= [k(x') : k(x)] \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal{O}_{x'}} \mathcal{O}_{x'} \cdot \deg g \\ &= \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal{O}_x} \mathcal{O}_{x'} \cdot \deg g \end{align*}. Dividing both sides by \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal{O}_x} \mathcal{O}_x yields \deg h = (\deg f)(\deg g), completing the proof. ◻

We are now equipped to state and prove the fundamental relationship between the pullback of invertible sheaves along a morphism and the intersection number.

Proposition 2.1 (Intersection Number under Morphisms). Let f: V' \to V be a morphism of irreducible algebraic schemes, and assume t \ge \dim V', \dim V. Let \mathcal{L}_1, \dots, \mathcal{L}_t be t invertible sheaves on V, and set their pullbacks as \mathcal{L}_1' = f^* \mathcal{L}_1, \dots, \mathcal{L}_t' = f^* \mathcal{L}_t. Then (\mathcal{L}_1' \cdots \mathcal{L}_t')_{V'} = \deg f \cdot (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t)_V.

Proof. The proof proceeds rigorously in five steps. The first three steps rely heavily on the Leray spectral sequence for the morphism f, which provides a cohomological tool to compute the Euler characteristic on V' via data pushed forward to V. Specifically, we use the fact that: \begin{align*} \chi(\mathcal{L}_1^{\prime \otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\prime \otimes n_t}) &= \sum_q (-1)^q \chi\Big(R^q f_*(\mathcal{O}_{V'} \otimes f^* \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes f^* \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t})\Big) \\ &= \sum_q (-1)^q \chi\Big((R^q f_* \mathcal{O}_{V'}) \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}\Big) \end{align*} where the second equality follows from the projection formula. The intersection number (\mathcal{L}_1' \cdots \mathcal{L}_t')_{V'} is exactly the coefficient of n_1 \cdots n_t in this alternating sum of polynomials.

Step 1. The case where \deg f = 0. By definition, if \deg f = 0, then \dim f(V') < t. The higher direct image sheaves R^q f_* \mathcal{O}_{V'} are supported entirely within f(V'), meaning \dim \operatorname{supp}R^q f_* \mathcal{O}_{V'} < t for all q. Consequently, each Euler characteristic \chi((R^q f_* \mathcal{O}_{V'}) \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}) is a polynomial in n_1, \dots, n_t of total degree strictly less than t. Their alternating sum, \chi(\mathcal{L}_1^{\prime \otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\prime \otimes n_t}), must also be of degree less than t. Therefore, its homogeneous component of degree t vanishes, yielding (\mathcal{L}_1' \cdots \mathcal{L}_t')_{V'} = 0 = \deg f \cdot (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t)_V.

Step 2. The case where f is birational. Here, f induces an isomorphism between a dense open set of V' and a dense open set of V. For any higher derived functor (q \neq 0), the support of R^q f_* \mathcal{O}_{V'} must avoid this open set, meaning \dim \operatorname{supp}R^q f_* \mathcal{O}_{V'} < \dim V \le t. Thus, the higher terms in the Leray spectral sequence do not contribute to the degree t homogeneous component. The homogeneous component of degree t of \chi(\mathcal{L}_1^{\prime \otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\prime \otimes n_t}) is identically the same as that of \chi((f_* \mathcal{O}_{V'}) \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}). Furthermore, considering the canonical map \alpha: \mathcal{O}_V \to f_* \mathcal{O}_{V'}, we obtain an exact sequence 0 \to \mathcal{K} \to \mathcal{O}_V \xrightarrow{\alpha} f_* \mathcal{O}_{V'} \to \mathcal{H} \to 0. Because f is birational, \alpha is bijective on a dense open set, forcing both \dim \operatorname{supp}\mathcal{K} < t and \dim \operatorname{supp}\mathcal{H} < t. Therefore, the degree t component of \chi((f_* \mathcal{O}_{V'}) \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}) is the same as that of \chi(\mathcal{L}_1^{\otimes n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathcal{L}_t^{\otimes n_t}). Putting these facts together, we obtain (\mathcal{L}_1' \cdots \mathcal{L}_t')_{V'} = (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t)_V, matching \deg f = 1.

Step 3. The case where f is finite, \deg f > 0, and both V' and V are reduced. Because f is a finite morphism, it is affine. Affine morphisms have no higher cohomology for quasi-coherent sheaves, meaning R^q f_* \mathcal{O}_{V'} = 0 for all q \neq 0. Thus, the Leray sequence collapses to the single term q=0, giving precisely (\mathcal{L}_1' \cdots \mathcal{L}_t')_{V'} = (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot f_* \mathcal{O}_{V'})_V. Since V is irreducible and reduced, we apply the generic length evaluation (established in previous corollaries) to the sheaf f_* \mathcal{O}_{V'} at the generic point x. We compute the length multiplier l = \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal{O}_x} (f_* \mathcal{O}_{V'})_x = [k(x') : k(x)] = \deg f (since V' and V are reduced). Hence, the intersection number becomes \deg f \cdot (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t)_V.

Step 4. The case where V' = V_{\operatorname{UniBlue}} and f: V_{\operatorname{UniBlue}} \hookrightarrow V is the natural inclusion. This follows directly from the generic length computation on reduced structures. By our previous examples and corollaries, (\mathcal{L}_1' \cdots \mathcal{L}_t')_{V'} = \deg f \cdot (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t)_V holds intrinsically.

As an immediate geometric application of Proposition , we can recover the classical result concerning the degree of a pullback divisor under a finite morphism of curves.

Corollary 2.1 (Degree of Pullback Divisor). Let f: X' \to X be a surjective morphism between smooth projective curves. Let D be a divisor on X, and f^*D be its pullback to X'. Then the degree of the pullback divisor is the product of the mapping degree and the degree of the original divisor: \deg(f^* D) = \deg f \cdot \deg D.

Proof. For a smooth projective curve X, the degree of a divisor D is classically defined and equivalent to the intersection number of its associated invertible sheaf \mathcal{O}_X(D) evaluated on the fundamental class of the curve; that is, \deg D = (\mathcal{O}_X(D) \cdot X). Applying Proposition with t=1 and \mathcal{L}_1 = \mathcal{O}_X(D), the pullback sheaf is \mathcal{L}_1' = f^* \mathcal{O}_X(D) \simeq \mathcal{O}_{X'}(f^* D). The proposition immediately yields: \deg(f^* D) = (\mathcal{O}_{X'}(f^* D) \cdot X') = (f^* \mathcal{L}_1)_{X'} = \deg f \cdot (\mathcal{L}_1)_X = \deg f \cdot \deg D. ◻

We conclude this section by establishing the universal property of the intersection number. The explicit formula via Euler characteristics is uniquely enforced by the geometric and algebraic properties we have deduced.

Proposition 2.1 (Uniqueness of the Intersection Symbol). The intersection number symbol (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F})_X is uniquely determined by the properties stated in Propositions 1 through 6.

Proof. Let \langle \mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F} \rangle_X be another arbitrary symbol satisfying the properties of Propositions 1-6. Because our previous corollaries (Decomposition, Generic Length, Divisor on Subscheme) were derived as formal, logical consequences of Propositions 1-6 alone, this new symbol \langle \cdots \rangle_X must also satisfy these corollaries.

We wish to prove that (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F})_X = \langle \mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot \mathcal{F} \rangle_X. To do so, we systematically reduce the general case to a simpler one using the shaUniBlue properties:

  1. Proposition (Restriction) implies that we may assume X = \operatorname{supp}\mathcal{F} without loss of generality.

  2. Corollary (Decomposition) implies that we may assume X is an irreducible scheme.

  3. Corollary (Multiplicity at Generic Point) implies that we may assume X is reduced (and therefore an integral variety), and that the sheaf is simply the structure sheaf \mathcal{F} = \mathcal{O}_X.

  4. Finally, an application of Chow’s Lemma combined with Proposition (Pullback under Morphisms) implies that we may assume X is a projective variety. (Note that in our global convention this is already assumed, but structurally the uniqueness holds under this reduction).

Thus, it suffices to show that (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot W)_X = \langle \mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot W \rangle_X where W is a reducible subvariety of X, which we prove by induction on t.

Base Case (t = 0): When t = 0, both symbols must evaluate to the number of points in W (or h^0(\mathcal{O}_W)) by Proposition . Thus they are equal.

Inductive Step (t > 0): Suppose the equality holds for t-1. Because X is projective, any invertible sheaf can be expressed as the difference of two very ample effective divisors. We write \mathcal{L}_1 \simeq \mathcal{O}_X(C - H), where C and H are suitable very ample effective divisors. Set the corresponding sheaves as \mathcal{C} = \mathcal{O}_X(C) and \mathcal{H} = \mathcal{O}_X(H). We then compute: \begin{align*} (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdot \mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot W) &= (\mathcal{C} \cdot \mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot W) - (\mathcal{H} \cdot \mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot W) & \text{by Proposition \ref{prop:int_multilinear}} \\ &= (\mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot C \cap W) - (\mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot H \cap W) & \text{by Proposition \ref{prop:int_effective}} \\ &= \langle \mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot C \cap W \rangle - \langle \mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot H \cap W \rangle & \text{by the induction hypothesis} \\ &= \langle \mathcal{C} \cdot \mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot W \rangle - \langle \mathcal{H} \cdot \mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot W \rangle & \text{by Proposition \ref{prop:int_effective} for } \langle \cdot \rangle \\ &= \langle \mathcal{L}_1 \cdot \mathcal{L}_2 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot W \rangle & \text{by Proposition \ref{prop:int_multilinear} for } \langle \cdot \rangle. \end{align*} This completes the induction and the proof of uniqueness. ◻

The dévissage argument utilized in the latter half of the preceding proof, decomposing the sheaf computation into successive intersections with effective divisors, provides a direct link to the classical geometric formulation of intersection theory.

Corollary 2.1 (Agreement with Classical Intersection). Suppose that X is a non-singular projective variety, that \mathcal{L}_1 = \mathcal{O}_X(D_1), \dots, \mathcal{L}_t = \mathcal{O}_X(D_t) where D_1, \dots, D_t are divisors, and that W is a reducible subvariety. Then our Euler-characteristic based intersection number agrees with the classical intersection symbol: (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdots \mathcal{L}_t \cdot W)_X = i(D_1 \cdots D_t \cdot W; X) the classical intersection number when the latter is properly defined through transversality.

To avoid the heavy homological algebra requiUniBlue for the multiple intersections of arbitrary divisors (which necessitates the full theory of Koszul complexes), this section focuses explicitly on the intersection of two effective divisors. This restricted case provides a foundational and geometrically intuitive approach before advancing to higher dimensions.

First, it is essential to establish the conditions under which the intersection of two divisors is well-behaved locally.

Definition 2.4 (Coprime Local Parameters and Transversality). Let X be a smooth projective variety. Let C and D be two effective divisors on X. At any point x \in X, let C and D be defined by local equations f \in \mathcal{O}_{X,x} and g \in \mathcal{O}_{X,x}, respectively.

  • C and D are said to have no common components if, for every point x \in C \cap D, the local parameters f and g are coprime in the unique factorization domain \mathcal{O}_{X,x}.

  • C and D intersect transversely at x if their local equations generate the maximal ideal, i.e., (f, g) = \mathfrak{m}_x.

When two divisors share no common components, their intersection defines a subscheme C \cap D of codimension 2. In the specific case where \dim X = 2 (a surface), or more generally when the intersection is restricted to a zero-dimensional locus, the global intersection number can be computed by summing the lengths of local modules.

Definition 2.5 (Intersection Multiplicity and Global Number). Let X be a smooth projective variety over a field k. Let C and D be effective divisors intersecting in a finite number of points. For a point x \in C \cap D with local equations f, g \in \mathcal{O}_{X,x}, the intersection multiplicity at x is defined as the length of the corresponding local quotient ring: m_x(C \cdot D) = \operatorname{length}_{\mathcal{O}_{X,x}} (\mathcal{O}_{X,x} / (f, g)) . The global intersection number is defined as the sum of these multiplicities, weighted by the degree of the field extensions: (C \cdot D) = \sum_{x \in C \cap D} m_x(C \cdot D) [k(x) : k].

This ideal-theoretic definition translates seamlessly into global sheaf-theoretic data.

Lemma 2.1 (Length as Dimension of Global Sections). The global intersection number (C \cdot D) is identically the dimension of the space of global sections of the structure sheaf of the intersection subscheme: (C \cdot D) = \dim_k \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_{C \cap D}) .

Proof. By the definition of the structure sheaf of the intersection, the stalk at x is \mathcal{O}_{C \cap D, x} = (\mathcal{O}_X / (\mathcal{O}_X(-C) + \mathcal{O}_X(-D)))_x. Thus, the local length evaluates exactly to \operatorname{length} (\mathcal{O}_{C \cap D, x}). Because C \cap D is a zero-dimensional scheme, its global sections decompose into the direct product of its stalks. The sum of the lengths, scaled by the field extension degrees [k(x) : k], directly computes the total vector space dimension over k, yielding \dim_k \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_{C \cap D}). ◻

Finally, we demonstrate that this ideal-theoretic definition of the intersection number is completely consistent with the definition formulated in the previous section using the Euler characteristics of invertible sheaves.

Proposition 2.1 (Consistency with Sheaf-Theoretic Intersection). For effective divisors C and D with no common components, the divisor intersection number coincides with the invertible sheaf intersection number: (C \cdot D) = (\mathcal{O}_X(C) \cdot \mathcal{O}_X(D)).

Proof. Let s \in \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X(C)) and s' \in \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X(D)) be the canonical sections defining the effective divisors C and D. We construct the following sequence of coherent sheaves: 0 \to \mathcal{O}_X(-C-D) \to \mathcal{O}_X(-C) \oplus \mathcal{O}_X(-D) \to \mathcal{O}_X \to \mathcal{O}_{C \cap D} \to 0 . The maps are defined locally as follows: the first non-trivial map sends a section r to (rs', -rs), and the second map sends (u, v) to us + vs'. The composition maps r to rs's - rss' = 0.

We must verify the exactness of this sequence at the stalks. Locally at a point x, this reduces to evaluating the sequence of free modules: 0 \to \mathcal{O}_{X,x} \to \mathcal{O}_{X,x}^2 \to \mathcal{O}_{X,x} \to \mathcal{O}_{C \cap D, x} \to 0 . The first map is given by k \mapsto (kg, -kf), and the second by (a, b) \mapsto af + bg. The composition is trivially zero because kgf - kfg = 0. To prove exactness in the middle, suppose a, b \in \mathcal{O}_{X,x} are such that af + bg = 0. Because C and D have no common components, their local equations f and g are coprime. Since \mathcal{O}_{X,x} is a UFD (due to the smoothness of X), f dividing bg implies f must divide b. Thus, b = -kf and consequently a = kg for some k \in \mathcal{O}_{X,x}, proving that the kernel is exactly the image of the preceding map.

With exactness established, we invoke the additivity of the Euler characteristic across exact sequences. Because \mathcal{O}_{C \cap D} is supported on a zero-dimensional scheme, its Euler characteristic is simply its global dimension: \chi(\mathcal{O}_{C \cap D}) = \dim_k \operatorname{H}^0(X, \mathcal{O}_{C \cap D}) = (C \cdot D). Taking the alternating sum of the Euler characteristics for the exact sequence yields: \chi(\mathcal{O}_{C \cap D}) = \chi(\mathcal{O}_X) - \chi(\mathcal{O}_X(-C)) - \chi(\mathcal{O}_X(-D)) + \chi(\mathcal{O}_X(-C-D)) . By the definition of the intersection number of invertible sheaves formulated via the Euler-Poincaré characteristic, the right-hand side is precisely the intersection number of the corresponding line bundles. Therefore, we conclude (C \cdot D) = (\mathcal{O}_X(C) \cdot \mathcal{O}_X(D)), confirming the consistency of the two approaches. ◻

Bézout’s Theorem in Projective Space

We now record the intersection-theoretic form of Bézout’s theorem. Although it is not logically a consequence of Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch, it follows very naturally from the Chow ring of projective space and the degree map.

Let \mathbb{P}^n=\mathbb{P}^n_k, \qquad h=\operatorname{c}_1(\mathcal O_{\mathbb{P}^n}(1)). Recall that \begin{equation} \operatorname{CH}^*(\mathbb{P}^n) \cong \mathbb Z[h]/(h^{n+1}). \end{equation} In particular, \operatorname{CH}^r(\mathbb{P}^n) = \mathbb Z h^r, \qquad 0\leq r\leq n.

Geometrically, if L\simeq\mathbb{P}^{n-r}\subset\mathbb{P}^n is a linear subspace of codimension r, then [L]=h^r.

Lemma 2.1 (Cycle class and degree in projective space). Let Z\subset\mathbb{P}^n be an integral closed subscheme of dimension d, or equivalently of codimension c=n-d. Then \begin{equation} [Z] = \deg(Z)\,h^c \qquad \text{in } \operatorname{CH}^c(\mathbb{P}^n). \end{equation} More generally, if \alpha\in\operatorname{CH}_d(\mathbb{P}^n) is any d-dimensional cycle, then \alpha = \deg(\alpha)\,h^{n-d}.

Proof. Since \operatorname{CH}^c(\mathbb{P}^n)=\mathbb Zh^c, there exists an integer m such that [Z]=mh^c. It remains to identify m.

Multiply both sides by h^d=h^{n-c}. Then [Z]\cdot h^d = mh^n. Since \deg(h^n)=1, taking degrees gives \deg([Z]\cdot h^d)=m.

But the degree of a d-dimensional projective scheme Z is intersection-theoretically characterized by \deg Z = \deg \left( [Z]\cdot h^d \right). Equivalently, if H_1,\dots,H_d are sufficiently general hyperplanes, then the proper intersection Z\cap H_1\cap\cdots\cap H_d is zero-dimensional and \deg Z = \deg \left( [Z]\cdot[H_1]\cdots[H_d] \right). Since every hyperplane has class h, this is precisely \deg([Z]h^d). Hence m=\deg Z, and therefore [Z]=\deg(Z)h^c.

The statement for arbitrary cycles follows by linearity. ◻

Theorem 2.1 (Bézout’s theorem in \mathbb{P}^n). Let X,Y\subset\mathbb{P}^n_k be pure-dimensional closed subschemes of codimensions p and q, respectively. Assume p+q\leq n and that X and Y intersect properly, i.e. every irreducible component W\subset X\cap Y has codimension \operatorname{codim}_{\mathbb{P}^n}W=p+q. Then \begin{equation} \boxed{ \deg(X\cdot Y) = \deg(X)\deg(Y) }. \end{equation}

More explicitly, the proper intersection cycle is \begin{equation} X\cdot Y = \sum_{W\in\mathrm{Irr}(X\cap Y)} i(W;X,Y;\mathbb{P}^n)[W], \end{equation} and hence \begin{equation} \boxed{ \sum_{W\in\mathrm{Irr}(X\cap Y)} i(W;X,Y;\mathbb{P}^n)\deg W = \deg X\deg Y. } \end{equation}

Here i(W;X,Y;\mathbb{P}^n) denotes the local intersection multiplicity of X and Y along W.

Proof. Since \mathbb{P}^n is smooth, its Chow groups carry an intersection product \operatorname{CH}^p(\mathbb{P}^n)\times\operatorname{CH}^q(\mathbb{P}^n) \longrightarrow \operatorname{CH}^{p+q}(\mathbb{P}^n).

Put a=\deg X, \qquad b=\deg Y. By Lemma , [X]=ah^p and [Y]=bh^q. Therefore \begin{align} [X]\cdot[Y] &= (ah^p)(bh^q)\notag\\ &= ab\,h^{p+q}. \end{align}

Because X and Y meet properly, the intersection product is represented by the proper intersection cycle X\cdot Y = \sum_W i(W;X,Y;\mathbb{P}^n)[W], where W runs through the irreducible components of X\cap Y.

Every such W has dimension \dim W = n-p-q. Set d=n-p-q. To compute the degree of the intersection cycle, multiply by h^d. We obtain \begin{align*} [X]\cdot[Y]\cdot h^d &= ab\,h^{p+q+d}\\ &= ab\,h^n. \end{align*} Taking degrees and using \deg(h^n)=1 gives \deg(X\cdot Y) = ab = \deg X\deg Y. This proves .

On the other hand, by linearity of the degree map, \begin{align*} \deg(X\cdot Y) &= \deg \left( \sum_W i(W;X,Y;\mathbb{P}^n)[W] \right)\\ &= \sum_W i(W;X,Y;\mathbb{P}^n)\deg W. \end{align*} Combining this with \deg(X\cdot Y) = \deg X\deg Y gives \sum_W i(W;X,Y;\mathbb{P}^n)\deg W = \deg X\deg Y. This is . ◻

Remark 2.1 (Local intersection multiplicity). Because \mathbb{P}^n is nonsingular, the local ring \mathcal O_{\mathbb{P}^n,\eta_W} at the generic point \eta_W of an irreducible component W is a regular local ring. Under the proper-intersection hypothesis, the intersection multiplicity may be expressed by Serre’s formula \begin{equation} i(W;X,Y;\mathbb{P}^n) = \sum_{j\geq0} (-1)^j \mathrm{length}_{\mathcal O_{\mathbb{P}^n,\eta_W}} \mathrm{Tor}_j^{\mathcal O_{\mathbb{P}^n,\eta_W}} \left( \mathcal O_{X,\eta_W}, \mathcal O_{Y,\eta_W} \right). \end{equation} Thus Bézout’s theorem counts components with their scheme-theoretic intersection multiplicities rather than merely counting the underlying points or components.

Corollary 2.1 (Zero-dimensional Bézout theorem). Let X,Y\subset\mathbb{P}^n_k be pure-dimensional closed subschemes such that \dim X+\dim Y=n and such that X\cap Y is zero-dimensional. Then \begin{equation} \sum_{x\in X\cap Y} i(x;X,Y;\mathbb{P}^n) [\kappa(x):k] = \deg X\deg Y. \end{equation}

If k is algebraically closed, then [\kappa(x):k]=1 for every closed point x, and therefore \begin{equation} \boxed{ \sum_{x\in X\cap Y} i(x;X,Y;\mathbb{P}^n) = \deg X\deg Y. } \end{equation}

Proof. Since the intersection is zero-dimensional, its irreducible components are precisely its closed points. Hence Theorem  gives X\cdot Y = \sum_{x\in X\cap Y} i(x;X,Y;\mathbb{P}^n)[x]. The degree of a closed point is \deg[x]=[\kappa(x):k], so \deg(X\cdot Y) = \sum_{x\in X\cap Y} i(x;X,Y;\mathbb{P}^n) [\kappa(x):k]. Bézout’s theorem gives \deg(X\cdot Y)=\deg X\deg Y, which proves the result. ◻

Corollary 2.1 (Bézout for hypersurfaces in \mathbb{P}^n). Let F_1,\dots,F_r \in k[x_0,\dots,x_n] be nonzero homogeneous polynomials of degrees d_1,\dots,d_r, and put D_i=V(F_i)\subset\mathbb{P}^n. Assume that the hypersurfaces meet properly, so that \operatorname{codim}_{\mathbb{P}^n} (D_1\cap\cdots\cap D_r) = r. Then their intersection cycle has degree \begin{equation} \boxed{ \deg(D_1\cdots D_r) = d_1d_2\cdots d_r. } \end{equation}

Proof. A homogeneous polynomial F_i of degree d_i determines a section of the line bundle \mathcal O_{\mathbb{P}^n}(d_i). Its zero divisor is D_i. Therefore [D_i] = \operatorname{c}_1 \left( \mathcal O_{\mathbb{P}^n}(d_i) \right). Since \mathcal O_{\mathbb{P}^n}(d_i) \simeq \mathcal O_{\mathbb{P}^n}(1)^{\otimes d_i}, we have \operatorname{c}_1 \left( \mathcal O_{\mathbb{P}^n}(d_i) \right) = d_i \operatorname{c}_1 \left( \mathcal O_{\mathbb{P}^n}(1) \right) = d_ih. Thus [D_i]=d_ih.

Consequently \begin{align*} [D_1]\cdots[D_r] &= (d_1h)\cdots(d_rh)\\ &= d_1\cdots d_r\,h^r. \end{align*} The intersection has dimension n-r. Multiplying by h^{n-r} gives [D_1]\cdots[D_r]h^{n-r} = d_1\cdots d_r\,h^n. Taking degrees yields \deg(D_1\cdots D_r) = d_1\cdots d_r, as claimed. ◻

Corollary 2.1 (Classical Bézout theorem for n hypersurfaces). Let F_1,\dots,F_n \in k[x_0,\dots,x_n] be homogeneous polynomials of degrees d_1,\dots,d_n. Suppose that V(F_1,\dots,F_n)\subset\mathbb{P}^n is zero-dimensional. Then \begin{equation} \sum_{x\in V(F_1,\dots,F_n)} i_x(F_1,\dots,F_n) [\kappa(x):k] = d_1d_2\cdots d_n. \end{equation}

If k is algebraically closed, then \begin{equation} \boxed{ \sum_{x\in V(F_1,\dots,F_n)} i_x(F_1,\dots,F_n) = d_1d_2\cdots d_n. } \end{equation}

In particular, if all intersections are transverse, so that every local intersection multiplicity equals 1, then \# V(F_1,\dots,F_n) = d_1d_2\cdots d_n.

Proof. Apply Corollary  with r=n. Since the intersection has codimension n, it is zero-dimensional, and therefore D_1\cdots D_n = \sum_x i_x(F_1,\dots,F_n)[x]. Taking degrees gives \sum_x i_x(F_1,\dots,F_n) [\kappa(x):k] = d_1\cdots d_n. If k is algebraically closed, every residue field equals k and the residue-field factors disappear.

Finally, if the hypersurfaces meet transversely at every intersection point, then i_x(F_1,\dots,F_n)=1 for every x, so the sum of the multiplicities is simply the number of intersection points. ◻

Example 2.1 (The plane-curve form of Bézout). Let C=V(F), \qquad D=V(G) \subset\mathbb{P}^2_k be plane curves of degrees d and e, respectively. Assume that C and D have no common irreducible component. Then their intersection is zero-dimensional and [C]=dh, \qquad [D]=eh. Hence [C]\cdot[D] = de\,h^2. Since \deg(h^2)=1, we obtain \sum_{x\in C\cap D} i(x;C,D;\mathbb{P}^2) [\kappa(x):k] = de. Over an algebraically closed field this becomes the familiar formula \boxed{ \sum_{x\in C\cap D} i(x;C,D;\mathbb{P}^2) = de. } Thus the classical theorem for plane curves is exactly the two-dimensional special case of Theorem .
