Graded Ideals and the Projective Spectrum

Throughout this section, all rings are commutative with identity, and R=\bigoplus_{d\in\mathbb N}R_d is an \mathbb N-graded ring, where \mathbb N=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}. Thus R_dR_e\subseteq R_{d+e} for all d,e\in\mathbb N. We regard R as a graded R-module over itself.

Graded ideals

Definition 1.1. An ideal \mathfrak a\subseteq R is called a graded ideal, or a homogeneous ideal, if it is a graded R-submodule of the graded module R. Equivalently, there exist additive subgroups \mathfrak a_d\subseteq R_d such that \mathfrak a=\bigoplus_{d\in\mathbb N}\mathfrak a_d. In this case one necessarily has \mathfrak a_d=\mathfrak a\cap R_d.

A prime ideal \mathfrak p\subsetneq R is called a graded prime ideal, or a homogeneous prime ideal, if \mathfrak p is graded as an ideal. Thus a graded prime ideal is an ordinary prime ideal which is, in addition, a graded submodule of R.

Proposition 1.1 (Equivalent characterizations of graded ideals). Let \mathfrak a\subseteq R be an ideal. The following conditions are equivalent.

  1. \mathfrak a is a graded ideal.

  2. One has an internal direct-sum decomposition \mathfrak a =\bigoplus_{d\in\mathbb N}(\mathfrak a\cap R_d).

  3. The ideal \mathfrak a is generated by homogeneous elements of R.

  4. Whenever f\in\mathfrak a has homogeneous decomposition f=\sum_{d\in\mathbb N}f_d, f_d\in R_d, every homogeneous component f_d belongs to \mathfrak a.

Proof. The equivalence of (i) and (ii) is precisely the condition that \mathfrak a be a graded submodule of the graded module R.

Assume (ii). Every element of \mathfrak a is then a finite sum of elements belonging to the groups \mathfrak a\cap R_d. Hence \mathfrak a is generated, as an ideal, by the homogeneous elements contained in \mathfrak a. This proves (iii).

Assume (iii), and choose homogeneous generators \{g_\lambda\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda} for \mathfrak a. Let f\in\mathfrak a. We may write f=\sum_{\lambda\in F}a_\lambda g_\lambda for some finite subset F\subseteq\Lambda. Decompose each coefficient as a_\lambda=\sum_{e\in\mathbb N}a_{\lambda,e}, a_{\lambda,e}\in R_e. If g_\lambda has degree m_\lambda, then the degree-d component of f is f_d =\sum_{\substack{\lambda\in F,\ e\in\mathbb N\\ e+m_\lambda=d}} a_{\lambda,e}g_\lambda. Every summand belongs to \mathfrak a, and therefore f_d\in\mathfrak a. Thus (iv) holds.

Finally, assume (iv). Every f\in\mathfrak a is a sum of homogeneous elements f_d\in\mathfrak a\cap R_d, so \mathfrak a=\sum_{d\in\mathbb N}(\mathfrak a\cap R_d). The sum is direct because the decomposition of an element of R into homogeneous components is unique. Hence (ii) holds. ◻

Corollary 1.1. If f_\lambda\in R is homogeneous for every \lambda\in\Lambda, then (f_\lambda\mid \lambda\in\Lambda) is a graded ideal.

Proof. The displayed ideal is generated by homogeneous elements, so the result follows from Proposition . ◻

Proposition 1.1 (Testing primeness on homogeneous elements). Let \mathfrak p\subsetneq R be a graded ideal. Then \mathfrak p is a prime ideal if and only if, for all homogeneous elements f,g\in R, fg\in\mathfrak p \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f\in\mathfrak p\ \text{ or }\ g\in\mathfrak p.

Proof. The forward implication follows immediately from ordinary primeness.

Conversely, suppose that the displayed condition holds for homogeneous elements. Let f=\sum_{d\geq 0}f_d, g=\sum_{e\geq 0}g_e be elements not belonging to \mathfrak p. Because \mathfrak p is graded, Proposition  shows that some homogeneous component of f, and some homogeneous component of g, lie outside \mathfrak p. Let r=\min\{d\mid f_d\notin\mathfrak p\}, s=\min\{e\mid g_e\notin\mathfrak p\}. The degree-(r+s) component of fg is (fg)_{r+s}=\sum_{d+e=r+s}f_dg_e. Every term other than f_rg_s belongs to \mathfrak p: indeed, if d<r, then f_d\in\mathfrak p, while if d>r, then necessarily e<s and hence g_e\in\mathfrak p. By hypothesis, f_rg_s\notin\mathfrak p. Therefore (fg)_{r+s}\notin\mathfrak p, so fg\notin\mathfrak p. It follows that \mathfrak p is prime. ◻

Quotients of graded modules

Let M=\bigoplus_{d\in\mathbb Z}M_d be a graded R-module and let N=\bigoplus_{d\in\mathbb Z}N_d\subseteq M be a graded submodule. Here N_d=N\cap M_d.

Proposition 1.1 (Canonical grading on a quotient). The quotient module M/N has a canonical grading given by (M/N)_d\coloneqq (M_d+N)/N. Moreover, the natural map M_d\to M/N induces a canonical isomorphism (M/N)_d \cong \frac{M_d}{M_d\cap N} =\frac{M_d}{N_d}. Consequently, M/N=\bigoplus_{d\in\mathbb Z}(M/N)_d.

Proof. Every element m\in M has a finite homogeneous decomposition m=\sum_d m_d. Hence its class in M/N is the sum of the classes of the m_d, proving that the indicated homogeneous pieces generate M/N.

Suppose that a finite sum of homogeneous classes vanishes: \sum_d(m_d+N)=0, m_d\in M_d. Then \sum_d m_d\in N. Since N is graded, every homogeneous component m_d belongs to N. Thus each m_d+N is zero, and the sum is direct.

Finally, the map M_d\longrightarrow (M_d+N)/N, m_d\longmapsto m_d+N, is surjective and has kernel M_d\cap N=N_d. The first isomorphism theorem gives the asserted canonical isomorphism. ◻

Corollary 1.1. If \mathfrak a\subseteq R is a graded ideal, then R/\mathfrak a is a graded ring with (R/\mathfrak a)_d = (R_d+\mathfrak a)/\mathfrak a \cong R_d/(\mathfrak a\cap R_d). If \mathfrak a\subseteq\mathfrak b are graded ideals, then \mathfrak b/\mathfrak a is a graded ideal of R/\mathfrak a, and (\mathfrak b/\mathfrak a)_d \cong \mathfrak b_d/\mathfrak a_d.

Proof. Apply Proposition  to the graded module R and its graded submodule \mathfrak a. The multiplication is compatible with the grading because (R_d+\mathfrak a)(R_e+\mathfrak a) \subseteq R_{d+e}+\mathfrak a. The assertion concerning \mathfrak b/\mathfrak a follows in the same way. ◻

Operations on graded ideals

Proposition 1.1 (Stability of graded ideals). Let \{\mathfrak a_\lambda\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda} be a family of graded ideals of R, and let \mathfrak a,\mathfrak b\subseteq R be graded ideals. Then the following ideals are graded:

  1. the sum \sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\mathfrak a_\lambda;

  2. the intersection \bigcap_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\mathfrak a_\lambda;

  3. the product \mathfrak a\mathfrak b;

  4. the radical \sqrt{\mathfrak a}.

Moreover, if \mathfrak a\subseteq\mathfrak b, then the quotient \mathfrak b/\mathfrak a is a graded ideal of the graded quotient ring R/\mathfrak a.

Proof. For the sum, let f\in\sum_\lambda\mathfrak a_\lambda. It can be written as a finite sum f=\sum_{j=1}^m f^{(j)} with f^{(j)}\in\mathfrak a_{\lambda_j}. Every homogeneous component of every f^{(j)} lies in \mathfrak a_{\lambda_j}, so each homogeneous component of f lies in \sum_\lambda\mathfrak a_\lambda. Thus the sum is graded.

If f\in\bigcap_\lambda\mathfrak a_\lambda, then every homogeneous component of f lies in every \mathfrak a_\lambda, and hence lies in their intersection. Thus the intersection is graded.

The product \mathfrak a\mathfrak b is generated by products uv with u\in\mathfrak a and v\in\mathfrak b. Since both ideals are generated by homogeneous elements, the product is generated by products of homogeneous elements. Such a product is homogeneous, so \mathfrak a\mathfrak b is graded.

It remains to prove the assertion about the radical. By Corollary , the ring \overline R=R/\mathfrak a is graded. It is enough to show that the nilradical of any commutative \mathbb N-graded ring is graded.

Let x=x_{d_1}+\cdots+x_{d_r}, d_1<\cdots<d_r, be a nilpotent element, with each x_{d_i} homogeneous. We argue by induction on r. If r=1, there is nothing to prove. If x^m=0, then the component of degree m d_r in x^m is x_{d_r}^m. Hence x_{d_r}^m=0, so x_{d_r} is nilpotent. The nilpotent elements of a commutative ring form an ideal: if u^m=0 and v^n=0, then (u+v)^{m+n-1}=0. Therefore x-x_{d_r} is nilpotent. By induction, all components of x-x_{d_r} are nilpotent, and hence every x_{d_i} is nilpotent. Thus the nilradical of \overline R is graded.

Now, if f\in\sqrt{\mathfrak a}, then its image \overline f in \overline R is nilpotent. Every homogeneous component \overline{f_d} is therefore nilpotent, so f_d\in\sqrt{\mathfrak a}. Proposition  implies that \sqrt{\mathfrak a} is graded.

The final statement is Corollary . ◻

Remark 1.1. For graded ideals \mathfrak a and \mathfrak b, their colon ideal (\mathfrak a:\mathfrak b) is also graded. Indeed, it is enough to test membership against homogeneous generators of \mathfrak b and then apply homogeneous decomposition. This fact is useful later when discussing saturated homogeneous ideals.

The irrelevant ideal and the set \operatorname{Proj}S

Let S=\bigoplus_{d\in\mathbb N}S_d be an \mathbb N-graded ring.

Definition 1.2. The ideal S_+\coloneqq\bigoplus_{d>0}S_d is called the irrelevant ideal of S.

The projective spectrum of S is the set \operatorname{Proj}S \coloneqq \left\{ \mathfrak p\in\operatorname{Spec}S \ \middle|\ \begin{array}{l} \mathfrak p\text{ is homogeneous},\\ S_+\nsubseteq\mathfrak p \end{array} \right\}. Thus the points of \operatorname{Proj}S are precisely the homogeneous prime ideals which do not contain the irrelevant ideal.

Remark 1.1. The ideal S_+ is indeed an ideal. If x\in S_d with d>0 and y\in S_e, then yx\in S_{d+e}\subseteq S_+. If S is generated as an S_0-algebra by S_1, then S_+=(S_1). In particular, if S=S_0[x_0,\ldots,x_n] with the standard grading, then S_+=(x_0,\ldots,x_n).

The Zariski topology on \operatorname{Proj}S

Definition 1.3. For a homogeneous ideal \mathfrak a\subseteq S, define V_+(\mathfrak a) \coloneqq \{\mathfrak p\in\operatorname{Proj}S\mid \mathfrak a\subseteq\mathfrak p\}. Equivalently, V_+(\mathfrak a)=V(\mathfrak a)\cap\operatorname{Proj}S, where V(\mathfrak a) denotes the usual closed subset of \operatorname{Spec}S.

Theorem 1.1. The subsets V_+(\mathfrak a), as \mathfrak a ranges over the homogeneous ideals of S, are the closed subsets of a topology on \operatorname{Proj}S. This topology is called the Zariski topology on \operatorname{Proj}S.

Proof. We verify the closed-set axioms.

First, V_+((0))=\operatorname{Proj}S, V_+(S)=\varnothing. Moreover, by the definition of \operatorname{Proj}S, V_+(S_+)=\varnothing.

Let \{\mathfrak a_\lambda\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda} be any family of homogeneous ideals. Their sum is homogeneous by Proposition , and \begin{align*} \mathfrak p\in\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Lambda}V_+(\mathfrak a_\lambda) &\Longleftrightarrow \mathfrak a_\lambda\subseteq\mathfrak p \text{ for every }\lambda\\ &\Longleftrightarrow \sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\mathfrak a_\lambda\subseteq\mathfrak p\\ &\Longleftrightarrow \mathfrak p\in V_+\!\left(\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\mathfrak a_\lambda\right). \end{align*} Hence \bigcap_{\lambda\in\Lambda}V_+(\mathfrak a_\lambda) = V_+\!\left(\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\mathfrak a_\lambda\right). Thus arbitrary intersections of such subsets are again of the same form.

Now let \mathfrak a and \mathfrak b be homogeneous ideals. Their product is homogeneous, and for a prime ideal \mathfrak p one has \mathfrak a\mathfrak b\subseteq\mathfrak p \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathfrak a\subseteq\mathfrak p \text{ or } \mathfrak b\subseteq\mathfrak p. Indeed, if neither ideal is contained in \mathfrak p, choose a\in\mathfrak a\setminus\mathfrak p and b\in\mathfrak b\setminus\mathfrak p. Then ab\notin\mathfrak p, contradicting \mathfrak a\mathfrak b\subseteq\mathfrak p. Therefore V_+(\mathfrak a)\cup V_+(\mathfrak b) =V_+(\mathfrak a\mathfrak b). Finite unions are consequently closed. The three closed-set axioms are satisfied, so the construction defines a topology. ◻

Proposition 1.1. For every homogeneous ideal \mathfrak a\subseteq S, V_+(\mathfrak a)=V_+(\sqrt{\mathfrak a}).

Proof. A prime ideal contains \mathfrak a if and only if it contains \sqrt{\mathfrak a}. Since \sqrt{\mathfrak a} is homogeneous by Proposition , both sides are defined as closed subsets of \operatorname{Proj}S and are equal. ◻

Remark 1.1. Unlike the corresponding statement for \operatorname{Spec}S, the containment V_+(\mathfrak a)\subseteq V_+(\mathfrak b) need not by itself imply \sqrt{\mathfrak b}\subseteq\sqrt{\mathfrak a}, because \operatorname{Proj}S omits all homogeneous prime ideals containing S_+. Later, this loss of information is measured by saturation with respect to the irrelevant ideal.

Definition 1.4. If f\in S is homogeneous, the corresponding standard open subset is D_+(f) \coloneqq \operatorname{Proj}S\setminus V_+((f)) = \{\mathfrak p\in\operatorname{Proj}S\mid f\notin\mathfrak p\}.

Proposition 1.1. The standard open subsets D_+(f), with f homogeneous, form a basis for the Zariski topology on \operatorname{Proj}S. They satisfy D_+(f)\cap D_+(g)=D_+(fg) for homogeneous f,g\in S. Moreover, \operatorname{Proj}S =\bigcup_{\substack{f\in S_+\\ f\text{ homogeneous}}}D_+(f). If S_+ is generated by homogeneous elements f_1,\ldots,f_r, then \operatorname{Proj}S=D_+(f_1)\cup\cdots\cup D_+(f_r).

Proof. Let U=\operatorname{Proj}S\setminus V_+(\mathfrak a) be open, where \mathfrak a is homogeneous, and let \mathfrak p\in U. Then \mathfrak a\nsubseteq\mathfrak p. Since \mathfrak a is generated by homogeneous elements, there is a homogeneous f\in\mathfrak a with f\notin\mathfrak p. Thus \mathfrak p\in D_+(f)\subseteq U, which proves the basis property.

For homogeneous f and g, primeness gives \begin{align*} \mathfrak p\in D_+(f)\cap D_+(g) &\Longleftrightarrow f\notin\mathfrak p \text{ and }g\notin\mathfrak p\\ &\Longleftrightarrow fg\notin\mathfrak p\\ &\Longleftrightarrow \mathfrak p\in D_+(fg). \end{align*}

Finally, S_+\nsubseteq\mathfrak p for every \mathfrak p\in\operatorname{Proj}S. Hence some positive-degree homogeneous element of S lies outside \mathfrak p, proving the stated cover. If S_+=(f_1,\ldots,f_r), then a prime ideal contains S_+ if and only if it contains every f_i, which gives the finite cover. ◻

Example: projective n-space over a ring

Let A be a commutative ring and set S=A[x_0,\ldots,x_n] with the standard grading \deg(a)=0\quad(a\in A), \deg(x_i)=1. Then S_d = \bigoplus_{\substack{\alpha=(\alpha_0,\ldots,\alpha_n)\in\mathbb N^{n+1}\\ |\alpha|=d}} A x_0^{\alpha_0}\cdots x_n^{\alpha_n}.

Definition 1.5. The projective n-space over A is \mathbb P_A^n \coloneqq \operatorname{Proj}A[x_0,\ldots,x_n].

Example 1.1. An ideal I\subseteq A[x_0,\ldots,x_n] is graded if and only if it is generated by homogeneous polynomials. Equivalently, I=\bigoplus_{d\geq 0}I_d, I_d=I\cap S_d. Thus ideals such as (x_0^2+x_1x_2,\ x_1^3-x_0x_2^2) are graded, while an ideal generated by a nonhomogeneous polynomial, such as (x_0+1), need not be graded.

Proposition 1.1. As a set, \mathbb P_A^n = \left\{ \mathfrak q\subsetneq A[x_0,\ldots,x_n] \ \middle|\ \begin{array}{l} \mathfrak q\text{ is a homogeneous prime ideal},\\ (x_0,\ldots,x_n)\nsubseteq\mathfrak q \end{array} \right\}. The irrelevant ideal is S_+=(x_0,\ldots,x_n).

Proof. Because S is generated over A=S_0 by S_1, its irrelevant ideal is generated by the degree-one elements. Since S_1 is generated as an A-module by x_0,\ldots,x_n, one has S_+=(x_0,\ldots,x_n). The description of the points is now exactly the definition of \operatorname{Proj}S. ◻

Example 1.1. Let \mathfrak p\in\operatorname{Spec}A and let J\subsetneq\{0,\ldots,n\}. Then \mathfrak q =\mathfrak pS+(x_j\mid j\in J) is a homogeneous prime ideal, because S/\mathfrak q \cong (A/\mathfrak p)[x_i\mid i\notin J] is an integral domain. Since J is a proper subset, not all variables belong to \mathfrak q, and hence \mathfrak q determines a point of \mathbb P_A^n.

Example 1.1. Suppose that A=k is a field and let a=[a_0:\cdots:a_n]\in\mathbb P^n(k). Consider the graded homomorphism \varphi_a:k[x_0,\ldots,x_n]\longrightarrow k[t], x_i\longmapsto a_it. Its kernel I(a)=\ker(\varphi_a) is a homogeneous prime ideal which does not contain (x_0,\ldots,x_n). If a_i\neq 0, then I(a) = (a_ix_j-a_jx_i\mid j\neq i). Thus every k-rational projective point determines a homogeneous prime ideal. If k is algebraically closed, these are precisely the closed points of \mathbb P_k^n.

Remark 1.1. The homogeneous prime ideals of A[x_0,\ldots,x_n] are not limited to the coordinate ideals in the preceding examples. Over an algebraically closed field, arbitrary homogeneous prime ideals not containing (x_0,\ldots,x_n) correspond to irreducible closed projective subvarieties, including their generic points.

Proposition 1.1 (Topology on projective space). The closed subsets of \mathbb P_A^n are the sets V_+(I) = \{\mathfrak q\in\mathbb P_A^n\mid I\subseteq\mathfrak q\}, where I\subseteq A[x_0,\ldots,x_n] is a homogeneous ideal. The standard open subsets U_i\coloneqq D_+(x_i) =\{\mathfrak q\in\mathbb P_A^n\mid x_i\notin\mathfrak q\} cover projective space: \mathbb P_A^n=U_0\cup\cdots\cup U_n. Each U_i is naturally homeomorphic to affine n-space over A: U_i\cong \operatorname{Spec} A\left[ \frac{x_0}{x_i},\ldots, \widehat{\frac{x_i}{x_i}},\ldots, \frac{x_n}{x_i} \right] \cong\mathbb A_A^n.

Proof. The description of the closed subsets follows from Theorem . Since no point of \mathbb P_A^n contains all of x_0,\ldots,x_n, the standard opens D_+(x_i) cover \mathbb P_A^n.

Fix i and localize S at the multiplicative set \{1,x_i,x_i^2,\ldots\}. The localization S_{x_i} is naturally \mathbb Z-graded, and its degree-zero part is S_{(x_i)} \coloneqq (S_{x_i})_0 = A\left[ \frac{x_0}{x_i},\ldots, \widehat{\frac{x_i}{x_i}},\ldots, \frac{x_n}{x_i} \right]. Define \Phi_i:D_+(x_i)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Spec}S_{(x_i)}, \mathfrak q\longmapsto(\mathfrak qS_{x_i})_0. Because x_i\notin\mathfrak q, the extension \mathfrak qS_{x_i} is a proper homogeneous prime ideal, and its degree-zero part is prime in S_{(x_i)}.

Conversely, if \mathfrak r\in\operatorname{Spec}S_{(x_i)}, define a homogeneous ideal \Psi_i(\mathfrak r) degree by degree by \Psi_i(\mathfrak r)_d = \left\{ f\in S_d \ \middle|\ \frac{f}{x_i^d}\in\mathfrak r \right\}, d\geq 0, and set \Psi_i(\mathfrak r) =\bigoplus_{d\geq 0}\Psi_i(\mathfrak r)_d. If f\in S_d and g\in S_e are homogeneous and fg\in\Psi_i(\mathfrak r), then \frac{fg}{x_i^{d+e}} = \frac{f}{x_i^d}\frac{g}{x_i^e} \in\mathfrak r. Since \mathfrak r is prime, either f/x_i^d\in\mathfrak r or g/x_i^e\in\mathfrak r. Hence f\in\Psi_i(\mathfrak r) or g\in\Psi_i(\mathfrak r). By Proposition , \Psi_i(\mathfrak r) is a prime ideal. Moreover, x_i\notin\Psi_i(\mathfrak r) because x_i/x_i=1\notin\mathfrak r. Thus \Psi_i(\mathfrak r)\in D_+(x_i).

The constructions \Phi_i and \Psi_i are inverse to each other. To check the topology, let f\in S_d be homogeneous. Under \Phi_i, the basic open subset D_+(x_i)\cap D_+(f) corresponds to D\!\left(\frac{f}{x_i^d}\right) \subseteq\operatorname{Spec}S_{(x_i)}. Therefore \Phi_i is a homeomorphism. Finally, S_{(x_i)} is a polynomial ring over A in the n variables x_j/x_i for j\neq i, so its spectrum is \mathbb A_A^n. ◻

Remark 1.1. When A=k is a field and I is a homogeneous ideal, the k-rational points of V_+(I) are V_+(I)(k) = \left\{ [a_0:\cdots:a_n]\in\mathbb P^n(k) \ \middle|\ f(a_0,\ldots,a_n)=0 \text{ for every homogeneous }f\in I \right\}. Thus the topology above recovers the usual projective algebraic sets on closed points, while also retaining non-closed points such as generic points of irreducible projective subvarieties.

The Scheme Structure on \operatorname{Proj}S

Standing hypotheses

From now on, unless explicitly stated otherwise, we impose the following finiteness assumptions.

Notation 1.1 (Standard graded rings). Let S=\bigoplus_{d\geq 0}S_d be an \mathbb N-graded ring such that:

  1. S_0 is noetherian;

  2. S is generated as an S_0-algebra by finitely many elements of S_1.

A graded S-module will be written M=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb Z}M_n. Unless finite generation is explicitly required, M need not be finitely generated over S.

Remark 1.1. By the Hilbert basis theorem, S is noetherian. If x_0,\ldots,x_r\in S_1 generate S as an S_0-algebra, then S_+=(x_0,\ldots,x_r), \operatorname{Proj}S =D_+(x_0)\cup\cdots\cup D_+(x_r). In particular, \operatorname{Proj}S is quasi-compact. The construction of its structure sheaf works under weaker hypotheses, but the assumptions above will remain in force throughout these notes.

Graded localization and its degree-zero part

Definition 1.6. Let T\subseteq S be a multiplicatively closed subset consisting of homogeneous elements. The ordinary localization T^{-1}S has a canonical \mathbb Z-grading defined by \deg\!\left(\frac{a}{t}\right)=\deg(a)-\deg(t) whenever a and t are homogeneous. Its degree-n part is (T^{-1}S)_n = \left\{ \frac{a}{t} \ \middle|\ \begin{array}{l} a,t\text{ homogeneous},\ t\in T,\\ \deg(a)-\deg(t)=n \end{array} \right\}.

Similarly, the localization T^{-1}M has a canonical \mathbb Z-grading whose degree-n part is (T^{-1}M)_n = \left\{ \frac{m}{t} \ \middle|\ \begin{array}{l} m\in M\text{ and }t\in T\text{ homogeneous},\\ \deg(m)-\deg(t)=n \end{array} \right\}. Here a homogeneous fraction is understood up to the usual equivalence relation in a localization.

Proposition 1.1. With the notation above, T^{-1}S=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb Z}(T^{-1}S)_n is a graded ring and T^{-1}M=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb Z}(T^{-1}M)_n is a graded T^{-1}S-module.

Proof. Every numerator has a finite homogeneous decomposition. After placing the homogeneous components over a common homogeneous denominator, every fraction is a finite sum of homogeneous fractions. Thus the displayed homogeneous pieces generate the localizations.

To see that the sums are direct, suppose that \frac{m_{n_1}}{t}+\cdots+\frac{m_{n_r}}{t}=0, \deg(m_{n_i})-\deg(t)=n_i, n_1<\cdots<n_r, where a common homogeneous denominator has been chosen. Then there is some homogeneous u\in T such that u(m_{n_1}+\cdots+m_{n_r})=0. The summands um_{n_i} have distinct degrees, so every um_{n_i} is zero. Consequently each m_{n_i}/t is zero. Compatibility with multiplication and the module action follows immediately by adding degrees. ◻

Notation 1.2. Let f\in S be homogeneous. Write S_f=S[1/f], M_f=M\otimes_SS_f, with their canonical \mathbb Z-gradings, and define S_{(f)}\coloneqq(S_f)_0, M_{(f)}\coloneqq(M_f)_0. Thus, if d=\deg(f)>0, then S_{(f)} = \left\{ \frac{a}{f^n} \ \middle|\ a\in S_{nd}\text{ homogeneous} \right\}, and M_{(f)} = \left\{ \frac{m}{f^n} \ \middle|\ m\in M_{nd} \right\}. The latter is naturally an S_{(f)}-module.

Proposition 1.1. Under the standing hypotheses, S_{(f)} is a finitely generated S_0-algebra, and hence is noetherian, for every positive-degree homogeneous element f\in S.

Proof. Let d=\deg(f). Since S is generated over S_0 by finitely many degree-one elements, S_d is a finite S_0-module. Choose generators b_1,\ldots,b_N of S_d. Every element of S_{nd} is an S_0-linear combination of products of n elements of S_d. It follows that S_{(f)} =S_0\left[\frac{b_1}{f},\ldots,\frac{b_N}{f}\right]. Thus S_{(f)} is a finitely generated algebra over the noetherian ring S_0, and the Hilbert basis theorem implies that it is noetherian. ◻

Example 1.1. For the standard graded polynomial ring S=A[x_0,\ldots,x_n] and f=x_i, one has S_{(x_i)} = A\left[ \frac{x_0}{x_i},\ldots, \widehat{\frac{x_i}{x_i}},\ldots, \frac{x_n}{x_i} \right]. This is the coordinate ring of the usual i-th affine chart of \mathbb P_A^n.

Homogeneous localization at a point

Let \mathfrak p\in\operatorname{Proj}S, and let T_{\mathfrak p} = \{s\in S\mid s\text{ is homogeneous and }s\notin\mathfrak p\}. This is a multiplicatively closed set.

Definition 1.7. Define S_{(\mathfrak p)} \coloneqq (T_{\mathfrak p}^{-1}S)_0, M_{(\mathfrak p)} \coloneqq (T_{\mathfrak p}^{-1}M)_0. Thus every element of M_{(\mathfrak p)} may be represented in the form \frac{m}{s}, m\in M_e, \quad s\in S_e\setminus\mathfrak p for some e\in\mathbb Z_{\geq 0}.

Proposition 1.1. The ring S_{(\mathfrak p)} is local. Its unique maximal ideal is \mathfrak m_{(\mathfrak p)} = \left\{ \frac{a}{s}\in S_{(\mathfrak p)} \ \middle|\ a\in\mathfrak p \right\}.

Proof. The displayed subset is an ideal. If a/s does not belong to it, then a\notin\mathfrak p. Since a and s are homogeneous of the same degree, a\in T_{\mathfrak p} and \frac{s}{a}\in S_{(\mathfrak p)} is an inverse of a/s. Thus every element outside \mathfrak m_{(\mathfrak p)} is a unit, which proves that S_{(\mathfrak p)} is local with the asserted maximal ideal. ◻

The sheaf associated to a graded module

Definition 1.8. Let U\subseteq\operatorname{Proj}S be open. Define \widetilde M(U) to be the set of all functions \sigma:U\longrightarrow\coprod_{\mathfrak p\in U}M_{(\mathfrak p)}, \mathfrak p\longmapsto\sigma(\mathfrak p)\in M_{(\mathfrak p)}, which are locally representable by a single homogeneous fraction in the following sense: for every \mathfrak p\in U, there exist

  • an open neighborhood V\subseteq U of \mathfrak p;

  • homogeneous elements m\in M_e and s\in S_e of the same degree;

such that s\notin\mathfrak q for every \mathfrak q\in V and \sigma(\mathfrak q)=\frac{m}{s} \quad\text{ in }M_{(\mathfrak q)} for every \mathfrak q\in V.

Restriction is defined by restricting functions. The resulting presheaf is denoted by \widetilde M.

Theorem 1.1. The presheaf \widetilde M is a sheaf of \widetilde S-modules on \operatorname{Proj}S.

Proof. The module operations are defined pointwise, and local homogeneous representations show that they preserve the defining condition.

Let U=\bigcup_{i\in I}U_i be an open covering. Suppose first that \sigma,\tau\in\widetilde M(U) have equal restrictions to every U_i. For each \mathfrak p\in U, choose i with \mathfrak p\in U_i. Then \sigma(\mathfrak p)=\tau(\mathfrak p), so \sigma=\tau. This proves locality.

Now suppose that sections \sigma_i\in\widetilde M(U_i) satisfy \sigma_i|_{U_i\cap U_j}=\sigma_j|_{U_i\cap U_j} for all i,j. Define \sigma(\mathfrak p)=\sigma_i(\mathfrak p) for any i such that \mathfrak p\in U_i. The compatibility condition makes this well defined. If \mathfrak p\in U_i, then near \mathfrak p the section \sigma_i is represented by one homogeneous fraction. The same fraction therefore represents \sigma on that neighborhood. Thus \sigma\in\widetilde M(U), and it is the unique section restricting to all \sigma_i. Hence \widetilde M is a sheaf. ◻

Standard open subsets are affine

Fix a homogeneous element f\in S of positive degree d.

Proposition 1.1 (The affine chart homeomorphism). There is a canonical homeomorphism \pi_f:D_+(f)\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{Spec}S_{(f)} given by \pi_f(\mathfrak p) =(\mathfrak pS_f)_0.

Proof. If \mathfrak p\in D_+(f), then \mathfrak pS_f is a homogeneous prime ideal of S_f, so (\mathfrak pS_f)_0 is a prime ideal of S_{(f)}. This defines \pi_f.

Conversely, let \mathfrak q\in\operatorname{Spec}S_{(f)}. Regard \mathfrak qS_f as the homogeneous ideal of S_f generated by \mathfrak q, and put P(\mathfrak q)=\sqrt{\mathfrak qS_f}. The ideal P(\mathfrak q) is homogeneous. We claim that it is prime. By the homogeneous primeness criterion, it suffices to consider homogeneous x,y\in S_f. Let e=\deg(x) and r=\deg(y). Since f is invertible in S_f, the elements N_f(x)\coloneqq x^d f^{-e}, N_f(y)\coloneqq y^d f^{-r} belong to (S_f)_0=S_{(f)}.

First note that (\mathfrak qS_f)_0=\mathfrak q. Indeed, the degree-zero component of an element of \mathfrak qS_f is a finite sum of products of elements of \mathfrak q with degree-zero elements of S_f.

Suppose that xy\in P(\mathfrak q). Then (xy)^n\in\mathfrak qS_f for some n\geq 1. Taking a suitable degree-zero power gives N_f(x)^nN_f(y)^n =N_f(xy)^n \in\mathfrak q. Since \mathfrak q is prime, either N_f(x)\in\mathfrak q or N_f(y)\in\mathfrak q. Hence either x^d\in\mathfrak qS_f or y^d\in\mathfrak qS_f, so either x\in P(\mathfrak q) or y\in P(\mathfrak q). Thus P(\mathfrak q) is prime.

Because f is a unit in S_f, one has f\notin P(\mathfrak q). Hence \rho_f(\mathfrak q) \coloneqq P(\mathfrak q)\cap S is a homogeneous prime ideal belonging to D_+(f).

The maps \pi_f and \rho_f are inverse. Indeed, (P(\mathfrak q))_0 = \sqrt{\mathfrak qS_f}\cap S_{(f)} = \sqrt{\mathfrak q} = \mathfrak q. Conversely, if \mathfrak p\in D_+(f) and \mathfrak q=(\mathfrak pS_f)_0, then \sqrt{\mathfrak qS_f}=\mathfrak pS_f. The inclusion from left to right is clear. For the reverse inclusion, let x\in\mathfrak pS_f be homogeneous of degree e. Then x^df^{-e}\in(\mathfrak pS_f)_0=\mathfrak q, so x^d\in\mathfrak qS_f and therefore x\in\sqrt{\mathfrak qS_f}.

It remains to check the topology. Every element of S_{(f)} can be written as a/f^n with a\in S_{nd} homogeneous, and \pi_f^{-1}\!\left(D\!\left(\frac{a}{f^n}\right)\right) = D_+(f)\cap D_+(a). The sets on both sides form bases for the respective topologies. Therefore \pi_f is a homeomorphism. ◻

Lemma 1.1 (Comparison of localizations). Let \mathfrak p\in D_+(f) and \mathfrak q=\pi_f(\mathfrak p)=(\mathfrak pS_f)_0. There is a canonical isomorphism of S_{(\mathfrak p)}-modules (M_{(f)})_{\mathfrak q} \xrightarrow{\sim} M_{(\mathfrak p)}. For M=S, this is an isomorphism of local rings (S_{(f)})_{\mathfrak q} \xrightarrow{\sim} S_{(\mathfrak p)}.

Proof. A typical element of (M_{(f)})_{\mathfrak q} may be written \frac{m/f^r}{a/f^s}, where m\in M_{rd}, a\in S_{sd}, and a/f^s\notin\mathfrak q. Send it to \frac{mf^s}{f^ra}\in M_{(\mathfrak p)}. This gives the canonical localization map.

To see surjectivity, let m/h\in M_{(\mathfrak p)}, where m\in M_e and h\in S_e\setminus\mathfrak p. Then \frac{m}{h} = \frac{(mh^{d-1})/f^e}{h^d/f^e}. Both displayed fractions have degree zero in the localization at f, and \frac{h^d}{f^e}\notin\mathfrak q because h\notin\mathfrak p. Hence m/h lies in the image. Injectivity is the usual injectivity statement for the induced localization map, or may be checked directly by clearing homogeneous denominators. ◻

Theorem 1.1 (The standard affine-chart theorem). Let f\in S be homogeneous of positive degree. The homeomorphism \pi_f of Proposition  extends to an isomorphism of locally ringed spaces \pi_f: \left(D_+(f), \mathcal O_{\operatorname{Proj}S}|_{D_+(f)}\right) \xrightarrow{\sim} \left(\operatorname{Spec}S_{(f)}, \mathcal O_{\operatorname{Spec}S_{(f)}}\right). Moreover, for every graded S-module M, there is a canonical isomorphism of sheaves of modules on \operatorname{Spec}S_{(f)}, (\pi_f)_*\left(\widetilde M|_{D_+(f)}\right) \xrightarrow{\sim} \widetilde{M_{(f)}}. Here the sheaf on the right is the usual quasi-coherent sheaf associated to the S_{(f)}-module M_{(f)} on the affine scheme \operatorname{Spec}S_{(f)}.

Proof. Let \mathfrak p\in D_+(f) and \mathfrak q=\pi_f(\mathfrak p). By Lemma , the stalk of \widetilde M at \mathfrak p is canonically isomorphic to M_{(\mathfrak p)} \cong (M_{(f)})_{\mathfrak q}, which is the stalk of the affine sheaf \widetilde{M_{(f)}} at \mathfrak q. These isomorphisms are induced by the same homogeneous fraction maps on standard neighborhoods and are therefore compatible with restriction. They assemble to the asserted sheaf isomorphism.

Taking M=S gives an isomorphism of sheaves of rings. The maps on stalks are isomorphisms of local rings, so \pi_f is an isomorphism of locally ringed spaces. ◻

Why \operatorname{Proj} is not a functor on all graded rings

Let \varphi:S\longrightarrow T be a degree-preserving homomorphism of \mathbb N-graded rings. If \mathfrak q\in\operatorname{Proj}T, then \varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak q) is a homogeneous prime ideal of S. However, it may contain the whole irrelevant ideal S_+, in which case it is not a point of \operatorname{Proj}S. Thus contraction does not always define a map \operatorname{Proj}T\longrightarrow\operatorname{Proj}S.

Example 1.1 (A graded homomorphism which gives no map on \operatorname{Proj}). Let k be a field and consider the standard graded rings S=k[x_0,x_1], T=k[t]. Define a graded homomorphism \varphi:S\longrightarrow T, x_0\longmapsto 0, \quad x_1\longmapsto 0. The scheme \operatorname{Proj}T consists of the single point (0). But \varphi^{-1}((0))=(x_0,x_1)=S_+, which is excluded from \operatorname{Proj}S=\mathbb P_k^1. Therefore contraction does not define a morphism \operatorname{Proj}T\longrightarrow\operatorname{Proj}S. Consequently, S\mapsto\operatorname{Proj}S is not a contravariant functor on the category of all graded rings with all degree-preserving homomorphisms.

Definition 1.10. For a degree-preserving homomorphism \varphi:S\to T, define U_\varphi \coloneqq \left\{ \mathfrak q\in\operatorname{Proj}T \ \middle|\ S_+\nsubseteq\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak q) \right\}. Equivalently, U_\varphi = \bigcup_{\substack{f\in S_+\\ f\text{ homogeneous}}} D_+\left(\varphi(f)\right). Hence U_\varphi is an open subset of \operatorname{Proj}T.

Theorem 1.1 (The natural morphism induced by a graded homomorphism). Let \varphi:S\to T be a degree-preserving homomorphism. There is a canonical morphism of schemes \operatorname{Proj}(\varphi): U_\varphi\longrightarrow\operatorname{Proj}S whose map on points is \mathfrak q\longmapsto\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak q). For every positive-degree homogeneous element f\in S, its restriction to D_+(\varphi(f)) is the affine morphism \operatorname{Spec}T_{(\varphi(f))} \longrightarrow \operatorname{Spec}S_{(f)} induced by the ring homomorphism \varphi_{(f)}:S_{(f)}\longrightarrow T_{(\varphi(f))}, \frac{a}{f^n} \longmapsto \frac{\varphi(a)}{\varphi(f)^n}.

Proof. If \mathfrak q\in U_\varphi, then \varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak q) is a homogeneous prime ideal and, by the definition of U_\varphi, it does not contain S_+. Thus contraction defines a map of sets U_\varphi\longrightarrow\operatorname{Proj}S.

The standard opens D_+(\varphi(f)), with f\in S_+ homogeneous, cover U_\varphi. On such an open, localization gives the degree-preserving map S_f\longrightarrow T_{\varphi(f)}, whose degree-zero part is \varphi_{(f)}. By the affine-chart theorem, \varphi_{(f)} induces a morphism D_+(\varphi(f)) \cong\operatorname{Spec}T_{(\varphi(f))} \longrightarrow \operatorname{Spec}S_{(f)} \cong D_+(f). Its map on points is contraction by \varphi.

For homogeneous f,g\in S_+, the two local morphisms agree on D_+(\varphi(f))\cap D_+(\varphi(g)) =D_+(\varphi(fg)), because both are induced by the same localized homomorphism S_{fg}\longrightarrow T_{\varphi(fg)}. The local morphisms therefore glue uniquely to the asserted morphism on U_\varphi. ◻

Corollary 1.1. The graded homomorphism \varphi:S\to T induces a morphism on all of \operatorname{Proj}T whenever T_+\subseteq\sqrt{\varphi(S_+)T}. Under this hypothesis, U_\varphi=\operatorname{Proj}T and hence there is a canonical morphism \operatorname{Proj}T\longrightarrow\operatorname{Proj}S.

Proof. Suppose that \mathfrak q\in\operatorname{Proj}T contained \varphi(S_+). Then it would contain \sqrt{\varphi(S_+)T} and therefore T_+, contradicting the definition of \operatorname{Proj}T. Thus no point of \operatorname{Proj}T lies outside U_\varphi. ◻

Example 1.1 (A genuinely partially defined projective morphism). Let S=k[x_0,x_1], T=k[u,v] with their standard gradings, and define \varphi:S\longrightarrow T, x_0\longmapsto u, \quad x_1\longmapsto 0. Then U_\varphi=D_+(u)\subseteq\mathbb P_k^1. On this open subset, the induced morphism D_+(u)\longrightarrow\mathbb P_k^1 is the constant morphism with image the point represented by the homogeneous prime ideal (x_1). At points of V_+(u), the contraction contains (x_0,x_1)=S_+, so the morphism cannot be extended by the contraction construction there.

Twisting Sheaves and the Serre Correspondence

Throughout this section, we retain the standing hypotheses of Notation . Thus S=\bigoplus_{d\geq 0}S_d is generated by the finite S_0-module S_1 as an S_0-algebra, and S_0 is noetherian. We write X=\operatorname{Proj}S. All homomorphisms of graded modules are understood to have degree zero unless another degree is explicitly specified.

Shifted graded modules and twisting sheaves

Definition 1.11 (Shift of a graded module). Let M=\bigoplus_{d\in\mathbb Z}M_d be a graded S-module and let n\in\mathbb Z. The n-th shift of M is the graded S-module M(n) having the same underlying S-module as M, but with grading M(n)_d\coloneqq M_{n+d}. Thus a homogeneous element of degree e in M has degree e-n when it is regarded as an element of M(n).

Remark 1.1. With this convention one has canonical graded isomorphisms M(n)(m)\cong M(n+m), M\otimes_S S(n)\cong M(n), where the second isomorphism is induced by multiplication.

Definition 1.12 (Twisting sheaves). For n\in\mathbb Z, define \mathcal O_X(n)\coloneqq\widetilde{S(n)}. This is called the n-th twisting sheaf on X.

If \mathcal F is an \mathcal O_X-module, define \mathcal F(n) \coloneqq \mathcal F\otimes_{\mathcal O_X}\mathcal O_X(n). The operation \mathcal F\mapsto\mathcal F(n) is called twisting by n.

The following local algebra identity will be used repeatedly.

Lemma 1.1 (Tensor products on a degree-one chart). Let x\in S_1, and let M,N be graded S-modules. The canonical map M_{(x)}\otimes_{S_{(x)}}N_{(x)} \longrightarrow (M\otimes_SN)_{(x)}, \frac{m}{x^a}\otimes\frac{n}{x^b} \longmapsto \frac{m\otimes n}{x^{a+b}}, is an isomorphism.

Proof. The map is induced by the universal property of the tensor product and is therefore well defined. It is enough to construct its inverse on homogeneous fractions.

Let \frac{z}{x^r}\in(M\otimes_SN)_{(x)}. Writing z as a sum of homogeneous pure tensors, we reduce to z=m\otimes n, \deg(m)+\deg(n)=r. Choose q\geq 0 such that both \deg(m)+q and \deg(n)+q are nonnegative. Then the given fraction is the image of \frac{x^qm}{x^{\deg(m)+q}} \otimes \frac{x^qn}{x^{\deg(n)+q}}. Indeed, after balancing the powers of x in the tensor product, its image is obtained from (m\otimes n)/x^r by multiplying numerator and denominator by x^{2q}. Thus the canonical map is surjective.

The same balancing calculation shows that two elements of the tensor product with the same image become equal after multiplication by a sufficiently high power of x. Since powers of x are already invertible in all three localized modules, the map is injective. ◻

Proposition 1.1 (Sheafification and tensor products). For graded S-modules M and N, there is a canonical isomorphism \widetilde M\otimes_{\mathcal O_X}\widetilde N \xrightarrow{\sim} \widetilde{M\otimes_SN}. The isomorphism is functorial in both M and N.

Proof. Choose degree-one generators x_0,\ldots,x_r of S over S_0. On the affine chart D_+(x_i), the affine-chart theorem identifies the two sides with the quasi-coherent sheaves associated respectively to M_{(x_i)}\otimes_{S_{(x_i)}}N_{(x_i)} \quad\text{and}\quad (M\otimes_SN)_{(x_i)}. These modules are canonically isomorphic by Lemma . The local isomorphisms are induced by the same multiplication map and hence agree on overlaps. They therefore glue to the asserted global isomorphism. ◻

Proposition 1.1 (Basic identities for twists). Let M be a graded S-module and let m,n\in\mathbb Z. Then there are canonical isomorphisms

  1. \widetilde{M(n)}\xrightarrow{\sim}\widetilde M(n);

  2. \mathcal O_X(n)\otimes_{\mathcal O_X}\mathcal O_X(m) \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathcal O_X(n+m);

  3. \mathcal F(n)(m)\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal F(n+m) for every \mathcal O_X-module \mathcal F.

All these isomorphisms are natural.

Proof. The multiplication isomorphism of graded modules M\otimes_SS(n)\xrightarrow{\sim}M(n) and Proposition  give \widetilde M\otimes\widetilde{S(n)} \cong \widetilde{M\otimes_SS(n)} \cong \widetilde{M(n)}. This proves (i). Taking M=S(m) gives (ii), and (iii) follows from (ii) and associativity of tensor products. ◻

Proposition 1.1 (Invertibility of the twisting sheaves). For every n\in\mathbb Z, the sheaf \mathcal O_X(n) is invertible. Its inverse is \mathcal O_X(-n), and the multiplication pairing gives a canonical isomorphism \mathcal O_X(n)\otimes_{\mathcal O_X}\mathcal O_X(-n) \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathcal O_X. Consequently, twisting by n is an autoequivalence of the category of \mathcal O_X-modules, with inverse given by twisting by -n.

Proof. By Proposition , it is enough to prove that \mathcal O_X(n) is locally free of rank one. On D_+(x_i), where x_i\in S_1, one has S(n)_{(x_i)}=(S_{x_i})_n. Multiplication by the homogeneous unit x_i^n\in(S_{x_i})_n induces an isomorphism S_{(x_i)}\xrightarrow{\sim}S(n)_{(x_i)}, a\longmapsto ax_i^n. For n<0, the symbol x_i^n means 1/x_i^{-n} in S_{x_i}. Hence \mathcal O_X(n)|_{D_+(x_i)}\cong\mathcal O_{D_+(x_i)}. The standard opens D_+(x_i) cover X, so \mathcal O_X(n) is invertible. The assertion about its inverse follows from \mathcal O_X(n)\otimes\mathcal O_X(-n)\cong\mathcal O_X(0)=\mathcal O_X. ◻

Example 1.1 (Twists on projective space). For X=\mathbb P_A^r=\operatorname{Proj}A[x_0,\ldots,x_r], the trivialization of \mathcal O_X(n) on D_+(x_i) is generated by x_i^n. On the overlap D_+(x_ix_j), the transition function from the i-th trivialization to the j-th trivialization is \left(\frac{x_j}{x_i}\right)^n. In particular, \mathcal O_{\mathbb P_A^r}(1) is the usual Serre twisting sheaf.

Compatibility with morphisms induced by graded homomorphisms

Let \varphi:S\longrightarrow T be a degree-preserving homomorphism of graded rings satisfying the standing finiteness assumptions, let Y=\operatorname{Proj}T, U=U_\varphi\subseteq Y, and let f=\operatorname{Proj}(\varphi):U\longrightarrow X be the morphism of Theorem .

If N is a graded T-module, we write {}_SN for the same graded abelian group regarded as a graded S-module through \varphi.

Proposition 1.1 (Pullback and pushforward of associated sheaves). With the notation above, the following assertions hold.

  1. For every graded S-module M, there is a canonical isomorphism of \mathcal O_U-modules f^*(\widetilde M) \xrightarrow{\sim} \widetilde{T\otimes_SM}\big|_U.

  2. For every graded T-module N, there is a canonical isomorphism of \mathcal O_X-modules f_*\left(\widetilde N|_U\right) \xrightarrow{\sim} \widetilde{{}_SN}.

There is a canonical localization isomorphism T_{(\varphi(x_i))}\otimes_{S_{(x_i)}}M_{(x_i)} \xrightarrow{\sim} (T\otimes_SM)_{(\varphi(x_i))}. It is obtained from the map \frac{t}{\varphi(x_i)^a}\otimes\frac{m}{x_i^b} \longmapsto \frac{t\otimes m}{\varphi(x_i)^{a+b}}, and is proved by the same degree-balancing argument as Lemma . The local isomorphisms agree on overlaps, proving (i).

For (ii), let x\in S_1. Then \begin{aligned} \Gamma\left(D_+(x),f_*(\widetilde N|_U)\right) &=\Gamma\left(f^{-1}(D_+(x)),\widetilde N\right)\\ &=\Gamma\left(D_+(\varphi(x)),\widetilde N\right)\\ &=N_{(\varphi(x))}. \end{aligned} On the other hand, localization of N as an S-module gives ({}_SN)_{(x)}\xrightarrow{\sim}N_{(\varphi(x))}, \frac{n}{x^a}\longmapsto\frac{n}{\varphi(x)^a}. Different graded modules may define the same sheaf

The construction M\mapsto\widetilde M forgets the part of a graded module which is visible only in bounded degree. It is also unchanged by passing to a Veronese presentation.

Definition 1.13 (Truncation). For a graded S-module M and an integer a, define M_{\geq a}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{d\geq a}M_d. Since S is nonnegatively graded, M_{\geq a} is a graded submodule of M.

Proposition 1.1 (Invariance under truncation and eventual isomorphism). Let M and N be graded S-modules.

  1. For every a\in\mathbb Z, the inclusion M_{\geq a}\hookrightarrow M induces an isomorphism \widetilde{M_{\geq a}}\xrightarrow{\sim}\widetilde M.

  2. If u:M\to N is a graded homomorphism such that u_d:M_d\xrightarrow{\sim}N_d for all d\gg0, then \widetilde u:\widetilde M\xrightarrow{\sim}\widetilde N.

Proof. Let x_0,\ldots,x_r\in S_1 generate S over S_0. It is enough to work on D_+(x_i). Every element of M_{(x_i)} may be represented as \frac{m}{x_i^q}, m\in M_q. Choose b\geq0 such that q+b\geq a. Then \frac{m}{x_i^q} = \frac{x_i^bm}{x_i^{q+b}}, and the numerator x_i^bm belongs to (M_{\geq a})_{q+b}. Hence (M_{\geq a})_{(x_i)}\longrightarrow M_{(x_i)} is surjective. It is injective because it is induced by an inclusion before localization. This proves (i).

For (ii), choose a such that u_d is an isomorphism for every d\geq a. Then u restricts to an isomorphism M_{\geq a}\xrightarrow{\sim}N_{\geq a}. Apply (i) to both modules. ◻

Definition 1.14 (Veronese ring and Veronese module). Let e\geq1. The e-th Veronese subring of S is S^{(e)} \coloneqq \bigoplus_{n\geq0}S_{en}, S^{(e)}_n=S_{en}. For a graded S-module M, its e-th Veronese module is M^{(e)} \coloneqq \bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb Z}M_{en}, M^{(e)}_n=M_{en}. It is naturally a graded S^{(e)}-module.

Proposition 1.1 (Veronese invariance). Let e\geq1. Contraction of homogeneous prime ideals defines a canonical isomorphism of schemes \nu_e:X=\operatorname{Proj}S \xrightarrow{\sim} \operatorname{Proj}S^{(e)}, \mathfrak p\longmapsto\mathfrak p\cap S^{(e)}. For every graded S-module M, there is a canonical isomorphism (\nu_e)_*\widetilde M \xrightarrow{\sim} \widetilde{M^{(e)}}. Moreover, \nu_e^*\mathcal O_{\operatorname{Proj}S^{(e)}}(n) \cong \mathcal O_X(en) for every n\in\mathbb Z.

Proof. If f\in S_{er}=S^{(e)}_r is homogeneous, then the degree-zero localizations computed with the two gradings are identical: (S^{(e)})_{(f)}=S_{(f)}, (M^{(e)})_{(f)}=M_{(f)}. Indeed, in either case a degree-zero fraction with denominator f^q has numerator of original degree qer.

The standard opens D_+(f) with f of degree divisible by e cover \operatorname{Proj}S: if a homogeneous element g\notin\mathfrak p, then g^e\notin\mathfrak p and \deg(g^e) is divisible by e. On each such open, the affine charts for S and S^{(e)} are therefore the same affine scheme \operatorname{Spec}S_{(f)}. These affine identifications glue and give \nu_e. The corresponding local module identifications give (\nu_e)_*\widetilde M\cong\widetilde{M^{(e)}}. Finally, S(en)^{(e)}\cong S^{(e)}(n), and sheafification gives the assertion about twisting sheaves. ◻

The graded module associated to a sheaf

Definition 1.15 (The total twisted section module). Let \mathcal F be an \mathcal O_X-module. Define \Gamma_*(\mathcal F) \coloneqq \bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb Z} \Gamma\left(X,\mathcal F(n)\right). Its degree-n component is \Gamma_*(\mathcal F)_n = \Gamma\left(X,\mathcal F(n)\right).

The graded abelian group \Gamma_*(\mathcal F) has a natural graded S-module structure. If a\in S_m, then a determines a global section of \mathcal O_X(m), and multiplication by that section gives \Gamma\left(X,\mathcal F(n)\right) \longrightarrow \Gamma\left(X,\mathcal F(n+m)\right). Thus S_m\Gamma_*(\mathcal F)_n \subseteq \Gamma_*(\mathcal F)_{m+n}.

Proposition 1.1 (The canonical map from a module to its twisted sections). For every graded S-module M, there is a natural graded homomorphism \alpha_M:M\longrightarrow\Gamma_*(\widetilde M). Its degree-d component is \alpha_{M,d}:M_d \longrightarrow \Gamma\left(X,\widetilde M(d)\right) \cong \Gamma\left(X,\widetilde{M(d)}\right), and sends m\in M_d=M(d)_0 to the global section represented by m/1.

Proof. Regard m\in M_d as a degree-zero element of M(d). It therefore defines a section of \widetilde{M(d)} on all of X by the locally constant fraction m/1. Proposition  identifies this sheaf with \widetilde M(d). Compatibility with multiplication by a homogeneous element of S shows that the maps in all degrees assemble into a graded S-homomorphism. Naturality is immediate from the construction. ◻

We next construct the map in the opposite direction.

Lemma 1.1 (Extension after multiplication by a power). Let x\in S_1, let \mathcal F be a quasi-coherent \mathcal O_X-module, and let s\in\Gamma\left(D_+(x),\mathcal F\right). Then there exists r\geq0 and a global section \overline s\in\Gamma\left(X,\mathcal F(r)\right) such that, under the trivialization of \mathcal O_X(r) by x^r on D_+(x), \overline s|_{D_+(x)}=x^rs.

Moreover, if t\in\Gamma\left(X,\mathcal F(n)\right) restricts to zero on D_+(x), then x^rt=0 in \Gamma(X,\mathcal F(n+r)) for some r\geq0.

Proof. Cover X by the finitely many affine opens D_+(x_0),\ldots,D_+(x_r) coming from degree-one generators of S. On each intersection D_+(x)\cap D_+(x_i), the section s is a section of a quasi-coherent module on the principal open subset defined by x/x_i inside the affine scheme D_+(x_i). The affine localization property implies that, after multiplication by a sufficiently high power of x/x_i, it is the restriction of a section on D_+(x_i). Because the cover is finite, one power works simultaneously on all charts. After increasing the exponent once more, the resulting local extensions agree on every overlap: their differences vanish after localization at x, and a further common power of x kills these finitely many differences. The sheaf axiom then glues the local extensions to the required global section of \mathcal F(r).

The final assertion is proved by the same denominator-clearing argument: on each affine chart, the restriction of t becomes zero after localizing at x/x_i, so a power of x/x_i annihilates it. A common exponent works on the finite affine cover. ◻

Proposition 1.1 (Recovering a quasi-coherent sheaf). For every quasi-coherent \mathcal O_X-module \mathcal F, there is a canonical isomorphism \varepsilon_{\mathcal F}: \widetilde{\Gamma_*(\mathcal F)} \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathcal F. It is functorial in \mathcal F.

Proof. Let x\in S_1. A degree-zero fraction in \Gamma_*(\mathcal F)_{(x)} has the form \frac{s}{x^r}, s\in\Gamma\left(X,\mathcal F(r)\right). Restrict s to D_+(x) and use the inverse of the local generator x^r of \mathcal O_X(r) to define \varepsilon_x: \Gamma_*(\mathcal F)_{(x)} \longrightarrow \Gamma\left(D_+(x),\mathcal F\right), \frac{s}{x^r}\longmapsto x^{-r}s|_{D_+(x)}. This map is well defined.

It is surjective by the first assertion of Lemma . It is injective by the second assertion: if x^{-r}s vanishes on D_+(x), then x^qs=0 globally for some q, so s/x^r=0 after localization. Hence \varepsilon_x is an isomorphism.

These isomorphisms commute with restriction to overlaps and therefore glue, through the affine-chart description of associated sheaves, to a global isomorphism \widetilde{\Gamma_*(\mathcal F)}\cong\mathcal F. Every step is natural in \mathcal F. ◻

High-degree comparison

The following graded vanishing lemma is the algebraic input behind the comparison of a finite graded module with its total twisted section module.

Lemma 1.1 (High-degree vanishing for graded local cohomology). Let M be a finitely generated graded S-module. Then, for every i\geq0, H^i_{S_+}(M)_d=0\text{ for all }d\gg0. In particular, the degree-d pieces of H^0_{S_+}(M) and H^1_{S_+}(M) vanish for all sufficiently large d.

Proof. Choose degree-one generators x_0,\ldots,x_r of S_+. The local cohomology modules are computed by the finite Čech complex 0\longrightarrow M \longrightarrow\bigoplus_iM_{x_i} \longrightarrow\bigoplus_{i<j}M_{x_ix_j} \longrightarrow\cdots \longrightarrow M_{x_0\cdots x_r} \longrightarrow0. The standard graded local-cohomology vanishing theorem states that the cohomology of this complex has zero homogeneous components in sufficiently large degree when S is noetherian and standard graded and M is finite. For completeness, one proves it by taking a finite set of homogeneous generators of M, clearing denominators in the above finite complex, and using noetherianity to make the required exponents uniform for the finitely many syzygy modules occurring in the kernels of the Čech differentials. Descending induction from the last term of the complex then gives, for each fixed cohomological degree i, an upper bound beyond which every cocycle is a coboundary. This is precisely the displayed vanishing. ◻

Proposition 1.1 (Eventual comparison with twisted global sections). Let M be a finitely generated graded S-module. Then \alpha_{M,d}:M_d \xrightarrow{\sim} \Gamma\left(X,\widetilde M(d)\right) is an isomorphism for every d\gg0.

Proof. Using the affine cover X=\bigcup_iD_+(x_i), the sheaf gluing condition identifies \Gamma\left(X,\widetilde M(d)\right) with the degree-d kernel of the first Čech differential \bigoplus_iM_{x_i} \longrightarrow \bigoplus_{i<j}M_{x_ix_j}. Consequently, the augmented Čech complex gives a natural exact sequence 0\longrightarrow H^0_{S_+}(M)_d \longrightarrow M_d \xrightarrow{\alpha_{M,d}} \Gamma\left(X,\widetilde M(d)\right) \longrightarrow H^1_{S_+}(M)_d \longrightarrow0. By Lemma , the outer terms vanish for all sufficiently large d. Hence \alpha_{M,d} is an isomorphism for d\gg0. ◻

The category of graded tails

Definition 1.16 (Quasi-finitely generated modules). Two graded S-modules M and M' are said to have the same eventual tail, written M\approx M', if there exists an integer d_0 and an isomorphism of graded modules M_{\geq d_0}\xrightarrow{\sim}M'_{\geq d_0}. A graded S-module M is called quasi-finitely generated if M\approx M' for some finitely generated graded S-module M'. We denote the category of such modules by \mathsf{QFgMod}(S).

Definition 1.17 (The tail category). Let \mathsf{Tail}(S) be the localization of \mathsf{QFgMod}(S) at the class of graded homomorphisms u:M\longrightarrow N for which u_d is an isomorphism for every d\gg0. Equivalently, one may view \mathsf{Tail}(S) as the category obtained by discarding bounded-degree information. We write \mathsf{Tail}(S)=\mathsf{QFgMod}(S)/{\approx}. The notation on the right is shorthand for this categorical localization; an equivalence relation on objects alone would not define a quotient category.

Remark 1.1. For finitely generated graded modules, a homomorphism is inverted in \mathsf{Tail}(S) exactly when its kernel and cokernel vanish in all sufficiently large degrees. Equivalently, one may describe the same category as the Serre quotient of finitely generated graded modules by the subcategory of modules annihilated by a power of S_+.

Lemma 1.1 (Finite graded presentations of coherent sheaves). Let \mathcal F be a coherent \mathcal O_X-module. Then there exist finite direct sums of shifted free modules E_1=\bigoplus_{j=1}^aS(-b_j), E_0=\bigoplus_{i=1}^cS(-a_i), and a graded homomorphism E_1\to E_0 such that, if M=\operatorname{coker}(E_1\to E_0), then M is finitely generated and \widetilde M\cong\mathcal F.

Proof. On each member of the finite affine cover D_+(x_i), choose finitely many generators of the finite module corresponding to \mathcal F|_{D_+(x_i)}. By Lemma , after multiplying each local generator by a suitable power of x_i, it extends to a global section of some twist \mathcal F(a). Each such section determines a morphism \mathcal O_X(-a)\longrightarrow\mathcal F. Taking the direct sum of the finitely many resulting morphisms gives a surjection \widetilde E_0\longrightarrow\mathcal F for some finite direct sum E_0 of shifts of S.

Its kernel \mathcal K is coherent. Applying the same argument to \mathcal K gives a finite direct sum E_1 and a surjection \widetilde E_1\longrightarrow\mathcal K. When extending the local generators of \mathcal K, we may multiply them by further powers of the relevant degree-one coordinates. We may therefore assume that every shift b_j occurring in E_1 is sufficiently large for Proposition  to apply to the finite free module E_0. The composite \mathcal O_X(-b_j)\longrightarrow\mathcal K \hookrightarrow\widetilde E_0 is then represented by a homogeneous element of (E_0)_{b_j}. Hence the composite \widetilde E_1\to\widetilde E_0 is the sheafification of a graded homomorphism E_1\to E_0. Sheafification is right exact, so \widetilde{\operatorname{coker}(E_1\to E_0)} \cong \operatorname{coker}(\widetilde E_1\to\widetilde E_0) \cong \mathcal F. ◻

Proposition 1.1 (Equivalence of Categories for Coherent Sheaves). Let S be a graded ring generated by S_1 as an S_0-algebra, where S_0=A is a finitely generated k-algebra for some field k, and S_1 is a finitely generated A-module. Let X=\operatorname{Proj}S. Then:

  1. For every graded S-module M, there is a natural graded homomorphism \alpha_M:M\longrightarrow\Gamma_*(\widetilde M).

  2. If M is finitely generated, then \alpha_{M,d}:M_d \xrightarrow{\sim} \Gamma\left(X,\widetilde M(d)\right) is an isomorphism for all d\gg0. Consequently, M\approx\Gamma_*(\widetilde M).

  3. The functors M\longmapsto\widetilde M, \mathcal F\longmapsto\Gamma_*(\mathcal F) induce quasi-inverse equivalences \mathsf{QFgMod}(S)/{\approx} \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathsf{Coh}(\mathcal O_X).

Proof. Part (a) is Proposition , and part (b) is Proposition .

We prove (c). By Proposition , every morphism which is an isomorphism in sufficiently large degree becomes an isomorphism after sheafification. Hence M\mapsto\widetilde M factors through the tail category.

Let \mathcal F be coherent. By Lemma , there exists a finitely generated graded module M with \widetilde M\cong\mathcal F. Therefore the sheafification functor is essentially surjective. Moreover, Proposition  gives a natural isomorphism \widetilde{\Gamma_*(\mathcal F)}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal F. Since \mathcal F\cong\widetilde M for a finite M, part (b) gives \Gamma_*(\mathcal F) \cong \Gamma_*(\widetilde M) \approx M. Thus \Gamma_*(\mathcal F) is quasi-finitely generated.

Finally, for every quasi-finitely generated module M, choose a finitely generated module M' with M\approx M'. Then M \approx M' \approx\Gamma_*(\widetilde{M'}) \cong\Gamma_*(\widetilde M). Thus the unit M\to\Gamma_*(\widetilde M) is an isomorphism in the tail category, while the counit \widetilde{\Gamma_*(\mathcal F)}\to\mathcal F is an isomorphism by Proposition . Hence the two induced functors are quasi-inverse equivalences. ◻

Remark 1.1 (The usual formulation). The preceding equivalence is often written \mathsf{qgr}(S) \simeq \mathsf{Coh}(\operatorname{Proj}S), where \mathsf{qgr}(S) denotes the Serre quotient of finitely generated graded S-modules by the finitely generated S_+-torsion modules. Under the present noetherian standard-graded hypotheses, this is equivalent to the tail formulation used above.

Homogeneous quotients and closed subschemes of \operatorname{Proj}

Let \mathfrak a\subseteq S be a homogeneous ideal and put \overline S=S/\mathfrak a. The quotient inherits its canonical grading \overline S_d=S_d/(\mathfrak a\cap S_d).

Lemma 1.1 (Homogeneous localization commutes with quotients). Let f\in S_d be homogeneous of positive degree, and let \overline f be its image in S/\mathfrak a. Then there is a canonical isomorphism (S/\mathfrak a)_{(\overline f)} \xrightarrow{\sim} S_{(f)}/\mathfrak a_{(f)}, where \mathfrak a_{(f)} \coloneqq (\mathfrak aS_f)_0 = \left\{ \frac{a}{f^r} \ \middle|\ a\in\mathfrak a\text{ homogeneous and }\deg a=rd \right\}. If \overline f=0, both sides are understood to be the zero ring and the corresponding standard open is empty.

Proof. The quotient homomorphism S\to S/\mathfrak a induces a surjective graded homomorphism S_f\longrightarrow(S/\mathfrak a)_{\overline f} whose kernel is \mathfrak aS_f. Taking degree-zero parts gives a surjective ring homomorphism S_{(f)}\longrightarrow(S/\mathfrak a)_{(\overline f)} with kernel (\mathfrak aS_f)_0=\mathfrak a_{(f)}. The first isomorphism theorem gives the assertion. ◻

Proposition 1.1 (Closed subschemes of projective spectra). Let \mathfrak a\subseteq S be a homogeneous ideal. The quotient map S\twoheadrightarrow S/\mathfrak a induces a canonical closed immersion i_{\mathfrak a}:\operatorname{Proj}(S/\mathfrak a) \hookrightarrow\operatorname{Proj}S. Its image on underlying topological spaces is V_+(\mathfrak a) = \{\mathfrak p\in\operatorname{Proj}S\mid \mathfrak a\subseteq\mathfrak p\}. Moreover, the defining quasi-coherent ideal sheaf is \widetilde{\mathfrak a}, and there is a canonical exact sequence 0\longrightarrow\widetilde{\mathfrak a} \longrightarrow\mathcal O_X \longrightarrow(i_{\mathfrak a})_* \mathcal O_{\operatorname{Proj}(S/\mathfrak a)} \longrightarrow0. Consequently, as closed subschemes of X, V_+(\mathfrak a)\cong\operatorname{Proj}(S/\mathfrak a).

Proof. A homogeneous prime ideal of S/\mathfrak a is uniquely of the form \mathfrak p/\mathfrak a, where \mathfrak p is a homogeneous prime ideal of S containing \mathfrak a. Furthermore, (S/\mathfrak a)_+ \subseteq\mathfrak p/\mathfrak a \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad S_+\subseteq\mathfrak p. Thus contraction gives a bijection \operatorname{Proj}(S/\mathfrak a) \xrightarrow{\sim}V_+(\mathfrak a). It is a homeomorphism because standard closed and open subsets are preserved under contraction.

To identify the scheme structure, let f\in S be homogeneous of positive degree. By the affine-chart theorem and Lemma , the restriction of the map to the standard chart is \operatorname{Spec}(S/\mathfrak a)_{(\overline f)} \cong \operatorname{Spec}\left(S_{(f)}/\mathfrak a_{(f)}\right) \hookrightarrow \operatorname{Spec}S_{(f)}. This is the affine closed immersion defined by the ideal \mathfrak a_{(f)}. These local closed immersions agree on overlaps, because they all arise from the same quotient map S\to S/\mathfrak a. They therefore glue to the asserted global closed immersion.

Finally, sheafifying the exact sequence of graded modules 0\longrightarrow\mathfrak a \longrightarrow S \longrightarrow S/\mathfrak a \longrightarrow0 gives the displayed exact sequence. Exactness can be checked on every standard affine chart, where it is precisely 0\longrightarrow\mathfrak a_{(f)} \longrightarrow S_{(f)} \longrightarrow(S/\mathfrak a)_{(\overline f)} \longrightarrow0. ◻

Remark 1.1 (Saturation). Different homogeneous ideals may define the same closed subscheme of \operatorname{Proj}S. In fact, replacing \mathfrak a by its S_+-saturation \mathfrak a^{\mathrm{sat}}=\{s\in S\mid S_+^r s\subseteq\mathfrak a \text{ for some }r\geq0\} does not change \widetilde{\mathfrak a} and hence does not change the closed subscheme V_+(\mathfrak a).

Every standard projective spectrum is globally projective

Theorem 1.1 (Projective embedding of a standard projective spectrum). Let A=S_0. Choose elements x_0,\ldots,x_n\in S_1 which generate S_1 as an A-module and hence generate S as an A-algebra. Then the graded homomorphism \Phi:A[X_0,\ldots,X_n]\longrightarrow S, X_i\longmapsto x_i, is surjective. Its kernel I=\ker\Phi is homogeneous, and it induces a canonical isomorphism \operatorname{Proj}S \xrightarrow{\sim} V_+(I)\subseteq\mathbb P_A^n. In particular, the structure morphism \operatorname{Proj}S\longrightarrow\operatorname{Spec}A is projective in the global sense. Under this closed immersion, \mathcal O_{\operatorname{Proj}S}(1) \cong \mathcal O_{\mathbb P_A^n}(1)|_{\operatorname{Proj}S}. Thus \mathcal O_{\operatorname{Proj}S}(1) is very ample over A.

Proof. Because S is generated by S_1 over A, the map \Phi is surjective. It preserves degrees, so its kernel I is homogeneous. Therefore S\cong A[X_0,\ldots,X_n]/I as graded A-algebras. Proposition gives \operatorname{Proj}S \cong \operatorname{Proj}\left(A[X_0,\ldots,X_n]/I\right) \cong V_+(I)\subseteq \operatorname{Proj}A[X_0,\ldots,X_n] = \mathbb P_A^n. This is a closed immersion over \operatorname{Spec}A, so the structure morphism is projective. The compatibility of twisting sheaves with graded quotients gives the last isomorphism. ◻

Proof. The ring S is noetherian by the Hilbert basis theorem, so \operatorname{Proj}S is noetherian. The preceding theorem realizes it as a closed subscheme of \mathbb P_A^n, and projective morphisms are proper. ◻

Ample Sheaf

Serre’s Theorem A

Definition 1.18 (Very Ample Invertible Sheaves). Let X be a scheme of finite type over S. An invertible sheaf \mathcal{L} on X is said to be very ample relative to S if there exists a closed immersion i: X \hookrightarrow \mathbb{P}^n_S into some projective space over S such that \mathcal{L} is isomorphic to the pullback of the Serre twisting sheaf: \mathcal{L} \cong i^* \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^n}(1).

Definition 1.19 (Ample Invertible Sheaves). Let X be a quasi-compact scheme over S. An invertible sheaf \mathcal{L} on X is said to be ample if for every coherent sheaf \mathcal{F} on X, there exists a positive integer n_0 (depending on \mathcal{F}) such that for all n \ge n_0, the twisted sheaf \mathcal{F} \otimes_{\mathcal{O}_X} \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n} is generated by its global sections.

Proposition 1.1 (Serre’s Theorem A). Let X be a scheme proper over \operatorname{Spec} A, let \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{O}_X(1) be a very ample invertible sheaf, and let \mathcal{F} be a coherent \mathcal{O}_X-module.

Then there exists an integer N such that for all n \ge N, the twisted sheaf \mathcal{F}(n) := \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n} is generated by global sections. Equivalently, there exists a surjection: \mathcal{O}_X^{\oplus m} \twoheadrightarrow \mathcal{F}(n).

Proof. Since \mathcal{L} is very ample, we have a closed immersion i: X \hookrightarrow \mathbb{P}^n_A (since X is proper, the immersion must be closed). We use the immersion to transfer the problem to \mathbb{P}^n_A.

Consider the pushforward sheaf i_* \mathcal{F}. Since i is a closed immersion, i_* is exact and preserves coherence. Furthermore, by the Projection Formula (Lemma ): i_*(\mathcal{F}(n)) \simeq i_*(\mathcal{F} \otimes i^*\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^n_A}(n)) \simeq (i_* \mathcal{F}) \otimes \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^n_A}(n) \simeq (i_* \mathcal{F})(n). Thus, \mathcal{F}(n) is globally generated on X if and only if (i_* \mathcal{F})(n) is globally generated on \mathbb{P}^n_A. We reduce to the case X = \mathbb{P}^n_A.

On \mathbb{P}^n_A, we cover the space by D_+(x_i). Restricted to D_+(x_i), the coherent sheaf corresponds to a finitely generated module M_i over the polynomial ring. Let \{s_{ij}\} be a finite set of generators for M_i. By the Extension Lemma (from previous sections), for n large enough, x_i^n s_{ij} extends to a global section of \mathcal{F}(n). Since there are finitely many charts and finitely many generators, we can choose a single large N such that for all n \ge N, the global sections generate the sheaf at every point. ◻

Proposition 1.1 (Serre’s Criterion for Ampleness). Let A be a Noetherian ring, and let X be a proper scheme over \operatorname{Spec}A. Let \mathcal{L} be an invertible sheaf on X. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

  1. \mathcal{L} is ample.

  2. For every coherent sheaf \mathcal{F} on X, there exists an integer n_0 (depending on \mathcal{F}) such that for all i > 0 and all n \ge n_0: \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n}) = 0.

Proof. Direction (i) \Rightarrow (ii): Reduction to Serre’s Vanishing

Suppose \mathcal{L} is ample. By the definition of ampleness (on a proper scheme over a Noetherian base), some tensor power \mathcal{L}^{\otimes m} is very ample over \operatorname{Spec}A for some m > 0. This implies there is an immersion X \hookrightarrow \mathbb{P}^N_A such that \mathcal{O}(1)|_X \cong \mathcal{L}^{\otimes m}. Since X is proper over A, this immersion is a closed immersion, so X is a projective scheme.

We need to show vanishing for \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n} for all large n, not just multiples of m. We use a division algorithm argument. Let \mathcal{F} be any coherent sheaf. For any integer r \in \{0, 1, \dots, m-1\}, consider the coherent sheaf: \mathcal{F}_r := \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes r}. Since X is projective (via the embedding given by \mathcal{L}^{\otimes m}), we can apply Serre’s Vanishing Theorem to the sheaf \mathcal{F}_r with respect to the very ample sheaf \mathcal{H} = \mathcal{L}^{\otimes m}. This theorem guarantees the existence of an integer k_r such that for all k \ge k_r and all i > 0: \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F}_r \otimes \mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}) = 0. Substituting the definitions, this means: \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes r} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes mk}) = \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes (mk+r)}) = 0. Let n_0 = \max_{0 \le r < m} \{ m k_r + r \}. Any integer n \ge n_0 can be written as n = mk + r for some r \in \{0, \dots, m-1\} and k \ge k_r. Thus, \operatorname{H}^i(X, \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n}) = 0 for all n \ge n_0.

Direction (ii) \Rightarrow (i): Establishing Global Generation

To prove \mathcal{L} is ample, it suffices to show that for any coherent sheaf \mathcal{F}, the twisted sheaf \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n} is generated by global sections for all n \gg 0.

Step 1: Pointwise Generation via \operatorname{H}^1 Vanishing. Let P \in X be a closed point. Let \mathcal{I}_P be the ideal sheaf of P. We have the standard short exact sequence: 0 \longrightarrow \mathcal{I}_P \longrightarrow \mathcal{O}_X \longrightarrow k(P) \longrightarrow 0, where k(P) is the skyscraper sheaf supported at P. Tensor this sequence with \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n} (which is right exact, and exact here since \mathcal{F} is locally free or we analyze stalks... actually, tensoring preserves exactness of sheaf sequences if the rightmost term is a skyscraper or we just tensor the whole complex). More precisely, since k(P) is a skyscraper, \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n} \otimes k(P) \cong (\mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n})|_P. The exact sequence is: 0 \longrightarrow \mathcal{I}_P \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n} \longrightarrow \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n} \longrightarrow \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n} \otimes k(P) \longrightarrow 0. Consider the long exact sequence of cohomology. The relevant part is: \Gamma(X, \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n}) \xrightarrow{\alpha} \Gamma(X, \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n} \otimes k(P)) \longrightarrow \operatorname{H}^1(X, \mathcal{I}_P \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n}). By hypothesis (ii), applied to the coherent sheaf \mathcal{I}_P \mathcal{F}, there exists n_0(P) such that for all n \ge n_0(P), the \operatorname{H}^1 term vanishes. Consequently, the map \alpha is surjective.

Step 2: From Fiber to Neighborhood (Nakayama’s Lemma). The term on the right, \Gamma(X, \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n} \otimes k(P)), is simply the fiber of the sheaf at P, i.e., (\mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n})_P / \mathfrak{m}_P (\mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n})_P. The surjectivity of \alpha means we can lift a basis of the fiber to global sections s_1, \dots, s_k \in \Gamma(X, \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n}). By Nakayama’s Lemma, these sections generate the stalk (\mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n})_P as an \mathcal{O}_{X,P}-module. Since \mathcal{F} is coherent (finite type), sections that generate the stalk at P will generate the sheaf over some open neighborhood U_P of P.

Step 3: Uniformity over X (The Arithmetic Trick). The above shows generation locally. We need global generation for all n \ge N. This requires a bit of care because n_0(P) depends on P.

Take \mathcal{F} = \mathcal{O}_X. We proved that for any P, there is a neighborhood V and an integer n_1 > 0 such that \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n_1} is generated by global sections on V. Applying the argument again for each r \in \{0, 1, \dots, n_1-1\}, there exists a neighborhood U_r of P and integer M_r such that \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes (M_r)} is generated by global sections. Let’s use the property of generated sheaves: if \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n_1} is generated by sections, then its powers (\mathcal{L}^{\otimes n_1})^{\otimes k} are also generated.

Let’s refine the covering argument: For a fixed P, we found n_1 and V such that \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n_1} is globally generated on V. For each r \in \{0, \dots, n_1-1\}, apply the local generation argument to \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes r}. We find a neighborhood V_r \subseteq V and a base threshold k_r such that \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes r} \otimes (\mathcal{L}^{\otimes n_1})^{\otimes k} is generated by global sections on V_r for k \ge k_r. (Note: If \mathcal{A} and \mathcal{B} are generated by global sections, so is \mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}. We are using sections of \mathcal{L}^{n_1 k} and sections of \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^r).

Let U_P = \bigcap_{r=0}^{n_1-1} V_r. On this neighborhood, \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n} is generated by global sections for all n \ge \max(n_1 k_r + r).

Since X is proper (hence quasi-compact), we can cover X by finitely many such neighborhoods U_{P_1}, \dots, U_{P_j}. Let N be the maximum of the thresholds for each neighborhood. Then for all n \ge N, \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{\otimes n} is generated by global sections everywhere on X. Thus \mathcal{L} is ample. ◻

Equivalent criteria for ampleness

In the remainder of this section, let X be a noetherian scheme and let \mathcal L be an invertible \mathcal O_X-module. For a section s\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal L^{\otimes n}),n>0, we continue to write X_s for its nonvanishing locus.

Theorem 1.1 (Equivalent criteria for an ample invertible sheaf). The following conditions are equivalent.

  1. \mathcal L is ample.

  2. For every integer m>0, the power \mathcal L^{\otimes m} is ample.

  3. For some integer m>0, the power \mathcal L^{\otimes m} is ample.

  4. For every coherent ideal sheaf \mathcal I\subseteq\mathcal O_X, there exists n>0 such that \mathcal I\otimes\mathcal L^{\otimes n} is generated by finitely many global sections.

  5. For every coherent ideal sheaf \mathcal I\subseteq\mathcal O_X, the sheaf \mathcal I\otimes\mathcal L^{\otimes n} is generated by finitely many global sections for all n\gg0.

  6. The open subsets X_s, s\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal L^{\otimes n}),\ n>0, form a basis for the topology of X.

  7. The affine open subsets of the form X_s, with s\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal L^{\otimes n}) and n>0, form a basis for the topology of X.

  8. There exist an integer d>0 and finitely many sections s_0,\ldots,s_r\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal L^{\otimes d}) such that every X_{s_i} is affine and X=X_{s_0}\cup\cdots\cup X_{s_r}.

Proof. We divide the proof into several steps.

Step 1: Positive tensor powers. Suppose \mathcal L is ample. If s\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal L^{\otimes n}), then X_s=X_{s^m}, s^m\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal L^{\otimes mn}). Hence every affine standard neighborhood used to verify the ampleness of \mathcal L is also a standard neighborhood for \mathcal L^{\otimes m}. Thus (i) implies (ii). The implication (ii)\Rightarrow(iii) is immediate. If \mathcal L^{\otimes m} is ample, then its standard neighborhoods are of the form X_s, s\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal L^{\otimes mr}), which are also standard neighborhoods arising from positive powers of \mathcal L. Hence (iii) implies (i).

Step 2: Global generation of ideal sheaves. By Serre’s Theorem A, if \mathcal L is ample and \mathcal F is coherent, then \mathcal F\otimes\mathcal L^{\otimes n} is generated by finitely many global sections for all n\gg0. Applying this to \mathcal F=\mathcal I proves (i)\Rightarrow(v), and (v)\Rightarrow(iv) is immediate.

Assume (iv). Let x\in X and let U be an open neighborhood of x. Choose an affine open neighborhood x\in V\subseteq U on which \mathcal L is trivial. Since X is noetherian, the closed subset Z=X\setminus V, endowed with its reduced closed subscheme structure, has a coherent ideal sheaf \mathcal I_Z\subseteq\mathcal O_X. By (iv), for some n>0 the sheaf \mathcal I_Z\otimes\mathcal L^{\otimes n} is globally generated. Since (\mathcal I_Z)_x=\mathcal O_{X,x}, one of its global generators has a germ which does not vanish at x. Through the inclusion \mathcal I_Z\otimes\mathcal L^{\otimes n} \hookrightarrow \mathcal L^{\otimes n}, regard this generator as a section s\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal L^{\otimes n}). It vanishes along Z and does not vanish at x. Therefore x\in X_s\subseteq V\subseteq U. This proves (vi).

Step 3: From a topological basis to affine standard opens. Assume (vi). Given x\in X and an open neighborhood U, choose an affine open x\in V\subseteq U on which \mathcal L is trivial. By (vi), there exist n>0 and s\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal L^{\otimes n}) such that x\in X_s\subseteq V. Choose a trivializing generator e of \mathcal L|_V. Then s|_V=g e^{\otimes n} for some g\in\Gamma(V,\mathcal O_V), and hence X_s=D_V(g). Thus X_s is affine. This proves (vii).

The implication (vii)\Rightarrow(i) is precisely the definition of ampleness.

Step 4: A finite cover in one common degree. Assume (vii). Since X is noetherian, it is quasi-compact, so there are finitely many affine standard opens X=X_{t_0}\cup\cdots\cup X_{t_r}, t_i\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal L^{\otimes d_i}), \quad d_i>0. Let d be a common multiple of d_0,\ldots,d_r and set s_i=t_i^{d/d_i} \in\Gamma(X,\mathcal L^{\otimes d}). Since X_{s_i}=X_{t_i}, condition (viii) follows. Conversely, (viii) immediately implies (i). ◻

Remark 1.1. Condition (iv) is often stated for quasi-coherent ideal sheaves of finite type. On a noetherian scheme this is the same as requiring the ideal sheaf to be coherent.

The proper noetherian case: very ampleness and cohomology

The next group of equivalences requires properness. Mere noetherianity is not sufficient.

Lemma 1.1 (Tensoring a very ample sheaf by a generated sheaf). Let A be a ring and let X be an A-scheme. If \mathcal V is very ample over A and \mathcal G is an invertible sheaf generated by finitely many global sections, then \mathcal V\otimes\mathcal G is very ample over A.

Proof. Choose a closed immersion i:X\hookrightarrow\mathbb P_A^N with \mathcal V\cong i^*\mathcal O_{\mathbb P_A^N}(1). A finite set of generators of \mathcal G defines a morphism g:X\longrightarrow\mathbb P_A^M with \mathcal G\cong g^*\mathcal O_{\mathbb P_A^M}(1). The product map (i,g):X\longrightarrow \mathbb P_A^N\times_A\mathbb P_A^M is a closed immersion because its first component is a closed immersion. Composing with the Segre closed immersion \mathbb P_A^N\times_A\mathbb P_A^M \hookrightarrow\mathbb P_A^{(N+1)(M+1)-1} gives a closed immersion whose pullback of \mathcal O(1) is \mathcal V\otimes\mathcal G. ◻

Proposition 1.1 (Finite generation of the section ring). Let A be noetherian, let p:X\to\operatorname{Spec}A be proper, and let \mathcal L be ample. Set R(X,\mathcal L) \coloneqq \bigoplus_{n\geq0} \Gamma(X,\mathcal L^{\otimes n}). Then R(X,\mathcal L) is a finitely generated graded A-algebra. For some e>0, the Veronese subring R(X,\mathcal L)^{(e)} = \bigoplus_{q\geq0} \Gamma(X,\mathcal L^{\otimes eq}) is generated in degree one.

Proof. By Serre’s Theorem A, choose d>0 such that \mathcal L^{\otimes d} is generated by finitely many global sections. Put V=\Gamma(X,\mathcal L^{\otimes d}). Properness over the noetherian ring A implies that V is a finite A-module. Let \mathcal K be the kernel of the evaluation map 0\longrightarrow\mathcal K \longrightarrow V\otimes_A\mathcal O_X \longrightarrow\mathcal L^{\otimes d} \longrightarrow0. The sheaf \mathcal K is coherent. Tensoring by \mathcal L^{\otimes qd} and taking cohomology gives V\otimes_A\Gamma(X,\mathcal L^{\otimes qd}) \longrightarrow \Gamma(X,\mathcal L^{\otimes(q+1)d}) \longrightarrow H^1(X,\mathcal K\otimes\mathcal L^{\otimes qd}). By Serre vanishing, the last term is zero for q\gg0. Hence multiplication R_d\otimes_A R_{qd}\longrightarrow R_{(q+1)d} is surjective for all sufficiently large q. It follows that the Veronese ring R(X,\mathcal L)^{(d)} is generated over A by finitely many of its homogeneous pieces. Each such piece is a finite A-module, so this Veronese ring is a finitely generated A-algebra.

To recover the full ring, fix 0\leq r<d and apply the same argument after tensoring the evaluation sequence by \mathcal L^{\otimes(qd+r)}. Serre vanishing gives surjections R_d\otimes_A R_{qd+r}\longrightarrow R_{(q+1)d+r} for all sufficiently large q. Hence \bigoplus_{q\geq0}R_{qd+r} is a finitely generated graded module over R(X,\mathcal L)^{(d)}. Summing over the finitely many residue classes r shows that the full section ring R(X,\mathcal L) is finite as a module over its d-th Veronese subring, and is therefore a finitely generated A-algebra.

A sufficiently divisible Veronese subring of a finitely generated graded algebra is generated in degree one. Thus R(X,\mathcal L)^{(e)} is standard graded for some e>0. ◻

Theorem 1.1 (Proper ampleness criteria). Let A be a noetherian ring, let p:X\longrightarrow\operatorname{Spec}A be a proper morphism, and let \mathcal L be an invertible sheaf on X. Then the conditions of Theorem  are also equivalent to each of the following.

  1. There exists N such that \mathcal L^{\otimes n} is very ample over A for every n\geq N.

  2. The power \mathcal L^{\otimes n} is very ample over A for some n>0.

  3. For every coherent \mathcal O_X-module \mathcal F, every i>0, and all n\gg0, one has H^i\!\left(X, \mathcal F\otimes\mathcal L^{\otimes n} \right)=0.

  4. The canonical morphism associated with the complete section ring is an isomorphism: X\xrightarrow{\sim} \operatorname{Proj} \left( \bigoplus_{n\geq0} \Gamma(X,\mathcal L^{\otimes n}) \right).

Proof. Let R=R(X,\mathcal L) = \bigoplus_{n\geq0}\Gamma(X,\mathcal L^{\otimes n}).

Ampleness implies recovery from the section ring. Assume \mathcal L is ample. By Theorem , the opens X_s, where s runs through homogeneous elements of positive degree in R, form a basis. The canonical morphism r_{\mathcal L}:X\longrightarrow\operatorname{Proj}R is therefore defined everywhere. On every affine standard open X_s, the localization theorem for sections gives \Gamma(X_s,\mathcal O_X)\cong R_{(s)}, so r_{\mathcal L} identifies X_s with D_+(s). Consequently, r_{\mathcal L} is an open immersion. Its image is dense in \operatorname{Proj}R.

Since X is proper over A and \operatorname{Proj}R is separated over A, the morphism r_{\mathcal L} is proper. A proper open immersion has closed image. The image is therefore both dense and closed, so it is all of \operatorname{Proj}R. Hence r_{\mathcal L} is an isomorphism. This proves (d).

Conversely, if (d) holds, then the inverse images of the standard affine opens D_+(s) are precisely the opens X_s. These opens form a basis, so Theorem  shows that \mathcal L is ample.

Existence of a very ample power. Assume \mathcal L is ample. By Proposition , choose e>0 such that R^{(e)} is generated in degree one. Veronese invariance and (d) give X\cong\operatorname{Proj}R \cong\operatorname{Proj}R^{(e)}. Choose finite A-module generators y_0,\ldots,y_N of R_e=\Gamma(X,\mathcal L^{\otimes e}). Since R^{(e)} is generated in degree one, they induce a surjection A[Y_0,\ldots,Y_N]\twoheadrightarrow R^{(e)}. By Theorem , this gives a closed immersion X\cong\operatorname{Proj}R^{(e)} \hookrightarrow\mathbb P_A^N whose pullback of \mathcal O(1) is \mathcal L^{\otimes e}. Thus \mathcal L^{\otimes e} is very ample, proving (b).

If some positive power of \mathcal L is very ample, then that power is ample. The power criterion in Theorem  implies that \mathcal L is ample. Hence (b) is equivalent to ampleness.

All sufficiently large powers are very ample. Choose d>0 such that \mathcal L^{\otimes d} is very ample. By Serre’s Theorem A, there exists r_0 such that \mathcal L^{\otimes r} is globally generated for every r\geq r_0. For each residue class modulo d, choose an integer r_j\geq r_0, r_j\equiv j\pmod d. For every sufficiently large n, one can write n=r_j+qd with q\geq1. Then \mathcal L^{\otimes n} \cong \mathcal L^{\otimes r_j} \otimes (\mathcal L^{\otimes d})^{\otimes q}. The first factor is globally generated, and the second is very ample. Lemma  shows that \mathcal L^{\otimes n} is very ample. This proves (a). Clearly (a) implies (b).

The cohomological criterion. If \mathcal L is ample, Serre vanishing gives H^i(X,\mathcal F\otimes\mathcal L^{\otimes n})=0 for every coherent \mathcal F, every i>0, and all n\gg0. Thus ampleness implies (c).

Conversely, assume (c). Applying it in degree i=1 to coherent ideal sheaves gives the cohomological criterion for ampleness: the vanishing of H^1(X,\mathcal I\otimes\mathcal L^{\otimes n}) for sufficiently large n, for every coherent ideal sheaf \mathcal I, forces the standard opens defined by positive powers of \mathcal L to form an affine basis. Hence \mathcal L is ample by Theorem . ◻

Remark 1.1 (Why properness is necessary). The equivalence with eventual very ampleness is false without properness. For example, on the affine line X=\mathbb A_k^1, the trivial invertible sheaf \mathcal O_X is ample because X is affine, but no power of it can give a closed immersion of X into a projective space over k.

For a general noetherian scheme with an ample invertible sheaf, the canonical morphism X\longrightarrow \operatorname{Proj} \left( \bigoplus_{n\geq0}\Gamma(X,\mathcal L^{\otimes n}) \right) is an open immersion with dense image. Properness is what forces this open immersion to be surjective.

Remark 1.1 (Coherent, not arbitrary quasi-coherent). In the cohomological criterion, \mathcal F must be coherent, or at least of finite type under suitable hypotheses. The assertion is false for an arbitrary quasi-coherent sheaf. For instance, on \mathbb P_k^1 take \mathcal F=\bigoplus_{m\geq0}\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(-m). For every fixed n, the group H^1(\mathbb P_k^1,\mathcal F(n)) contains nonzero summands H^1(\mathbb P_k^1,\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(n-m)) for all sufficiently large m. Thus no uniform positive twist annihilates the higher cohomology of this \mathcal F.

Tensor products involving ample invertible sheaves

We first record several useful stability properties. Throughout this subsection, X is a noetherian scheme and \mathcal L,\mathcal M are invertible \mathcal O_X-modules.

Lemma 1.1 (A generated factor preserves ampleness). Suppose that \mathcal A is ample and that \mathcal G is an invertible sheaf generated by global sections. Then \mathcal A\otimes_{\mathcal O_X}\mathcal G is ample.

More generally, it is enough to assume that, for some m>0, \mathcal G^{\otimes m} is generated by global sections.

Proof. Since \mathcal G^{\otimes m} is globally generated, the open subsets X_t, t\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal G^{\otimes m}), cover X. Let x\in X. Since \mathcal A is ample, there exist an integer n>0 and a section s\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal A^{\otimes n}) such that x\in X_s and X_s is affine. Choose t\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal G^{\otimes m}) with x\in X_t. Then s^m\otimes t^n \in \Gamma\!\left( X,(\mathcal A\otimes\mathcal G)^{\otimes mn} \right) and X_{s^m\otimes t^n}=X_s\cap X_t. The latter is a principal open subset of the affine scheme X_s, hence is affine. It contains x, so \mathcal A\otimes\mathcal G is ample. ◻

Proposition 1.1 (Twisting by an arbitrary invertible sheaf). Assume that \mathcal L is ample. Then, for every invertible sheaf \mathcal M, there exists an integer N such that, for every n\geq N, \mathcal L^{\otimes n}\otimes\mathcal M is both ample and generated by finitely many global sections.

Proof. By Serre’s Theorem A, applied to the coherent sheaf \mathcal M, there is an integer N_0 such that \mathcal L^{\otimes r}\otimes\mathcal M is generated by finitely many global sections for every r\geq N_0. For n\geq N_0+1, write \mathcal L^{\otimes n}\otimes\mathcal M \cong \mathcal L\otimes \left(\mathcal L^{\otimes(n-1)}\otimes\mathcal M\right). The first factor is ample, and the second factor is globally generated. Lemma  therefore shows that the tensor product is ample. Increasing N_0 if necessary gives the assertion. ◻

Proposition 1.1 (Products and a cancellation criterion). Let \mathcal L and \mathcal M be invertible sheaves on X.

  1. If both \mathcal L and \mathcal M are ample, then \mathcal L\otimes\mathcal M is ample.

  2. Suppose that \mathcal M^{\otimes m} is generated by global sections for some m>0. If \mathcal L\otimes\mathcal M^{-1} is ample, then \mathcal L is ample.

Proof. For (i), ampleness of \mathcal M implies that the open subsets X_t, with t\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal M^{\otimes r}), r>0, cover X. Applying Lemma  in its proof-level form to the ample sheaf \mathcal L and to \mathcal M gives the result.

For (ii), set \mathcal A=\mathcal L\otimes\mathcal M^{-1}. By hypothesis \mathcal A is ample, while the positive power \mathcal M^{\otimes m} is globally generated. Hence Lemma  gives \mathcal L \cong \mathcal A\otimes\mathcal M as an ample invertible sheaf. ◻

Remark 1.1 (A tempting but false cancellation statement). The condition that \mathcal M^{\otimes m} be globally generated and that \mathcal L\otimes\mathcal M be ample does not imply that \mathcal L is ample. For example, on \mathbb P_k^1 take \mathcal L=\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(-1), \mathcal M=\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(2). Then \mathcal M is globally generated and \mathcal L\otimes\mathcal M \cong \mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(1) is ample, whereas \mathcal L is not ample. This explains the inverse on \mathcal M in Proposition .

Projective Bundles and Relative Very Ampleness

The relative projective spectrum

Let S be a scheme and let \mathcal S_\bullet = \bigoplus_{d\geq0}\mathcal S_d be a quasi-coherent graded \mathcal O_S-algebra. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, we assume \mathcal S_0=\mathcal O_S and that \mathcal S_\bullet is generated by \mathcal S_1 as an \mathcal O_S-algebra. We write \mathcal S_+ = \bigoplus_{d>0}\mathcal S_d for its irrelevant ideal sheaf.

The purpose of the relative construction is to perform the ordinary \operatorname{Proj} construction on affine open subsets of S and then glue the resulting schemes.

Lemma 1.1 (Affine base change for projective spectra). Let A\to B be a homomorphism of rings and let R=\bigoplus_{d\geq0}R_d be a graded A-algebra. Give B\otimes_A R the grading (B\otimes_A R)_d=B\otimes_A R_d. Then there is a canonical isomorphism \operatorname{Proj}(B\otimes_A R) \xrightarrow{\sim} \operatorname{Proj}(R)\times_{\operatorname{Spec}A} \operatorname{Spec}B. For every graded R-module M and every n\in\mathbb Z, this isomorphism identifies the pullback of \widetilde{M(n)} with \widetilde{(B\otimes_A M)(n)}. In particular, it identifies the pullback of \mathcal O_{\operatorname{Proj}R}(n) with \mathcal O_{\operatorname{Proj}(B\otimes_A R)}(n).

Proof. Let f\in R_d be homogeneous of positive degree. Since B is placed in degree zero, localization and passage to the degree-zero part give canonical isomorphisms (B\otimes_A R)_{(1\otimes f)} \cong B\otimes_A R_{(f)} and (B\otimes_A M)_{(1\otimes f)} \cong B\otimes_A M_{(f)}. Consequently, D_+(1\otimes f) \cong \operatorname{Spec}(B\otimes_A R_{(f)}) \cong D_+(f)\times_{\operatorname{Spec}A}\operatorname{Spec}B. These isomorphisms are compatible with further homogeneous localization. The standard open subsets D_+(f) cover \operatorname{Proj}R, and the subsets D_+(1\otimes f) cover the inverse image of this scheme after base change. Hence the affine isomorphisms glue to the asserted global isomorphism.

The module assertion follows from the same calculation applied to M(n). Since B\otimes_A M(n)\cong(B\otimes_A M)(n) as graded B\otimes_A R-modules, the claimed compatibility with twists follows. ◻

Definition 1.20 (Relative projective spectrum). Choose an affine open covering S=\bigcup_{i\in I}U_i, U_i=\operatorname{Spec}A_i, and put R_i = \Gamma(U_i,\mathcal S_\bullet) = \bigoplus_{d\geq0}\Gamma(U_i,\mathcal S_d). For each i, form the ordinary projective spectrum X_i=\operatorname{Proj}R_i. If V=D(a)\subseteq U_i is a principal affine open subset, then quasi-coherence gives \Gamma(V,\mathcal S_\bullet) \cong (R_i)_a \cong A_{i,a}\otimes_{A_i}R_i. Lemma  therefore yields a canonical identification \operatorname{Proj}\Gamma(V,\mathcal S_\bullet) \cong X_i\times_{U_i}V. These identifications satisfy the cocycle condition under further restriction. They therefore glue the schemes X_i to an S-scheme \pi: \mathbf{Proj}_S(\mathcal S_\bullet) = \underline{\operatorname{Proj}}_S(\mathcal S_\bullet) \longrightarrow S, called the relative projective spectrum of \mathcal S_\bullet.

Remark 1.1 (Independence of the affine covering). The preceding construction is independent, up to unique canonical isomorphism, of the chosen affine covering. Indeed, two affine coverings have a common refinement by principal affine open subsets, and the localization isomorphisms used in the construction agree on every such refinement.

Proposition 1.1 (Local description and standard opens). Let X=\mathbf{Proj}_S(\mathcal S_\bullet), \pi:X\to S. If U=\operatorname{Spec}A\subseteq S is affine and R=\Gamma(U,\mathcal S_\bullet), then there is a canonical isomorphism of U-schemes \pi^{-1}(U)\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{Proj}R. If f\in\Gamma(U,\mathcal S_d)=R_d is homogeneous of positive degree, then D_+(f) \coloneqq \{x\in\pi^{-1}(U)\mid f\notin\mathfrak p_x\} is affine and D_+(f)\cong\operatorname{Spec}R_{(f)}. As U and f vary, these subsets form a basis for the topology of X.

Proof. The first assertion is part of the gluing construction. Under this identification, the second assertion is the usual affine-chart theorem for \operatorname{Proj}R. Every point of \operatorname{Proj}R avoids some homogeneous element of R_+, so the D_+(f) cover \pi^{-1}(U). Refining the affine covering of S and using D_+(f)\cap D_+(g)=D_+(fg) shows that all such standard opens form a basis of X. ◻

Definition 1.21 (Twisting sheaves on a relative projective spectrum). For n\in\mathbb Z, the sheaves \widetilde{R(n)} \quad\text{on}\quad \operatorname{Proj}R are compatible with restriction to principal affine open subsets of the base. They therefore glue to a quasi-coherent sheaf \mathcal O_X(n) = \mathcal O_{\mathbf{Proj}_S(\mathcal S_\bullet)}(n) on X.

More generally, if \mathcal M_\bullet=\bigoplus_{d\in\mathbb Z}\mathcal M_d is a quasi-coherent graded \mathcal S_\bullet-module, the affine sheaves associated to \Gamma(U,\mathcal M_\bullet) glue to a quasi-coherent sheaf \widetilde{\mathcal M_\bullet} on X.

Proposition 1.1 (Invertibility of the relative twisting sheaf). Assume that \mathcal S_\bullet is generated by \mathcal S_1 over \mathcal O_S. Then \mathcal O_X(1) is invertible and, for every n\in\mathbb Z, there are canonical isomorphisms \mathcal O_X(n) \cong \mathcal O_X(1)^{\otimes n}. In particular, \mathcal O_X(n)\otimes\mathcal O_X(m) \cong \mathcal O_X(n+m).

Proof. The assertion is local on S. Let U=\operatorname{Spec}A and R=\Gamma(U,\mathcal S_\bullet). Since R is generated by R_1, the open subsets D_+(f), f\in R_1, cover \operatorname{Proj}R. On D_+(f), multiplication by f^n gives an isomorphism \mathcal O_{D_+(f)} \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathcal O_X(n)|_{D_+(f)}. These local descriptions imply that \mathcal O_X(1) is invertible and that the natural multiplication maps \mathcal O_X(n)\otimes\mathcal O_X(m) \longrightarrow \mathcal O_X(n+m) are isomorphisms. ◻

Theorem 1.1 (Base change for relative projective spectra). Let g:T\to S be any morphism of schemes. Then there is a canonical isomorphism of T-schemes { \mathbf{Proj}_T(g^*\mathcal S_\bullet) \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathbf{Proj}_S(\mathcal S_\bullet)\times_S T. } If p denotes the projection from the fiber product to \mathbf{Proj}_S(\mathcal S_\bullet), then for every n\in\mathbb Z, p^*\mathcal O_{\mathbf{Proj}_S(\mathcal S_\bullet)}(n) \cong \mathcal O_{\mathbf{Proj}_T(g^*\mathcal S_\bullet)}(n). More generally, for every quasi-coherent graded \mathcal S_\bullet-module \mathcal M_\bullet, p^*\widetilde{\mathcal M_\bullet} \cong \widetilde{g^*\mathcal M_\bullet}.

Proof. The assertion is local on both S and T. Choose affine open subsets U=\operatorname{Spec}A\subseteq S, W=\operatorname{Spec}B\subseteq g^{-1}(U). Put R=\Gamma(U,\mathcal S_\bullet). On W, the graded algebra g^*\mathcal S_\bullet corresponds to B\otimes_A R. Lemma  gives \operatorname{Proj}(B\otimes_A R) \cong \operatorname{Proj}R\times_{\operatorname{Spec}A}\operatorname{Spec}B. These affine-local isomorphisms are canonical and compatible on overlaps, so they glue to the displayed global isomorphism. The statements about \mathcal O(n) and graded modules are the corresponding module assertions in Lemma . ◻

Morphisms induced by graded algebra homomorphisms

Let \phi:\mathcal S_\bullet\longrightarrow\mathcal T_\bullet be a homomorphism of quasi-coherent graded \mathcal O_S-algebras which preserves degrees and is the identity in degree zero. Put X=\mathbf{Proj}_S(\mathcal S_\bullet), Y=\mathbf{Proj}_S(\mathcal T_\bullet). As in the absolute case, \phi need not define a morphism on all of Y.

Definition 1.22 (The natural domain of definition). For an affine open subset U\subseteq S, write R=\Gamma(U,\mathcal S_\bullet), T=\Gamma(U,\mathcal T_\bullet). Inside Y_U=\operatorname{Proj}T define V_{\phi,U} = \bigcup_{\substack{f\in R_d\\d>0}} D_+(\phi(f)). Equivalently, V_{\phi,U} = \left\{ \mathfrak q\in\operatorname{Proj}T \ \middle|\ R_+\nsubseteq\phi^{-1}(\mathfrak q) \right\}. These open subsets are compatible with restriction on the base and hence glue to an open subscheme V_\phi\subseteq Y. We call V_\phi the natural domain of definition of \mathbf{Proj}(\phi).

Theorem 1.1 (The morphism induced by a graded homomorphism). There is a canonical S-morphism { \mathbf{Proj}(\phi): V_\phi\longrightarrow \mathbf{Proj}_S(\mathcal S_\bullet). } On an affine open U=\operatorname{Spec}A\subseteq S, its map on points is \mathfrak q\longmapsto\phi^{-1}(\mathfrak q). For every homogeneous f\in\Gamma(U,\mathcal S_d) of positive degree, its restriction D_+(\phi(f))\longrightarrow D_+(f) is the affine morphism induced by the degree-zero localization map \mathcal S(U)_{(f)} \longrightarrow \mathcal T(U)_{(\phi(f))}, \frac{a}{f^r} \longmapsto \frac{\phi(a)}{\phi(f)^r}.

Proof. The displayed localization map is well defined and induces an affine morphism D_+(\phi(f)) = \operatorname{Spec}\mathcal T(U)_{(\phi(f))} \longrightarrow \operatorname{Spec}\mathcal S(U)_{(f)} = D_+(f). If f and g are homogeneous, the two morphisms agree on D_+(\phi(f))\cap D_+(\phi(g)) = D_+(\phi(fg)), because both are induced by the same graded map after further localization. Hence the affine morphisms glue over V_{\phi,U}. Their compatibility with degree-zero localization on the base shows that the morphisms obtained for different affine open subsets U also glue.

For a homogeneous prime \mathfrak q\in V_{\phi,U}, the inverse image \phi^{-1}(\mathfrak q) is a homogeneous prime ideal. The defining condition of V_{\phi,U} says precisely that it does not contain R_+, so it is a point of \operatorname{Proj}R. This proves the asserted formula on points. ◻

Proposition 1.1 (Compatibility with the twisting sheaf). Assume that \mathcal S_\bullet is generated by \mathcal S_1 over \mathcal O_S. Then, for every n\in\mathbb Z, there is a canonical isomorphism \mathbf{Proj}(\phi)^* \mathcal O_{\mathbf{Proj}_S(\mathcal S_\bullet)}(n) \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathcal O_{\mathbf{Proj}_S(\mathcal T_\bullet)}(n)|_{V_\phi}. In particular, { \mathbf{Proj}(\phi)^* \mathcal O_{\mathbf{Proj}_S(\mathcal S_\bullet)}(1) \cong \mathcal O_{\mathbf{Proj}_S(\mathcal T_\bullet)}(1)|_{V_\phi}. }

Proof. The assertion is local on S and on V_\phi. Since \mathcal S_\bullet is generated in degree one, V_\phi is covered by open subsets D_+(\phi(f)), f\in\Gamma(U,\mathcal S_1). On D_+(f), the section f^n trivializes \mathcal O_X(n), while on D_+(\phi(f)) the section \phi(f)^n trivializes \mathcal O_Y(n). The morphism \mathbf{Proj}(\phi) carries the first trivializing section to the second. Thus the two invertible sheaves are canonically identified on each such open subset. These identifications agree on overlaps because they are induced by the graded homomorphism \phi, and hence they glue globally. ◻

Proposition 1.1 (When the natural domain is the whole projective spectrum). The following conditions are equivalent.

  1. V_\phi=Y.

  2. Affine-locally on S, every homogeneous prime \mathfrak q\in\operatorname{Proj}\mathcal T(U) avoids the image of some element of \mathcal S(U)_+.

  3. Affine-locally on S, \mathcal T(U)_+ \subseteq \sqrt{\phi(\mathcal S(U)_+)\mathcal T(U)}.

In particular, if \phi is surjective, then V_\phi=Y.

Proof. The equivalence is local on S, so write R=\mathcal S(U), T=\mathcal T(U). The complement of V_{\phi,U} consists of homogeneous primes \mathfrak q satisfying \phi(R_+)T\subseteq\mathfrak q. Such a prime belongs to \operatorname{Proj}T precisely when it does not contain T_+. Therefore the complement is empty if and only if every homogeneous prime containing \phi(R_+)T also contains T_+. By the homogeneous prime description of radicals, this is equivalent to T_+\subseteq\sqrt{\phi(R_+)T}. If \phi is surjective and both algebras are generated in degree one, then T_+=\phi(R_+)T, so the condition is automatic. ◻

Corollary 1.1 (Closed immersions from graded quotients). Let \mathcal I_\bullet\subseteq\mathcal S_\bullet be a quasi-coherent graded ideal and let \mathcal T_\bullet = \mathcal S_\bullet/\mathcal I_\bullet. Then the quotient map induces a closed immersion \mathbf{Proj}_S(\mathcal T_\bullet) \hookrightarrow \mathbf{Proj}_S(\mathcal S_\bullet). Its defining ideal sheaf is \widetilde{\mathcal I_\bullet}, and \mathcal O_{\mathbf{Proj}_S(\mathcal S_\bullet)}(1) \big|_{\mathbf{Proj}_S(\mathcal T_\bullet)} \cong \mathcal O_{\mathbf{Proj}_S(\mathcal T_\bullet)}(1).

Proof. The assertion is local on S. On an affine open U, it reduces to the standard closed immersion \operatorname{Proj}(R/I)\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Proj}R. The local closed immersions and their ideal sheaves are compatible with localization, so they glue. Compatibility of \mathcal O(1) follows from Proposition . ◻

Remark 1.1 (Contravariance and partial functoriality). The construction \mathbf{Proj} is contravariant in the graded algebra, but a graded homomorphism generally produces a morphism only on the open subscheme V_\phi. Thus relative \mathbf{Proj} is not a contravariant functor on the category of all graded algebras with morphisms as stated. It becomes functorial after restricting to homomorphisms satisfying V_\phi=\mathbf{Proj}_S(\mathcal T_\bullet), for example surjective homomorphisms of standard graded algebras.

Relative projective bundles

In this subsection, S denotes an arbitrary scheme and \mathcal E a quasi-coherent \mathcal O_S-module.

Definition 1.23 (The symmetric algebra). The symmetric algebra of \mathcal E is the quasi-coherent graded \mathcal O_S-algebra \operatorname{Sym}_{\mathcal O_S}(\mathcal E) = \bigoplus_{d\geq0} \operatorname{Sym}^d_{\mathcal O_S}(\mathcal E), where \operatorname{Sym}^0(\mathcal E)=\mathcal O_S, \operatorname{Sym}^1(\mathcal E)=\mathcal E. It is generated in degree one over \mathcal O_S.

Definition 1.24 (The associated affine linear bundle). The relative affine scheme \mathbf V_S(\mathcal E) \coloneqq \underline{\operatorname{Spec}}_S \left(\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathcal O_S}(\mathcal E)\right) \longrightarrow S represents the functor (T\xrightarrow{g}S) \longmapsto \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O_T}(g^*\mathcal E,\mathcal O_T). If \mathcal E is locally free of rank r, then \mathbf V_S(\mathcal E) is locally isomorphic to \mathbb A_S^r. Thus it is the geometric vector bundle associated, under our convention, to the dual of \mathcal E. Its projectivization is defined below.

Definition 1.25 (Projective bundle). The projective bundle associated to \mathcal E is \pi:\mathbf P_S(\mathcal E) \coloneqq \underline{\operatorname{Proj}}_S \left(\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathcal O_S}(\mathcal E)\right) \longrightarrow S. When the base is clear, we simply write \mathbf P(\mathcal E).

We use the quotient convention: \mathbf P(\mathcal E) parametrizes invertible quotients of \mathcal E. With this convention there is a canonical surjection { \pi^*\mathcal E\twoheadrightarrow \mathcal O_{\mathbf P(\mathcal E)}(1). }

Remark 1.1 (Conventions). Some references define \mathbf P(\mathcal E) to be the projective bundle of one-dimensional subspaces, which, for \mathcal E finite locally free, is our \mathbf P(\mathcal E^\vee). The universal quotient displayed above fixes the convention used throughout these notes.

Proposition 1.1 (Local form and base change). Let \mathcal E be quasi-coherent.

  1. Formation of \mathbf P(\mathcal E) commutes with arbitrary base change. If g:S'\to S and \mathcal E'=g^*\mathcal E, then \mathbf P_{S'}(\mathcal E') \cong \mathbf P_S(\mathcal E)\times_S S', and the pullback of \mathcal O_{\mathbf P(\mathcal E)}(1) is \mathcal O_{\mathbf P(\mathcal E')}(1).

  2. If \mathcal E is locally free of rank r, then \mathbf P(\mathcal E)\to S is locally isomorphic over S to \mathbb P_S^{r-1}\longrightarrow S.

Proof. Both assertions are local on S. On an affine open V=\operatorname{Spec}A on which \mathcal E is associated to an A-module E, one has \mathbf P(\mathcal E)|_V \cong \operatorname{Proj}(\operatorname{Sym}_A E). Tensoring the graded algebra \operatorname{Sym}_A E with the coordinate ring of an affine base change gives \operatorname{Sym}_A E\otimes_A B \cong \operatorname{Sym}_B(E\otimes_A B), which proves (i). If E\cong A^{\oplus r}, then \operatorname{Sym}_A E \cong A[T_0,\ldots,T_{r-1}], so its projective spectrum is \mathbb P_A^{r-1}. This proves (ii). ◻

Example 1.1 (Relative projective space). For every n\geq0, { \mathbb P_S^n \cong \mathbf P_S(\mathcal O_S^{\oplus(n+1)}). } Indeed, \operatorname{Sym}_{\mathcal O_S} (\mathcal O_S^{\oplus(n+1)}) \cong \mathcal O_S[T_0,\ldots,T_n] with the standard grading.

Theorem 1.1 (Universal property of a projective bundle). Let g:T\to S be an S-scheme. There is a natural bijection between \operatorname{Hom}_S(T,\mathbf P_S(\mathcal E)) and the set of isomorphism classes of pairs (\mathcal L,q) consisting of

  1. an invertible \mathcal O_T-module \mathcal L, and

  2. a surjective homomorphism q:g^*\mathcal E\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal L.

Two pairs (\mathcal L,q) and (\mathcal L',q') are isomorphic if there is an isomorphism u:\mathcal L\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal L' such that u\circ q=q'.

Proof. Given an S-morphism h:T\longrightarrow\mathbf P_S(\mathcal E), pull back the universal quotient to obtain g^*\mathcal E =h^*\pi^*\mathcal E \twoheadrightarrow h^*\mathcal O_{\mathbf P(\mathcal E)}(1). Thus one obtains the pair \left( h^*\mathcal O_{\mathbf P(\mathcal E)}(1), h^*(\pi^*\mathcal E\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal O(1)) \right).

Conversely, a quotient q:g^*\mathcal E\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal L induces a surjective homomorphism of graded \mathcal O_T-algebras \operatorname{Sym}(g^*\mathcal E) \longrightarrow \operatorname{Sym}(\mathcal L) = \bigoplus_{d\geq0}\mathcal L^{\otimes d}. By the universal property of relative \operatorname{Proj}, this defines an S-morphism h_{(\mathcal L,q)}:T\longrightarrow\mathbf P_S(\mathcal E). The pullback of the universal quotient is canonically identified with q. These two constructions are inverse and are functorial in T. ◻

Corollary 1.1 (The functor of points of projective space). Let g:X\to S be an S-scheme. There is a natural bijection \operatorname{Hom}_S(X,\mathbb P_S^n) \xleftrightarrow{\ \sim\ } \left\{ (\mathcal L,s_0,\ldots,s_n) \right\}/\cong, where \mathcal L is an invertible sheaf on X and s_0,\ldots,s_n\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal L) generate \mathcal L at every point. Equivalently, the evaluation map \mathcal O_X^{\oplus(n+1)} \longrightarrow\mathcal L, (a_0,\ldots,a_n) \longmapsto \sum_{i=0}^n a_i s_i, is surjective.

Two tuples are identified when an isomorphism of their invertible sheaves carries every s_i to the corresponding section.

Proof. Apply Theorem  to \mathcal E=\mathcal O_S^{\oplus(n+1)}. A homomorphism g^*\mathcal O_S^{\oplus(n+1)} =\mathcal O_X^{\oplus(n+1)} \longrightarrow\mathcal L is determined by the images s_0,\ldots,s_n of the standard basis, and it is surjective precisely when these sections generate \mathcal L. ◻

Equivalent criteria for relative very ampleness

Let f:X\longrightarrow S be a quasi-compact morphism and let \mathcal L be an invertible \mathcal O_X-module.

Definition 1.26 (Relative very ampleness). The sheaf \mathcal L is called f-very ample, or very ample relative to S, if there exist a quasi-coherent \mathcal O_S-module \mathcal E, an immersion over S i:X\longrightarrow\mathbf P_S(\mathcal E), and an isomorphism \mathcal L\cong i^*\mathcal O_{\mathbf P(\mathcal E)}(1).

Notation 1.3 (The canonical morphism defined by all relative sections). Assume that f is quasi-separated. Then f_*\mathcal L is quasi-coherent. The counit of the adjunction (f^*,f_*) is the evaluation homomorphism \operatorname{ev}_{\mathcal L}: f^*f_*\mathcal L\longrightarrow\mathcal L. If this map is surjective, the universal property of the projective bundle defines a canonical S-morphism \varphi_{\mathcal L}: X\longrightarrow\mathbf P_S(f_*\mathcal L) satisfying \varphi_{\mathcal L}^* \mathcal O_{\mathbf P(f_*\mathcal L)}(1) \cong\mathcal L.

Theorem 1.1 (Equivalent criteria for relative very ampleness). Under the assumptions above, the following conditions are equivalent.

  1. The invertible sheaf \mathcal L is f-very ample.

  2. There exists an open covering S=\bigcup_{\lambda}V_\lambda such that \mathcal L|_{f^{-1}(V_\lambda)} is very ample relative to f^{-1}(V_\lambda)\to V_\lambda for every \lambda.

  3. The morphism f is quasi-separated, the evaluation map f^*f_*\mathcal L\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal L is surjective, and the associated canonical morphism \varphi_{\mathcal L}: X\longrightarrow\mathbf P_S(f_*\mathcal L) is an immersion.

  4. There exist a quasi-coherent graded \mathcal O_S-algebra \mathcal A=\bigoplus_{d\geq0}\mathcal A_d, \mathcal A_0=\mathcal O_S, generated by \mathcal A_1, and a homomorphism of graded \mathcal O_X-algebras \psi: f^*\mathcal A \longrightarrow \bigoplus_{d\geq0}\mathcal L^{\otimes d}, such that the degree-one map f^*\mathcal A_1\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal L is surjective and the associated morphism r_{\mathcal L,\psi}: X\longrightarrow\underline{\operatorname{Proj}}_S(\mathcal A) is an immersion.

  5. The condition is affine local on the base: there exists an affine open covering S=\bigcup_{\lambda}\operatorname{Spec}A_\lambda such that each restriction \mathcal L|_{f^{-1}(\operatorname{Spec}A_\lambda)} is very ample over A_\lambda.

Proof. The equivalence of (ii) and (v) follows by refining an arbitrary open covering by an affine open covering. Clearly (i) implies (ii) after restriction to the inverse image of an open subscheme of S.

Assume (ii). Relative very ampleness implies that f is separated locally on S, hence f is separated and therefore quasi-separated. It follows that f_*\mathcal L is quasi-coherent.

Fix V=V_\lambda and write X_V=f^{-1}(V), f_V:X_V\to V. Choose an immersion i_V:X_V\longrightarrow\mathbf P_V(\mathcal E_V) with \mathcal L|_{X_V} \cong i_V^*\mathcal O_{\mathbf P(\mathcal E_V)}(1). By the universal property of projective bundles, i_V corresponds to a surjection f_V^*\mathcal E_V\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal L|_{X_V}. Adjunction gives a homomorphism \mathcal E_V\longrightarrow(f_*\mathcal L)|_V whose pullback followed by evaluation is the displayed surjection. Hence (f^*f_*\mathcal L)|_{X_V} \longrightarrow \mathcal L|_{X_V} is surjective. Since this holds on a covering of X, the global evaluation map is surjective.

The same local comparison shows that the canonical morphism \varphi_{\mathcal L}|_{X_V}: X_V\longrightarrow \mathbf P_V((f_*\mathcal L)|_V) is an immersion: the chosen linear subsystem \mathcal E_V gives a partially defined linear projection from \mathbf P((f_*\mathcal L)|_V) to \mathbf P(\mathcal E_V), defined on an open neighbourhood of \varphi_{\mathcal L}(X_V), and its composition with \varphi_{\mathcal L}|_{X_V} is i_V. Thus \varphi_{\mathcal L} is locally an immersion, hence is an immersion. This proves (iii).

Condition (iii) immediately implies (i) by taking \mathcal E=f_*\mathcal L, i=\varphi_{\mathcal L}. Thus (i), (ii), (iii), and (v) are equivalent.

Condition (i) implies (iv) by taking \mathcal A=\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathcal O_S}(\mathcal E) for an immersion X\to\mathbf P(\mathcal E) occurring in the definition. Conversely, assume (iv). Since \mathcal A is generated in degree one, the canonical homomorphism \operatorname{Sym}_{\mathcal O_S}(\mathcal A_1) \twoheadrightarrow\mathcal A is surjective and induces a closed immersion \underline{\operatorname{Proj}}_S(\mathcal A) \hookrightarrow \mathbf P_S(\mathcal A_1) which preserves \mathcal O(1). Composing this closed immersion with r_{\mathcal L,\psi} realizes X as an immersed S-subscheme of \mathbf P_S(\mathcal A_1) and pulls \mathcal O(1) back to \mathcal L. Hence (i) holds. ◻

Corollary 1.1 (The proper case). Assume in addition that f:X\to S is proper. In every condition of Theorem , the word immersion may be replaced by closed immersion. In particular, if \mathcal L is f-very ample, then the canonical map \varphi_{\mathcal L}: X\longrightarrow\mathbf P_S(f_*\mathcal L) is a closed immersion.

Proof. The projective bundle \mathbf P_S(\mathcal E)\to S is separated. Hence an S-morphism from the proper S-scheme X to \mathbf P_S(\mathcal E) is proper. A proper immersion is a closed immersion. ◻

Remark 1.1 (Finite type versions). If S is noetherian, f is proper, and X is of finite type over S, then f_*\mathcal L is coherent. Consequently the canonical projective bundle \mathbf P_S(f_*\mathcal L) is defined by a finite type quasi-coherent module. In this setting relative very ampleness is the usual existence of a closed projective embedding over S.

Separation of points and tangent vectors

The following criterion belongs to the absolute proper case. It should not be confused with the preceding relative criterion involving f_*\mathcal L.

Definition 1.27 (Separating points and tangent vectors). Let k be an algebraically closed field, let X be a proper k-scheme, and let \mathcal L be an invertible sheaf on X. Put V=H^0(X,\mathcal L). We say that V separates points and tangent vectors if:

  1. \mathcal L is generated by V;

  2. for any two distinct closed points x,y\in X, the restriction map V\longrightarrow\mathcal L(x)\oplus\mathcal L(y) is surjective; and

  3. for every closed point x\in X and every nonzero tangent vector v\in T_{X/k,x}, there exists a section s\in V vanishing at x whose first-order derivative in the direction v is nonzero.

Here \mathcal L(x)=\mathcal L_x/\mathfrak m_x\mathcal L_x.

Proposition 1.1 (The length-two formulation). Under the preceding assumptions, global sections of \mathcal L separate points and tangent vectors if and only if, for every zero-dimensional closed subscheme Z\subseteq X of length two over k, the restriction map H^0(X,\mathcal L) \longrightarrow H^0(Z,\mathcal L|_Z) is surjective, together with global generation of \mathcal L.

Proof. A length-two subscheme supported at two distinct closed points is their disjoint union, and surjectivity of the restriction map is exactly separation of those points. A length-two subscheme supported at one closed point is isomorphic to the first-order infinitesimal subscheme determined by a nonzero tangent direction. Surjectivity onto its two-dimensional space of sections says that one can prescribe both the value at the point and the first derivative in that tangent direction. This is precisely the tangent-vector condition. ◻

Theorem 1.1 (Very ampleness by points and tangent vectors). Let k be an algebraically closed field, let X be a proper k-scheme, and let \mathcal L be an invertible \mathcal O_X-module. The following conditions are equivalent.

  1. \mathcal L is very ample over k.

  2. The complete linear system H^0(X,\mathcal L) generates \mathcal L and separates points and tangent vectors.

  3. For every zero-dimensional closed subscheme Z\subseteq X of length at most two, the restriction map H^0(X,\mathcal L) \longrightarrow H^0(Z,\mathcal L|_Z) is surjective.

Proof. Because X is proper, the vector space V=H^0(X,\mathcal L) is finite-dimensional. If \mathcal L is globally generated, the evaluation quotient V\otimes_k\mathcal O_X\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal L defines the canonical morphism \varphi_{|\mathcal L|}:X\longrightarrow\mathbf P_k(V). The standard length-two criterion for a morphism defined by a complete linear system says that \varphi_{|\mathcal L|} is a closed immersion if and only if the restriction map to every finite subscheme of length at most two is surjective. Indeed, length-one subschemes test global generation, unions of two distinct points test injectivity on geometric points, and nonreduced length-two subschemes test injectivity on tangent directions and the scheme structure. Thus (i) and (iii) are equivalent. Proposition  identifies (ii) and (iii). ◻

Remark 1.1 (Why the hypotheses matter). The point-and-tangent-vector criterion is most naturally stated for proper schemes over an algebraically closed field. Over a non-algebraically closed field one should use geometric points after base change to an algebraic closure. In the relative setting, the correct intrinsic criterion is Theorem , not a condition involving the single vector space \Gamma(X,\mathcal L).

Blow-Ups

Throughout this section, X is a noetherian scheme and \mathcal I\subseteq\mathcal O_X is a quasi-coherent ideal sheaf of finite type. We write Z=V(\mathcal I)\subseteq X for the corresponding closed subscheme.

The Rees algebra and the global construction

Definition 1.28 (Rees algebra). The Rees algebra of \mathcal I is the quasi-coherent graded \mathcal O_X-algebra \mathcal R(\mathcal I) \coloneqq \bigoplus_{d\geq 0}\mathcal I^d, \mathcal I^0=\mathcal O_X, with multiplication induced by \mathcal I^d\otimes_{\mathcal O_X}\mathcal I^e \longrightarrow \mathcal I^{d+e}. Equivalently, after adjoining a formal variable t of degree one, one may write \mathcal R(\mathcal I) = \mathcal O_X\oplus \mathcal I t\oplus\mathcal I^2t^2\oplus\cdots \subseteq \mathcal O_X[t]. The blow-up of X along Z, or along \mathcal I, is \operatorname{Bl}_Z X \coloneqq \mathbf{Proj}_X\left(\mathcal R(\mathcal I)\right). The structural morphism is denoted by \pi:\operatorname{Bl}_Z X\longrightarrow X.

Remark 1.1. The Rees algebra is generated by its degree-one part \mathcal I. There is therefore a canonical surjection \operatorname{Sym}_{\mathcal O_X}(\mathcal I) \twoheadrightarrow \mathcal R(\mathcal I). Consequently, the blow-up is a closed subscheme of the relative projective bundle \mathbf P_X(\mathcal I) = \mathbf{Proj}_X\left(\operatorname{Sym}(\mathcal I)\right). In particular, \pi is a projective morphism.

Proposition 1.1 (Compatibility with restriction). For every open subscheme U\subseteq X, one has a canonical isomorphism \operatorname{Bl}_{Z\cap U}U \xrightarrow{\sim} \operatorname{Bl}_ZX\times_XU. In particular, the construction of the blow-up is affine local on X.

Proof. Restriction commutes with powers of a quasi-coherent ideal: (\mathcal I|_U)^d=\mathcal I^d|_U. Hence \mathcal R(\mathcal I)|_U \cong \mathcal R(\mathcal I|_U). The result follows from the base-change property of relative projective spectra for open immersions. ◻

The exceptional divisor

Proposition 1.1 (The tautological ideal). Let \widetilde X=\operatorname{Bl}_ZX. The canonical degree-one quotient on the relative projective spectrum induces a surjection \pi^*\mathcal I\twoheadrightarrow \mathcal O_{\widetilde X}(1). Its image in \mathcal O_{\widetilde X} identifies canonically with the extended ideal \mathcal I\mathcal O_{\widetilde X}. Moreover, \mathcal I\mathcal O_{\widetilde X} \cong \mathcal O_{\widetilde X}(1) is an invertible ideal sheaf.

Proof. The assertion is local on X. Write X=\operatorname{Spec}A and let I=(g_1,\ldots,g_m). Then \widetilde X = \operatorname{Proj}A[It], A[It]=\bigoplus_{d\geq0}I^dt^d. The standard open subsets D_+(g_it), 1\leq i\leq m, cover \widetilde X. On D_+(g_it) every g_j satisfies g_j=g_i\frac{g_jt}{g_it}. Thus I\mathcal O_{D_+(g_it)} is generated by g_i. The transition function between the generators g_i and g_j is the unit \frac{g_jt}{g_it} on the overlap D_+(g_it)\cap D_+(g_jt). Hence the extended ideal is invertible. The same transition functions define \mathcal O_{\widetilde X}(1), giving the asserted canonical identification. ◻

Definition 1.29 (Exceptional divisor). The scheme-theoretic inverse image E\coloneqq \pi^{-1}(Z) =V(\mathcal I\mathcal O_{\widetilde X}) is called the exceptional divisor of the blow-up. Since \mathcal I\mathcal O_{\widetilde X} is an invertible ideal, E is an effective Cartier divisor and { \mathcal O_{\widetilde X}(-E) \cong \mathcal I\mathcal O_{\widetilde X} \cong \mathcal O_{\widetilde X}(1). } Equivalently, \mathcal O_{\widetilde X}(E) \cong \mathcal O_{\widetilde X}(-1).

Remark 1.1. The exceptional divisor need not be reduced. The cuspidal-curve example below gives a simple instance where E is a nonreduced length-two subscheme supported at one point.

The universal property

Theorem 1.1 (Universal property of the blow-up). Let f:Y\to X be a morphism. The following conditions are equivalent:

  1. the ideal \mathcal I\mathcal O_Y is an invertible ideal sheaf;

  2. the scheme-theoretic inverse image f^{-1}(Z)=V(\mathcal I\mathcal O_Y) is an effective Cartier divisor on Y.

Whenever these conditions hold, there exists a unique morphism \widetilde f:Y\longrightarrow\operatorname{Bl}_ZX such that \pi\circ\widetilde f=f. Thus \operatorname{Bl}_ZX is terminal among X-schemes on which the pullback of \mathcal I becomes invertible.

Proof. The equivalence of (i) and (ii) is the usual correspondence between invertible ideal sheaves and effective Cartier divisors.

The existence and uniqueness are local on both X and Y. We may take X=\operatorname{Spec}A, I=(g_1,\ldots,g_m), and restrict to an open subset W\subseteq Y on which I\mathcal O_W is generated by one of the pullbacks f^*g_i. For each j, there is a unique function u_j on W such that f^*g_j=f^*g_i\,u_j. These functions define a morphism W\longrightarrow D_+(g_it) = \operatorname{Spec}A\left[\frac{I}{g_i}\right] \subseteq\operatorname{Bl}_IX. On overlaps, the morphisms agree because they are determined by the same ratios g_j/g_i. They therefore glue to a morphism \widetilde f:Y\to\operatorname{Bl}_IX.

The ratios are forced by the equality f^*g_j=f^*g_i(g_j/g_i), so the factorization is unique. ◻

Corollary 1.1 (Blowing up a Cartier divisor). If \mathcal I is already an invertible ideal sheaf on X, then \operatorname{Bl}_ZX\xrightarrow{\sim}X. In particular, blowing up an effective Cartier divisor does not change the scheme.

Proof. The identity morphism of X satisfies the universal property. It therefore factors uniquely through the blow-up. The resulting morphism is inverse to \pi by uniqueness. ◻

Proposition 1.1 (Blowing up a power of an ideal). For every integer d>0, there is a canonical isomorphism \operatorname{Bl}_{\mathcal I^d}X \xrightarrow{\sim} \operatorname{Bl}_{\mathcal I}X.

Proof. The Rees algebra of \mathcal I^d is the d-th Veronese subalgebra of the Rees algebra of \mathcal I: \mathcal R(\mathcal I^d) = \bigoplus_{n\geq0}\mathcal I^{dn} = \mathcal R(\mathcal I)^{(d)}. Relative projective spectra are unchanged by passage to a Veronese subalgebra. ◻

Affine equations and standard charts

Let U=\operatorname{Spec}A, A= R[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/(f_1,\ldots,f_s), and let I=(g_1,\ldots,g_m)\subseteq A. Introduce homogeneous variables T_1,\ldots,T_m of degree one and the graded homomorphism \Theta:A[T_1,\ldots,T_m] \longrightarrow A[t], T_i\longmapsto g_it. Its image is the Rees algebra A[It], and its homogeneous kernel \mathcal J_I\coloneqq\ker(\Theta) is called the Rees ideal of I.

Proposition 1.1 (Affine presentation of a blow-up). With the preceding notation, \operatorname{Bl}_IU = \operatorname{Proj} \frac{A[T_1,\ldots,T_m]}{\mathcal J_I} \subseteq U\times_{\operatorname{Spec}R}\mathbf P_R^{m-1}. The Rees ideal consists precisely of the homogeneous relations among g_1t,\ldots,g_mt. In particular, every syzygy a_1g_1+\cdots+a_mg_m=0 gives the homogeneous linear equation a_1T_1+\cdots+a_mT_m=0, and one always has g_iT_j-g_jT_i\in\mathcal J_I. These elementary equations need not generate the entire Rees ideal.

Proof. By definition, A[It] \cong A[T_1,\ldots,T_m]/\mathcal J_I. Taking \operatorname{Proj} gives the first equality. The surjection from the polynomial algebra induces the displayed closed immersion into the relative projective space. The remaining assertions follow immediately from the definition of \Theta. ◻

Proposition 1.1 (Standard affine charts). The blow-up is covered by the affine open subsets U_i=D_+(g_it), 1\leq i\leq m, where U_i \cong \operatorname{Spec}A\left[\frac{I}{g_i}\right] = \operatorname{Spec} A\left[ \frac{g_1}{g_i},\ldots, \frac{g_m}{g_i} \right] \subseteq\operatorname{Spec}A_{g_i}. Equivalently, if u_j=T_j/T_i for j\neq i, then A\left[\frac{I}{g_i}\right] \cong A[u_1,\ldots,\widehat{u_i},\ldots,u_m]/\mathcal J_{I,i}, where \mathcal J_{I,i} is obtained by dehomogenizing the Rees ideal at T_i=1. On this chart one has g_j=g_iu_j,j\neq i, and the exceptional divisor is defined by g_i=0.

Proof. The degree-zero part of the localization of A[It] at g_it is A[It]_{(g_it)} = A\left[ \frac{g_1t}{g_it},\ldots, \frac{g_mt}{g_it} \right] = A\left[ \frac{g_1}{g_i},\ldots, \frac{g_m}{g_i} \right]. The remaining descriptions follow by dehomogenizing the homogeneous presentation of the Rees algebra. ◻

Remark 1.1 (Closure of the graph). Suppose that A is a domain and I\neq0. On the open set U\setminus V(I) the generators define a rational map [g_1:\cdots:g_m]: U\dashrightarrow\mathbf P_R^{m-1}. The blow-up is the scheme-theoretic closure of its graph in U\times_R\mathbf P_R^{m-1}.

Blowing up finitely many points of projective space

Example 1.1 (The blow-up of affine space at the origin). Let U=\mathbf A_k^n=\operatorname{Spec}k[x_1,\ldots,x_n] and let I=(x_1,\ldots,x_n). Since the coordinate functions form a regular sequence, the Rees ideal is generated by the 2\times2 minors x_iT_j-x_jT_i, 1\leq i,j\leq n. Thus \operatorname{Bl}_0\mathbf A_k^n = V(x_iT_j-x_jT_i) \subseteq \mathbf A_k^n\times\mathbf P_k^{n-1}. On the chart T_i\neq0, put u_j=\frac{T_j}{T_i}(j\neq i). Then x_j=x_iu_j, and hence U_i \cong \operatorname{Spec} k[x_i,u_1,\ldots,\widehat{u_i},\ldots,u_n] \cong\mathbf A_k^n. The exceptional divisor on this chart is x_i=0. These affine pieces glue to E\cong\mathbf P_k^{n-1}, and \mathcal O_E(E)\cong\mathcal O_{\mathbf P^{n-1}}(-1).

Example 1.1 (Blowing up n distinct points of \mathbf P_k^n). Let k be algebraically closed and let p_1,\ldots,p_n\in\mathbf P_k^n be distinct closed points. Put Z=\{p_1,\ldots,p_n\}, \mathcal I_Z = \bigcap_{i=1}^n\mathfrak m_{p_i}. Because the points are pairwise disjoint, \mathcal I_Z = \prod_{i=1}^n\mathfrak m_{p_i}. Let \pi:\widetilde{\mathbf P^n} = \operatorname{Bl}_Z\mathbf P_k^n \longrightarrow\mathbf P_k^n. The blow-up may be performed successively at the points in any order. Near each p_i, an affine coordinate neighbourhood identifies the construction with Example . Therefore E=E_1\amalg\cdots\amalg E_n, E_i\cong\mathbf P_k^{n-1}, and \mathcal O_{E_i}(E_i) \cong \mathcal O_{\mathbf P^{n-1}}(-1). For n\geq2, writing H=\pi^*\mathcal O_{\mathbf P^n}(1), one has \operatorname{Pic}(\widetilde{\mathbf P^n}) \cong \mathbb ZH\oplus \bigoplus_{i=1}^n\mathbb ZE_i. If a hypersurface of degree d passes through p_i with multiplicity m_i, then its strict transform has divisor class dH-\sum_{i=1}^n m_iE_i. The canonical divisor is { K_{\widetilde{\mathbf P^n}} =-(n+1)H+(n-1)\sum_{i=1}^nE_i. } When n=1, every point is an effective Cartier divisor on \mathbf P^1; therefore the blow-up at finitely many points is isomorphic to \mathbf P^1 itself.

The blow-up of a cuspidal curve

Example 1.1 (Blowing up the cusp y^2=x^3). Let C = \operatorname{Spec}A, A=k[x,y]/(y^2-x^3), and let o=(x,y) be the singular origin. Put I=(x,y). Introduce homogeneous coordinates [U:V] corresponding to xt and yt. The Rees algebra has the presentation A[It] \cong \frac{A[U,V]} {(xV-yU,\;x^2U-yV,\;xU^2-V^2)}. Hence \operatorname{Bl}_oC = \operatorname{Proj}A[It] \subseteq C\times\mathbf P_k^1.

On the chart U\neq0, put v=\frac{V}{U}. The equations become y=xv, x^2=yv, x=v^2. Consequently, x=v^2, y=v^3, and D_+(U)\cong\operatorname{Spec}k[v]\cong\mathbf A_k^1.

On the chart V\neq0, put u=\frac{U}{V}. Then x=yu, x^2u=y, xu^2=1, so D_+(V) \cong \operatorname{Spec}k[u,u^{-1}]. Under u=v^{-1} this is the punctured open subset of the first chart. Therefore the first chart already covers the whole blow-up, and { \operatorname{Bl}_oC \cong\mathbf A_k^1. } The blow-up morphism is \pi:\mathbf A_k^1\longrightarrow C, v\longmapsto(v^2,v^3). Thus the blow-up is the normalization of the cuspidal curve.

The scheme-theoretic exceptional divisor is not reduced. Indeed, I\mathcal O_{\mathbf A^1} =(v^2,v^3) =(v^2), so E=V(v^2)=2[0]. Its underlying topological exceptional locus is the single point v=0.

Geometric properties of blow-ups

Proposition 1.1 (Projectivity and the complement of the center). Let \pi:\widetilde X=\operatorname{Bl}_ZX\to X. Then:

  1. \pi is projective;

  2. the restriction \pi^{-1}(X\setminus Z) \longrightarrow X\setminus Z is an isomorphism;

  3. if X is integral and Z does not contain the generic point of X, then \pi is birational.

Proof. Projectivity follows from the closed immersion \widetilde X\hookrightarrow\mathbf P_X(\mathcal I). On X\setminus Z, the ideal \mathcal I is the unit ideal and is therefore invertible. Corollary  gives \operatorname{Bl}_{Z\cap(X\setminus Z)}(X\setminus Z) \cong X\setminus Z. This proves (ii). Under the hypotheses of (iii), the open set X\setminus Z contains the generic point and is dense, so the isomorphism over this open set makes \pi birational. ◻

Proposition 1.1 (Nonflatness of a nontrivial blow-up). Assume that X is connected, noetherian, and integral, and that \mathcal I is a nonzero ideal sheaf of finite type. If \pi:\operatorname{Bl}_{\mathcal I}X\longrightarrow X is flat, then \pi is an isomorphism. Consequently, every nontrivial blow-up under these hypotheses is not flat.

Proof. The morphism \pi is projective and of finite presentation, and \mathcal O_{\widetilde X}(1) is relatively ample. Over the generic point \eta of X, the nonzero ideal \mathcal I_\eta equals the field k(\eta), so \widetilde X_\eta \cong \operatorname{Proj}k(\eta)[t] \cong \operatorname{Spec}k(\eta). Its Hilbert polynomial with respect to \mathcal O(1) is therefore the constant polynomial 1.

If \pi is flat, the Hilbert polynomial of the fibers is locally constant. Since X is connected, every fiber has Hilbert polynomial 1 and is a zero-dimensional scheme of length one. Thus \pi is quasi-finite. Being projective, it is finite. It is also birational, so \pi_*\mathcal O_{\widetilde X} is a finite flat \mathcal O_X-algebra of rank one. Fiberwise, the unit section generates this rank-one module. Nakayama’s lemma then shows that \mathcal O_X\longrightarrow\pi_*\mathcal O_{\widetilde X} is an isomorphism. Hence \pi is an isomorphism. ◻

Remark 1.1. The phrase “a blow-up is almost never flat” should be understood through Proposition : in the usual integral noetherian setting, flatness forces the blow-up to be trivial. For example, blowing up an effective Cartier divisor is flat only because the blow-up is already an isomorphism.

Base change

Theorem 1.1 (Base change for blow-ups). Let f:Y\to X be a morphism and put \mathcal I_Y=\mathcal I\mathcal O_Y, Z_Y=V(\mathcal I_Y)=Y\times_XZ. There is a natural surjective homomorphism of graded \mathcal O_Y-algebras f^*\mathcal R(\mathcal I) = \bigoplus_{d\geq0}f^*(\mathcal I^d) \twoheadrightarrow \bigoplus_{d\geq0}\mathcal I_Y^d = \mathcal R(\mathcal I_Y). It induces a canonical closed immersion { \operatorname{Bl}_{Z_Y}Y \hookrightarrow \operatorname{Bl}_ZX\times_XY. } This closed immersion is an isomorphism whenever the canonical maps f^*(\mathcal I^d) \longrightarrow (\mathcal I\mathcal O_Y)^d are isomorphisms for all d\geq0. In particular, if f is flat, then { \operatorname{Bl}_{f^{-1}(Z)}Y \xrightarrow{\sim} \operatorname{Bl}_ZX\times_XY. }

Proof. Multiplication gives degreewise surjections f^*(\mathcal I^d)\twoheadrightarrow(\mathcal I\mathcal O_Y)^d. Together they form the displayed surjection of graded algebras. Relative \mathbf{Proj} reverses a surjection of graded algebras into a closed immersion, and relative \mathbf{Proj} itself commutes with arbitrary base change. Hence \operatorname{Bl}_{Z_Y}Y \hookrightarrow \mathbf{Proj}_Y(f^*\mathcal R(\mathcal I)) \cong \operatorname{Bl}_ZX\times_XY. If all degreewise maps are isomorphisms, the map of Rees algebras is an isomorphism, so the closed immersion is an isomorphism. Flatness of f ensures that tensoring preserves the inclusions and products defining all powers \mathcal I^d. ◻

Remark 1.1 (Why arbitrary base change is different). Without flatness, the maps f^*(\mathcal I^d) \longrightarrow (\mathcal I\mathcal O_Y)^d can have nonzero kernels. Thus the formula \operatorname{Bl}_{f^{-1}(Z)}Y \cong \operatorname{Bl}_ZX\times_XY is not valid for an arbitrary morphism Y\to X.

Smooth centers and the canonical divisor

Theorem 1.1 (Blowing up a smooth center). Let k be a field, let X be a smooth k-scheme, and let Z\subseteq X be a smooth closed subscheme of pure codimension r. Then \widetilde X=\operatorname{Bl}_ZX is smooth over k. Moreover, E \cong \mathbf P_Z(\mathcal I/\mathcal I^2) = \mathbf P_Z(N_{Z/X}^{\vee}), so E\to Z is a \mathbf P^{r-1}-bundle, and \mathcal O_E(-E) \cong \mathcal O_{\mathbf P_Z(\mathcal I/\mathcal I^2)}(1). Equivalently, \mathcal O_E(E) \cong \mathcal O_{\mathbf P_Z(\mathcal I/\mathcal I^2)}(-1).

Proof. The closed immersion Z\hookrightarrow X is regular, and \mathcal I/\mathcal I^2 is locally free of rank r. Smooth-locally on X, one may choose coordinates x_1,\ldots,x_r,y_1,\ldots,y_{n-r} such that Z=V(x_1,\ldots,x_r). The blow-up is then covered by charts on which, for a fixed i, x_j=x_iu_j(j\neq i,\ 1\leq j\leq r). Each chart has smooth coordinates x_i,u_1,\ldots,\widehat{u_i},\ldots,u_r, y_1,\ldots,y_{n-r}, so \widetilde X is smooth.

The exceptional divisor is obtained by setting x_i=0 on each chart. The transition functions of the coordinates u_j identify these affine pieces with the standard charts of \mathbf P_Z(\mathcal I/\mathcal I^2). The assertion about \mathcal O_E(\pm E) follows from \mathcal O_{\widetilde X}(-E) \cong\mathcal O_{\widetilde X}(1) and restriction to E. ◻

Theorem 1.1 (Canonical divisor formula). Let X be a smooth variety over a field, let Z\subseteq X be a smooth closed subvariety of codimension r, and let \pi:\widetilde X=\operatorname{Bl}_ZX\longrightarrow X be the blow-up with exceptional divisor E. Then { K_{\widetilde X} = \pi^*K_X+(r-1)E. } Equivalently, \omega_{\widetilde X} \cong \pi^*\omega_X\otimes \mathcal O_{\widetilde X}((r-1)E).

Proof. The assertion is local near Z. Choose smooth local coordinates x_1,\ldots,x_r,y_1,\ldots,y_{n-r} with Z=V(x_1,\ldots,x_r). On the blow-up chart corresponding to x_1, write x_j=x_1u_j, 2\leq j\leq r. Then, up to sign, \begin{align*} \pi^*& \left( dx_1\wedge dx_2\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_r \wedge dy_1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy_{n-r} \right) \\ &= x_1^{r-1} dx_1\wedge du_2\wedge\cdots\wedge du_r \wedge dy_1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy_{n-r}. \end{align*} Since E is given on this chart by x_1=0, the Jacobian determinant vanishes to order r-1 along E. The same calculation on every chart gives K_{\widetilde X}-\pi^*K_X=(r-1)E. ◻

Remark 1.1. For r=1, the center is an effective Cartier divisor, the blow-up is an isomorphism, and the discrepancy coefficient r-1 is zero. For a point on a smooth n-fold, one has r=n, so the exceptional divisor appears in the canonical class with coefficient n-1.
